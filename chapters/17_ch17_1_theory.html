1. Frame of Reference, Distance vs. Displacement & Average Kinematics

First-Principles Foundation: Frame of Reference

Motion is never absolute; it exists only relative to a chosen Frame of Reference—a rigid coordinate system (an origin $O$ and directional axes) paired with a synchronized clock. In rectilinear (1D) motion, a particle is constrained to move along a single straight line (typically the $x$-axis or vertical $y$-axis), so its entire vector character reduces to a single algebraic sign ($+$ or $-$).

  • Position ($x$): The signed coordinate of the particle relative to the origin $O$ at instant $t$, written as $x(t)$.
  • Displacement ($\Delta x$): The net change in position vector over $[t_1, t_2]$: $$\Delta x = x(t_2) - x(t_1)$$ Displacement depends solely on endpoints, can be positive, negative, or zero, and is completely blind to the path taken in between.
  • Distance ($d$): The total actual path length traversed over $[t_1, t_2]$. Formally, $d = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} |v(t)|\,dt$. Distance is a non-negative scalar that can never decrease with time.
  • Universal Inequality: For any interval of motion, $$\text{Distance} \ge |\text{Displacement}| \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{Average Speed} \ge |\text{Average Velocity}|$$ Equality holds if and only if the particle moves along a straight line without reversing direction (i.e., $v(t)$ never changes sign in $(t_1, t_2)$).
Critical BEST Trap: The Velocity-Reversal (Turnaround) Protocol

Students routinely substitute endpoints $t_1$ and $t_2$ into a polynomial position function such as $x(t) = 2t^2 - 5t + 6$ and report $|x(t_2) - x(t_1)|$ as the distance. That gives only the magnitude of displacement! Whenever velocity changes sign inside $(t_1, t_2)$, the particle turns around and retraces ground.

Mandatory 4-Step Distance Algorithm for any $x(t)$ over $[t_1, t_2]$:

  1. Differentiate to find instantaneous velocity: $v(t) = \dfrac{dx}{dt}$.
  2. Locate all turning instants $t_0$ by solving $v(t_0) = 0$.
  3. Filter only those roots $t_0$ that lie strictly inside the open interval $(t_1, t_2)$.
  4. Split the motion at each interior turning point and sum the absolute segment lengths: $$d = |x(t_0) - x(t_1)| + |x(t_2) - x(t_0)|$$

Quick Verification on $x(t) = 2t^2 - 5t + 6$ over $t \in [0, 3]\text{ s}$: Here $v(t) = 4t - 5 = 0 \implies t_0 = 1.25\text{ s} \in (0, 3)$. Computing positions: $x(0) = 6\text{ m}$, $x(1.25) = 2(1.5625) - 6.25 + 6 = 2.875\text{ m}$, and $x(3) = 18 - 15 + 6 = 9\text{ m}$. Thus $\Delta x = 9 - 6 = +3\text{ m}$, whereas true distance $d = |2.875 - 6| + |9 - 2.875| = 3.125 + 6.125 = 9.25\text{ m}$ (more than triple the displacement!).

Average Velocity vs. Average Speed: Exact Derivations

Over a finite time interval $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$, average velocity and average speed are defined strictly from totals—never by blindly averaging individual speeds:

$$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x(t_2) - x(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}, \qquad \langle \text{speed} \rangle = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}}$$

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: AM ≥ HM Inequality Check

For any positive distinct speeds $v_1, v_2$, the Arithmetic Mean always strictly exceeds the Harmonic Mean ($\frac{v_1+v_2}{2} > \frac{2v_1v_2}{v_1+v_2}$). Physically, when covering equal distances, the particle spends more time moving at the slower speed, dragging the time-weighted average closer to the smaller speed. In fact, $v_{\text{avg}}$ for two equal distances can never exceed $2 v_{\min}$, even if the faster speed approaches infinity!

2. Uniformly Accelerated Motion (UAM), $n$-th Second Trap & Reaction Time

First-Principles Derivations of the 5 UAM Equations ($a = \text{constant}$)

Every standard kinematic equation rests on one strict prerequisite: acceleration $a$ must be strictly constant in both magnitude and direction over the entire interval $[0, t]$. Let $u$ be initial velocity at $t = 0$, $v$ be velocity at time $t$, and $s = x(t) - x(0)$ be displacement.

  1. Velocity–Time Relation: From $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt}$, separate variables and integrate: $$\int_u^v dv = \int_0^t a\,dt \implies v - u = at \implies \mathbf{v = u + at}$$
  2. Displacement–Time Relation: Substitute $v = \dfrac{dx}{dt} = u + at$ and integrate: $$\int_0^s dx = \int_0^t (u + at)\,dt \implies \mathbf{s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2}$$
  3. Average-Velocity Displacement Form: Factor $t$ from $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2 = t\left(u + \frac{1}{2}at\right)$. Replacing $at = v - u$ yields: $$s = t\left(u + \frac{v - u}{2}\right) \implies \mathbf{s = \left(\frac{u + v}{2}\right)t}$$ Warning: $v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{u+v}{2}$ is valid only for constant acceleration (linear $v\text{-}t$ graph).
  4. Velocity–Displacement Relation (Time-Independent): From $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{dv}{dx}\cdot\dfrac{dx}{dt} = v\dfrac{dv}{dx}$: $$\int_u^v v\,dv = \int_0^s a\,dx \implies \frac{v^2 - u^2}{2} = as \implies \mathbf{v^2 - u^2 = 2as}$$
  5. Displacement in the $n$-th Second ($t = n-1$ to $t = n$): $$s_n = s(n) - s(n-1) = \left[un + \frac{1}{2}an^2\right] - \left[u(n-1) + \frac{1}{2}a(n-1)^2\right]$$ Expanding $(n-1)^2 = n^2 - 2n + 1$: $$s_n = u(n - n + 1) + \frac{1}{2}a\left(n^2 - n^2 + 2n - 1\right) \implies \mathbf{s_n = u + \frac{a}{2}(2n - 1)}$$ Dimensional Check: Though $s_n = u + \frac{a}{2}(2n-1)$ looks dimensionally mismatched at first glance, it carries a hidden unit time factor $\Delta t_0 = 1\text{ s}$: $s_n = u(1\text{ s}) + \frac{a}{2}(2n-1)(1\text{ s})^2$, which strictly has dimensions $[L]$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Mid-Time vs. Mid-Point Velocity

For uniformly accelerated motion from speed $u$ to speed $v$ over distance $s$ and time $T$:

  • Velocity at Mid-Time ($t = T/2$): $v_{\text{mid-time}} = u + a\left(\dfrac{T}{2}\right) = u + \dfrac{v - u}{2} = \dfrac{u + v}{2}$ (Arithmetic Mean).
  • Velocity at Mid-Point ($x = s/2$): Apply $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$ separately to each half: $$v_m^2 - u^2 = 2a\left(\frac{s}{2}\right) = as \quad \text{and} \quad v^2 - v_m^2 = 2a\left(\frac{s}{2}\right) = as$$ Equating the left sides gives $v_m^2 - u^2 = v^2 - v_m^2 \implies 2v_m^2 = u^2 + v^2 \implies \mathbf{v_{\text{mid-point}} = \sqrt{\frac{u^2 + v^2}{2}}}$ (Root-Mean-Square).

Because $\text{RMS} \ge \text{AM}$, we always have $v_{\text{mid-point}} \ge v_{\text{mid-time}}$ (valid for both acceleration and constant retardation without reversal).

Fatal Trap: Reversal INSIDE the $n$-th Second

When $u$ and $a$ have opposite signs, the particle momentarily stops at $t_0 = -\dfrac{u}{a}$. If $t_0$ falls strictly inside the $n$-th second—meaning $n - 1 < t_0 < n$—then $s_n = u + \frac{a}{2}(2n-1)$ yields the net displacement, NOT the distance travelled!

Exact Distance During Reversal in the $n$-th Second: Split at $t_0$. Since the particle has zero speed at $t_0$, the forward distance over $[n-1, t_0]$ is $\frac{1}{2}|a|(t_0 - n + 1)^2$ and the backward distance over $[t_0, n]$ is $\frac{1}{2}|a|(n - t_0)^2$. Therefore:

$$d_n = \frac{1}{2}|a|\left[(t_0 - n + 1)^2 + (n - t_0)^2\right]$$

Classic BEST Special Case: If the particle stops right at the midpoint of the $n$-th second ($t_0 = n - 0.5$), its displacement in the $n$-th second is $s_n = 0$, while its true distance is $d_n = \frac{1}{2}|a|\left(\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4}\right) = \dfrac{|a|}{4}$.

Stopping Distance & Reaction-Time Mechanics

When a driver spots an obstacle while travelling at speed $u$, two sequential phases govern stopping:

  1. Reaction Phase (constant speed $u$ for reaction time $t_r$): The car travels $s_r = u t_r$ before the brake pads even engage.
  2. Braking Phase (constant retardation $a_r$ until $v = 0$): Using $0^2 - u^2 = -2a_r s_b$, the braking distance is $s_b = \dfrac{u^2}{2a_r} \propto u^2$, and braking time is $t_b = \dfrac{u}{a_r}$.
$$S_{\text{total}} = u t_r + \frac{u^2}{2a_r}$$

Exam Trap: Doubling initial speed $u$ quadruples the braking distance ($s_b \propto u^2$), but it does not quadruple the total stopping distance when $t_r > 0$ because the reaction distance $u t_r$ only doubles.

3. Motion Under Gravity: Symmetry, Quadratic Identities & Moving Platforms

Near Earth's surface (neglecting air resistance and height variation of gravity), every freely falling body—regardless of its mass—experiences the exact same downward acceleration $g$ ($9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ or $10\text{ m/s}^2$, always check the problem statement). Unless solving a pure drop problem, lock your sign convention as upward positive ($+y$), so $a = -g$.

Galileo's Odd-Number Law & Equal-Height Time Ratios

For an object dropped from rest ($u = 0$, downward $+y$, $a = +g$):

  • Equal Time Intervals ($\tau$): Distances fallen in consecutive equal intervals $\tau$ follow $s_n = \frac{1}{2}g\tau^2(2n-1)$: $$s_1 : s_2 : s_3 : s_4 : \dots = 1 : 3 : 5 : 7 : \dots$$ Cumulative distances from the drop point scale as squares: $y_1 : y_2 : y_3 : y_4 = 1 : 4 : 9 : 16$.
  • Equal Distance Segments ($h$): Time to fall cumulative distance $kh$ is $T_k = \sqrt{\frac{2kh}{g}}$. Subtracting consecutive times gives the ratio of times to cross successive equal heights $h$: $$\Delta t_1 : \Delta t_2 : \Delta t_3 : \dots = 1 : (\sqrt{2} - 1) : (\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{2}) : (\sqrt{4} - \sqrt{3}) : \dots$$
  • Leaking Tap Droplet Spacing: If water drops detach at regular time intervals $\tau$, then at the instant the $N$-th drop is about to fall, the $k$-th drop ($k = 1, 2, \dots, N-1$) has fallen for time $(N-k)\tau$, placing it at distance $y_k = \frac{1}{2}g\tau^2(N-k)^2$ below the nozzle. Going from the tap downward, drop positions stand in the ratio $1 : 4 : 9 : 16$, and the gaps between consecutive drops stand in the ratio $1 : 3 : 5 : 7$.
Figure 17.1: Vertical projection exhibits exact kinematic symmetry across the apex ($t = u/g$), yielding Vieta's quadratic identities directly from $h = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$.
First-Principles Proof of the Height–Time Quadratic Identities

When a stone is projected vertically upward with initial velocity $u$ from ground ($y = 0$), its height $h$ at time $t$ satisfies:

$$h = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \implies gt^2 - 2ut + 2h = 0$$

For any height $h < H = \dfrac{u^2}{2g}$, this quadratic has two distinct positive roots $t_1$ (during ascent) and $t_2$ (during descent). Applying Vieta's formulas:

  • Sum of roots: $t_1 + t_2 = \dfrac{2u}{g} = T \implies u = \dfrac{g(t_1 + t_2)}{2}$.
  • Product of roots: $t_1 t_2 = \dfrac{2h}{g} \implies h = \dfrac{1}{2}g t_1 t_2$.
  • Maximum height: $H = \dfrac{u^2}{2g} = \dfrac{1}{2g}\left[\dfrac{g(t_1+t_2)}{2}\right]^2 = \dfrac{1}{8}g(t_1 + t_2)^2$.
  • Height above $h$ to the apex: $H - h = \dfrac{1}{8}g(t_1 + t_2)^2 - \dfrac{1}{2}gt_1 t_2 = \dfrac{1}{8}g\left[(t_1+t_2)^2 - 4t_1 t_2\right] = \dfrac{1}{8}g(t_2 - t_1)^2$.
  • Universal Last-Second Ascent Property: Regardless of initial velocity $u$ (provided $u \ge g$), the distance travelled during the last $1\text{ s}$ of ascent equals the distance fallen during the first $1\text{ s}$ of descent: $\Delta y = \frac{1}{2}g(1)^2 = \dfrac{g}{2}$ ($5\text{ m}$ if $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$).
Moving-Platform Release Rule (Rising/Descending Balloon or Elevator)

When an object is "dropped" or "gently released" from a moving balloon, helicopter, or elevator at height $H$ above the ground:

  1. Velocity IS inherited: At the instant of release, the object carries the exact instantaneous velocity $v_0$ of the platform (upward $+v_0$ if rising, downward $-v_0$ if descending).
  2. Acceleration is NOT inherited: Even if the balloon or elevator is accelerating upward at $a_{\text{lift}}$, the very millisecond the object loses physical contact, the only force acting on it is gravity, so its acceleration immediately becomes $a = -g$.

With upward $+y$ and origin at the release point, the ground sits at $y = -H$. Therefore, the time of flight $t$ is obtained in a single equation without splitting ascent and descent: $$-H = v_0 t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \implies \frac{1}{2}gt^2 - v_0 t - H = 0$$

4. One-Dimensional Relative Motion (Resonance / Olympiad Bridge)

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Transforming Into a Moving Frame

Whenever two bodies A and B move along the same straight line, switching from the ground frame to the reference frame of B collapses a two-body tracking problem into a single-body problem. Define relative position, velocity, and acceleration of A with respect to B as:

$$x_{AB} = x_A - x_B, \qquad v_{AB} = v_A - v_B, \qquad a_{AB} = a_A - a_B$$

If both $a_A$ and $a_B$ are constant, then $a_{AB}$ is constant, and all UAM equations hold intact in relative coordinates:

$$v_{AB} = u_{AB} + a_{AB}t, \qquad s_{AB} = u_{AB}t + \frac{1}{2}a_{AB}t^2, \qquad v_{AB}^2 - u_{AB}^2 = 2a_{AB}s_{AB}$$

Three High-Yield BEST Applications of 1D Relative Motion

  1. Overtaking Extended Bodies (Trains / Convoys): For Train A (length $L_A$) to completely overtake or cross Train B (length $L_B$), the rear of A must pass the front of B (same direction) or rear of B (opposite direction). In both cases, total relative displacement needed is $s_{\text{rel}} = L_A + L_B$: $$t_{\text{cross}} = \frac{L_A + L_B}{|v_A - v_B|} \text{ (same direction)}, \qquad t_{\text{cross}} = \frac{L_A + L_B}{v_A + v_B} \text{ (opposite directions)}$$
  2. Catching an Accelerating Bus (Minimum Velocity & Minimum Separation): Suppose a runner starts distance $d$ behind a stationary bus that accelerates forward at constant $a$, while the runner moves at constant speed $u$. In the bus's frame: $u_{\text{rel}} = u$, $a_{\text{rel}} = -a$, and the runner must cover $s_{\text{rel}} = d$.
    • Condition to just catch: Relative velocity reaches zero right at $s_{\text{rel}} = d$: $$0^2 - u_{\min}^2 = 2(-a)d \implies \mathbf{u_{\min} = \sqrt{2ad}}$$
    • If $u < \sqrt{2ad}$ (cannot catch): Separation shrinks as long as $v_{\text{rel}} > 0$ and widens once $v_{\text{bus}} > u$. Hence minimum separation occurs when $v_{\text{rel}} = 0$ (i.e., $v_{\text{bus}} = u$ at $t = u/a$), giving: $$d_{\min} = d - \frac{u^2}{2a}$$
    • If $u > \sqrt{2ad}$: The quadratic $\frac{1}{2}at^2 - ut + d = 0$ yields two positive roots $t_1 < t_2$: the runner overtakes the bus at $t_1$, and if the runner keeps going without boarding, the faster-accelerating bus overtakes the runner back at $t_2$.
  3. The Zero Relative-Acceleration Shortcut Under Gravity: Consider any two projectiles A and B simultaneously in free fall along a vertical line. Since both share the exact same gravitational acceleration $-g$: $$a_{AB} = (-g) - (-g) = \mathbf{0}$$ Relative velocity $v_{AB} = u_A - u_B$ remains strictly constant throughout their simultaneous flight! Hence, the meeting instant is simply: $$t_{\text{meet}} = \frac{\text{Initial separation } s_{\text{rel},0}}{\text{Initial relative speed of approach } v_{\text{approach}}}$$ Mandatory Sanity Check: Always verify that $t_{\text{meet}}$ is smaller than the ground-impact time of both stones; otherwise they never meet in mid-air.

Five Impossible-Graph Traps (Frequent BEST MCQ Eliminators)
  • Double-Valued Time: Any $x\text{-}t$, $v\text{-}t$, or $a\text{-}t$ graph that folds back on itself (fails the vertical line test, such as a circle or sideways parabola) is physically impossible—a single particle cannot occupy two positions or possess two velocities at the same instant.
  • Vertical Segment in $x\text{-}t$: Implies infinite slope ($\Delta x / \Delta t \to \infty$), i.e., infinite speed / instantaneous teleportation—impossible.
  • Decreasing Distance–Time Graph: While position $x(t)$ can rise and fall, total distance $d(t)$ can never have a negative slope because $\frac{dd}{dt} = |v| \ge 0$.
  • Negative Speed–Time Graph: Velocity $v(t)$ can go below the time axis, but speed $= |v(t)|$ can never be negative.
  • Sharp Kink (Corner) in Real $x\text{-}t$: A sharp corner implies an instantaneous jump in slope ($v$), requiring infinite acceleration ($a \to \infty$). (Note: Idealized textbook elastic-bounce models sometimes draw kinks, but strictly speaking a finite force always rounds the corner over a tiny contact time.)

Trapezoidal & Triangular $v\text{-}t$ Acceleration–Cruise–Retardation Profiles

A signature BEST / Resonance problem describes a train or vehicle starting from rest, accelerating uniformly at $\alpha$, optionally cruising at maximum speed $v_{\max}$, and decelerating uniformly at $\beta$ to rest. Always sketch the $v\text{-}t$ trapezoid first!

Figure 17.4: Trapezoidal $v\text{-}t$ profile. Setting the cruise duration $t_2 = 0$ immediately collapses the trapezoid into the minimum-time triangular profile.
First-Principles Proof of the Triangular & Trapezoidal Shortcuts

Case A (Triangular Profile, $t_2 = 0$): Since the body reaches peak speed $v_{\max}$ from rest at rate $\alpha$ and brakes to rest at rate $\beta$, we have $t_1 = \dfrac{v_{\max}}{\alpha}$ and $t_3 = \dfrac{v_{\max}}{\beta}$. Adding the two durations gives:

$$T = t_1 + t_3 = v_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}\right) = v_{\max}\left(\frac{\alpha + \beta}{\alpha\beta}\right) \implies \mathbf{v_{\max} = \left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha + \beta}\right)T}$$

Total distance $S$ equals the area of the triangle $\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} T v_{\max}$:

$$\mathbf{S = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha + \beta}\right)T^2}$$

Case B (Trapezoidal Profile, $t_2 > 0$): The area of the trapezoid with parallel bases $T$ and $t_2$ is $S = \frac{1}{2}(T + t_2)v_{\max}$. Substituting $t_2 = T - t_1 - t_3 = T - v_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}\right)$ yields:

$$S = v_{\max}T - \frac{v_{\max}^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}\right) \implies \mathbf{T = \frac{S}{v_{\max}} + \frac{v_{\max}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}\right)}$$

With just two algebraic rules of calculus—the Power Rule for Differentiation and the Power Rule for Integration—you unlock every non-uniform kinematics problem cleanly.

Olympiad Classic Derivation: Time Given as $t = \alpha x^2 + \beta x$

A famous NSEJS / Resonance Foundation problem states that time $t$ depends on position $x$ via $t = \alpha x^2 + \beta x$ (where $\alpha, \beta$ are positive constants) and asks for the retardation in terms of velocity $v$. Here is the complete first-principles derivation:

  1. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$: $$\frac{dt}{dx} = 2\alpha x + \beta$$
  2. Since $v = \dfrac{dx}{dt}$, we have $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{dt}{dx} = 2\alpha x + \beta$.
  3. Now differentiate both sides with respect to time $t$ (using the Chain Rule $\frac{d}{dt}(v^{-1}) = -v^{-2}\frac{dv}{dt}$): $$-\frac{1}{v^2}\frac{dv}{dt} = 2\alpha \frac{dx}{dt} + 0$$
  4. Substitute $\dfrac{dv}{dt} = a$ and $\dfrac{dx}{dt} = v$: $$-\frac{a}{v^2} = 2\alpha v \implies \mathbf{a = -2\alpha v^3}$$

Thus the retardation magnitude is $2\alpha v^3$—entirely independent of $\beta$!

Chapter 17 Formula Vault (High-Density Revision Grid)
  • Turnaround Distance: Solve $v(t_0) = 0$; if $t_1 < t_0 < t_2$, $d = |x(t_0)-x(t_1)| + |x(t_2)-x(t_0)|$.
  • Average Speeds: Equal times $\implies \frac{v_1+v_2}{2}$; Equal distances $\implies \frac{2v_1v_2}{v_1+v_2}$; Hybrid $\implies \frac{2v_0(v_1+v_2)}{2v_0+v_1+v_2}$.
  • UAM Core: $v = u + at$; $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$; $v^2 - u^2 = 2as$; $s = \left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)t$; $s_n = u + \frac{a}{2}(2n-1)$.
  • Mid-Point vs. Mid-Time: $v_{\text{mid-point}} = \sqrt{\frac{u^2+v^2}{2}} \ge \frac{u+v}{2} = v_{\text{mid-time}}$.
  • Stopping Distance: $S_{\text{total}} = u t_r + \frac{u^2}{2a_r}$.
  • Vertical Projection: $T = \frac{2u}{g}$; $H = \frac{u^2}{2g}$; $t_1+t_2 = \frac{2u}{g}$; $h = \frac{1}{2}gt_1 t_2$; $H = \frac{1}{8}g(t_1+t_2)^2$; $H-h = \frac{1}{8}g(t_2-t_1)^2$.
  • Relative Motion: Catching bus $u_{\min} = \sqrt{2ad}$; $d_{\min} = d - \frac{u^2}{2a}$. Free-fall pair: $a_{\text{rel}} = 0 \implies t_{\text{meet}} = s_{\text{rel},0}/v_{\text{approach}}$.
  • Triangular / Trapezoidal $v\text{-}t$: $v_{\max} = \frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}T$, $S = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta}T^2$; Trapezoid $T = \frac{S}{v_{\max}} + \frac{v_{\max}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\beta}\right)$.
  • Calculus Kinematics: $v = \frac{dx}{dt}$, $a = \frac{dv}{dt} = v\frac{dv}{dx}$; $\Delta v = \int a\,dt$; $\Delta x = \int v\,dt$; $\frac{v^2-u^2}{2} = \int a\,dx$.

Level 1 Turnaround Distance & Averages Worked Example 1: Polynomial Velocity Reversal
A particle moves along the $x$-axis such that its position (in metres) as a function of time $t$ (in seconds) is given by $x(t) = 2t^2 - 8t + 10$. Calculate (a) the displacement, (b) the total distance travelled, (c) the average velocity, and (d) the average speed during the time interval from $t = 0\text{ s}$ to $t = 5\text{ s}$.

Step 1 — Check for a turning point inside $(0, 5)\text{ s}$:

Differentiate $x(t)$ with respect to $t$ to get instantaneous velocity:

$$v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = 4t - 8$$

Set $v(t) = 0$: $4t - 8 = 0 \implies t_0 = 2\text{ s}$. Because $0 < 2 < 5$, the particle moves in the $-x$ direction for $t \in [0, 2]\text{ s}$, stops momentarily at $t = 2\text{ s}$, and reverses into the $+x$ direction for $t \in [2, 5]\text{ s}$.

Step 2 — Evaluate positions at boundaries and the turning instant:

  • At $t = 0\text{ s}$: $x(0) = 2(0)^2 - 8(0) + 10 = 10\text{ m}$.
  • At $t = 2\text{ s}$: $x(2) = 2(2)^2 - 8(2) + 10 = 8 - 16 + 10 = 2\text{ m}$.
  • At $t = 5\text{ s}$: $x(5) = 2(5)^2 - 8(5) + 10 = 50 - 40 + 10 = 20\text{ m}$.

Step 3 — Compute all four required quantities:

  1. Displacement: $\Delta x = x(5) - x(0) = 20 - 10 = \mathbf{+10\text{ m}}$.
  2. Total Distance: $d = |x(2) - x(0)| + |x(5) - x(2)| = |2 - 10| + |20 - 2| = 8 + 18 = \mathbf{26\text{ m}}$.
  3. Average Velocity: $v_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{+10\text{ m}}{5\text{ s}} = \mathbf{+2\text{ m/s}}$.
  4. Average Speed: $\langle \text{speed} \rangle = \dfrac{d}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{26\text{ m}}{5\text{ s}} = \mathbf{5.2\text{ m/s}}$.
Level 1 Stopping Distance & Reaction Time Worked Example 2: Doubling Speed with Non-Zero Reaction Time
A car driver has a reaction time of $t_r = 0.5\text{ s}$. Once the brakes are applied, the car decelerates uniformly at $a_r = 6\text{ m/s}^2$. Calculate the total stopping distance when the car moves at $u_1 = 12\text{ m/s}$ and when its speed is doubled to $u_2 = 24\text{ m/s}$. By what exact factor does the total stopping distance increase?

Step 1 — Recall the two-stage stopping distance relation:

$$S_{\text{total}} = s_{\text{reaction}} + s_{\text{braking}} = u t_r + \frac{u^2}{2a_r}$$

Step 2 — Compute for $u_1 = 12\text{ m/s}$:

$$s_{r1} = (12)(0.5) = 6\text{ m}, \qquad s_{b1} = \frac{12^2}{2(6)} = \frac{144}{12} = 12\text{ m}$$ $$S_1 = 6 + 12 = \mathbf{18\text{ m}}$$

Step 3 — Compute for $u_2 = 24\text{ m/s}$:

$$s_{r2} = (24)(0.5) = 12\text{ m}, \qquad s_{b2} = \frac{24^2}{2(6)} = \frac{576}{12} = 48\text{ m}$$ $$S_2 = 12 + 48 = \mathbf{60\text{ m}}$$

Step 4 — Ratio comparison:

$$\frac{S_2}{S_1} = \frac{60}{18} = \mathbf{\frac{10}{3}} \approx 3.33$$

Notice that while the braking distance quadrupled ($12\text{ m} \to 48\text{ m}$), the reaction distance only doubled ($6\text{ m} \to 12\text{ m}$), so the overall factor is $\frac{10}{3}$, not $4$.

Level 1 Gravity Symmetry Worked Example 3: Passing a Window Twice During Vertical Flight
A stone is thrown vertically upward from the ground with initial speed $u$. It passes a window at height $h$ twice—at $t_1 = 2\text{ s}$ while rising and at $t_2 = 6\text{ s}$ while falling. Taking $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$, find (a) the initial speed $u$, (b) the height $h$ of the window, and (c) the maximum height $H$ reached by the stone.

Step 1 — Set up the vertical position quadratic (upward $+y$, ground $y = 0$):

$$h = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2 \implies 5t^2 - ut + h = 0$$

The given instants $t_1 = 2\text{ s}$ and $t_2 = 6\text{ s}$ are the two roots of this quadratic equation.

Step 2 — Apply Vieta's Sum and Product of Roots:

  • (a) Initial speed $u$: $$t_1 + t_2 = \frac{u}{5} \implies 2 + 6 = \frac{u}{5} \implies u = \mathbf{40\text{ m/s}}$$
  • (b) Window height $h$: $$t_1 t_2 = \frac{h}{5} \implies (2)(6) = \frac{h}{5} \implies h = \mathbf{60\text{ m}}$$ Verification: At $t = 2\text{ s}$, $y = 40(2) - 5(2)^2 = 80 - 20 = 60\text{ m}$; at $t = 6\text{ s}$, $y = 40(6) - 5(6)^2 = 240 - 180 = 60\text{ m}$. Confirmed!
  • (c) Maximum height $H$: $$H = \frac{u^2}{2g} = \frac{40^2}{2(10)} = \frac{1600}{20} = \mathbf{80\text{ m}}$$ Cross-check via identity: $H = \frac{1}{8}g(t_1+t_2)^2 = \frac{10}{8}(8)^2 = 80\text{ m}$.
Tier 2 — Level 2: Exam-Real BEST / NSEJS Applications (Examples 4–6)
Level 2 $n$-th Second Reversal Trap Worked Example 4: Distance vs. Displacement Inside the 3rd Second
A particle is projected along a straight line with an initial velocity $u = 25\text{ m/s}$ and undergoes a constant opposing acceleration $a = -10\text{ m/s}^2$. Find (a) the net displacement and (b) the exact distance travelled by the particle during the 3rd second of its motion ($t = 2\text{ s}$ to $t = 3\text{ s}$).

Step 1 — Locate the turning instant $t_0$:

$$v(t_0) = u + a t_0 = 0 \implies 25 - 10t_0 = 0 \implies t_0 = 2.5\text{ s}$$

Since $2\text{ s} < 2.5\text{ s} < 3\text{ s}$, the particle reverses direction exactly in the middle of the 3rd second.

Step 2 — (a) Net displacement during the 3rd second ($n = 3$):

$$s_3 = u + \frac{a}{2}(2n - 1) = 25 + \frac{-10}{2}\left(2(3) - 1\right) = 25 - 5(5) = \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$$

Direct verification: $x(2) = 25(2) - 5(2)^2 = 30\text{ m}$ and $x(3) = 25(3) - 5(3)^2 = 75 - 45 = 30\text{ m}$, confirming $\Delta x = 30 - 30 = 0\text{ m}$.

Step 3 — (b) Exact distance during the 3rd second (split at $t_0 = 2.5\text{ s}$):

  • Position at $t_0 = 2.5\text{ s}$: $x(2.5) = 25(2.5) - 5(2.5)^2 = 62.5 - 31.25 = 31.25\text{ m}$.
  • Forward leg ($t = 2\text{ s}$ to $2.5\text{ s}$): $d_1 = |x(2.5) - x(2)| = |31.25 - 30| = 1.25\text{ m}$.
  • Backward leg ($t = 2.5\text{ s}$ to $3\text{ s}$): $d_2 = |x(3) - x(2.5)| = |30 - 31.25| = 1.25\text{ m}$.
$$d_{\text{3rd sec}} = d_1 + d_2 = 1.25 + 1.25 = \mathbf{2.5\text{ m}}$$

Formula check: Because $t_0 = n - 0.5$, $d_n = \frac{|a|}{4} = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5\text{ m}$. Never blindly report $0\text{ m}$ when the question asks for distance!

Level 2 Moving-Platform Gravity Worked Example 5: Sandbag Released from an Ascending Balloon
A hot-air balloon is rising vertically at a constant velocity of $10\text{ m/s}$. When the balloon is $75\text{ m}$ above the ground, a sandbag is gently released from it. Taking $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$, find (a) the maximum height above the ground attained by the sandbag, (b) the total time taken by the sandbag to strike the ground, and (c) the height of the balloon above the ground at the moment the sandbag hits.

Step 1 — Establish sign convention and initial state of the sandbag:

Take upward as $+y$ and place the origin $y = 0$ at the release point ($75\text{ m}$ above ground). Thus the ground is at $y = -75\text{ m}$. Upon release, the sandbag inherits the balloon's upward velocity: $u = +10\text{ m/s}$, and immediately enters free fall: $a = -g = -10\text{ m/s}^2$.

Step 2 — (a) Maximum height above ground:

Additional height climbed above the release point until $v = 0$:

$$\Delta h = \frac{u^2}{2g} = \frac{10^2}{2(10)} = 5\text{ m}$$ $$H_{\max} = 75\text{ m} + 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{80\text{ m}}$$

Step 3 — (b) Total time of flight to ground ($y = -75\text{ m}$):

Apply $y = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$ directly for the entire trip:

$$-75 = 10t - \frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 \implies 5t^2 - 10t - 75 = 0 \implies t^2 - 2t - 15 = 0$$ $$(t - 5)(t + 3) = 0$$

Discarding the non-physical negative root $t = -3\text{ s}$, we get $t = \mathbf{5\text{ s}}$.

Step 4 — (c) Height of the balloon at $t = 5\text{ s}$:

The balloon continues ascending at constant speed $10\text{ m/s}$:

$$H_{\text{balloon}} = 75 + (10)(5) = \mathbf{125\text{ m}}$$

Relative-frame sanity check: Relative to the balloon, $u_{\text{rel}} = 0$ and $a_{\text{rel}} = g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$ downward, so after $5\text{ s}$ the sandbag is $\frac{1}{2}gt^2 = \frac{1}{2}(10)(25) = 125\text{ m}$ below the balloon—which puts the balloon exactly $125\text{ m}$ above the ground!

Level 2 1D Relative Motion Worked Example 6: Catching an Accelerating Bus & Two-Root Physics
A student stands $36\text{ m}$ behind the rear door of a stationary bus. At $t = 0$, the bus begins accelerating forward uniformly at $a = 2\text{ m/s}^2$, and simultaneously the student runs toward the bus at a constant speed $u$.
(a) Find the minimum running speed $u_{\min}$ required for the student to just catch the bus.
(b) If the student runs at $u = 10\text{ m/s}$, find the minimum distance between the student and the bus.
(c) If the student runs at $u = 13\text{ m/s}$, find the time when the student first catches the bus and interpret the second mathematical root.

Step 1 — Switch to the reference frame of the bus:

Taking forward as $+x$: initial relative velocity of student w.r.t. bus is $u_{\text{rel}} = u - 0 = +u$; relative acceleration is $a_{\text{rel}} = 0 - a = -2\text{ m/s}^2$; required relative displacement to catch the bus is $s_{\text{rel}} = +36\text{ m}$.

Step 2 — (a) Minimum speed $u_{\min}$:

To just catch the bus, the student's relative velocity drops to zero right upon closing the $36\text{ m}$ gap:

$$v_{\text{rel}}^2 - u_{\min}^2 = 2 a_{\text{rel}} s_{\text{rel}} \implies 0 - u_{\min}^2 = 2(-2)(36) = -144 \implies u_{\min} = \mathbf{12\text{ m/s}}$$

Step 3 — (b) Closest approach for $u = 10\text{ m/s}$:

Since $10\text{ m/s} < 12\text{ m/s}$, the student cannot catch the bus. Closest approach occurs when $v_{\text{rel}} = 0$ (i.e., $v_{\text{bus}} = 10\text{ m/s}$ at $t = 10/2 = 5\text{ s}$). Maximum relative distance gained:

$$s_{\text{rel, max}} = \frac{u^2}{2a} = \frac{10^2}{2(2)} = 25\text{ m}$$ $$d_{\min} = 36 - 25 = \mathbf{11\text{ m}}$$

Step 4 — (c) Catching instants for $u = 13\text{ m/s}$:

Set $s_{\text{rel}} = u_{\text{rel}}t + \frac{1}{2}a_{\text{rel}}t^2 = 36$:

$$13t + \frac{1}{2}(-2)t^2 = 36 \implies t^2 - 13t + 36 = 0 \implies (t - 4)(t - 9) = 0$$

The student first catches the bus at $t_1 = \mathbf{4\text{ s}}$ (at $x = 52\text{ m}$, where the bus speed is only $8\text{ m/s}$). Physical meaning of $t_2 = 9\text{ s}$: If the student keeps sprinting past the bus without boarding, the continuously accelerating bus reaches $18\text{ m/s}$ and overtakes the student again at $t_2 = 9\text{ s}$ (at $x = 117\text{ m}$).

Tier 3 — Level 3: Rank-Booster Problems & 30-Second Shortcuts (Examples 7–9)
Level 3 Two-Body Gravity & Relative Frame Worked Example 7: Simultaneous Drop & Upward Launch
From the top of a tower of height $H = 100\text{ m}$, Stone A is dropped from rest. At the exact same instant, Stone B is projected vertically upward from the base of the tower with initial speed $u_B = 40\text{ m/s}$. Taking $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$, find (a) the time $t$ when the two stones pass each other, (b) the height above the ground where they meet, and (c) whether Stone B is rising or falling at that instant.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Ground Frame):

Choose upward as $+y$ and ground level as $y = 0$. Both stones have $a = -10\text{ m/s}^2$.

  • Position of Stone A ($y_{A0} = 100\text{ m}$, $u_A = 0$): $y_A(t) = 100 - \frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 = 100 - 5t^2$.
  • Position of Stone B ($y_{B0} = 0$, $u_B = +40\text{ m/s}$): $y_B(t) = 40t - \frac{1}{2}(10)t^2 = 40t - 5t^2$.

(a) Meeting time: Equate $y_A(t) = y_B(t)$:

$$100 - 5t^2 = 40t - 5t^2 \implies 40t = 100 \implies t = \mathbf{2.5\text{ s}}$$

Validity check: Stone A reaches ground at $t_A = \sqrt{200/10} = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47\text{ s} > 2.5\text{ s}$, and Stone B's flight time is $T_B = 2(40)/10 = 8\text{ s} > 2.5\text{ s}$, so they genuinely meet in mid-air.

(b) Height above ground:

$$y = 100 - 5(2.5)^2 = 100 - 5(6.25) = 100 - 31.25 = \mathbf{68.75\text{ m}}$$

(c) Direction of Stone B at $t = 2.5\text{ s}$:

$$v_B(2.5) = 40 - 10(2.5) = +15\text{ m/s} > 0$$

Since $v_B > 0$, Stone B is still rising (it does not reach its apex until $t = 40/10 = 4\text{ s}$).

30-Second BEST Exam Shortcut: Zero Relative Acceleration

Because both stones are in free fall simultaneously, $a_{BA} = (-g) - (-g) = 0$. Their relative speed of approach is strictly constant: $v_{\text{approach}} = u_B - u_A = 40 - 0 = 40\text{ m/s}$.

1) $t_{\text{meet}} = \dfrac{H}{v_{\text{approach}}} = \dfrac{100}{40} = \mathbf{2.5\text{ s}}$ (done in 5 seconds!).

2) Distance fallen by Stone A from top $= 5(2.5)^2 = 31.25\text{ m} \implies h_{\text{ground}} = 100 - 31.25 = \mathbf{68.75\text{ m}}$.

3) Ascent time of B $= 40/10 = 4\text{ s} > 2.5\text{ s} \implies$ B is rising.

Level 3 Trapezoidal $v\text{-}t$ Optimization Worked Example 8: Metro Train Uncapped vs. Speed-Capped Journey
A metro train starts from rest at Station P, accelerates uniformly at $\alpha = 2\text{ m/s}^2$, optionally cruises at constant speed, and decelerates uniformly at $\beta = 3\text{ m/s}^2$ to stop at Station Q, located $S = 1500\text{ m}$ away.
(a) With no speed limit, find the minimum possible journey time $T_{\min}$ and the peak speed $v_p$ reached.
(b) If safety rules cap the speed at $v_{\max} = 30\text{ m/s}$, find the total journey time $T$ and the ratio $v_{\text{avg}} / v_{\max}$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

(a) Minimum time (triangular $v\text{-}t$ profile, zero cruise time): To minimize total time for fixed area $S$, the train must accelerate up to peak speed $v_p$ for time $t_1$ and immediately brake to rest for time $t_3$. Using $v_p = \alpha t_1 = \beta t_3$:

$$t_1 = \frac{v_p}{2}, \qquad t_3 = \frac{v_p}{3} \implies T_{\min} = t_1 + t_3 = v_p\left(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{5}{6}v_p \implies v_p = \frac{6}{5}T_{\min}$$

Equate the area of the $v\text{-}t$ triangle to $S = 1500\text{ m}$:

$$S = \frac{1}{2} v_p T_{\min} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{6}{5}T_{\min}\right)T_{\min} = \frac{3}{5}T_{\min}^2 = 1500 \implies T_{\min}^2 = 2500 \implies T_{\min} = \mathbf{50\text{ s}}$$ $$v_p = \frac{6}{5}(50) = \mathbf{60\text{ m/s}}$$

(b) Speed-capped journey ($v_{\max} = 30\text{ m/s}$, trapezoidal $v\text{-}t$ profile):

  • Phase I (Acceleration at $2\text{ m/s}^2$): $t_1 = \dfrac{30}{2} = 15\text{ s}$; $S_1 = \frac{1}{2}(30)(15) = 225\text{ m}$.
  • Phase III (Braking at $3\text{ m/s}^2$): $t_3 = \dfrac{30}{3} = 10\text{ s}$; $S_3 = \frac{1}{2}(30)(10) = 150\text{ m}$.
  • Phase II (Cruise at $30\text{ m/s}$): Remaining distance $S_2 = 1500 - (225 + 150) = 1125\text{ m}$, so $t_2 = \dfrac{1125}{30} = 37.5\text{ s}$.

Total journey time: $T = 15 + 37.5 + 10 = \mathbf{62.5\text{ s}}$. Average speed: $v_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{1500}{62.5} = 24\text{ m/s}$, so $\dfrac{v_{\text{avg}}}{v_{\max}} = \dfrac{24}{30} = \mathbf{\dfrac{4}{5}} = 0.8$.

30-Second BEST Exam Shortcut: Direct Profile Formulas

(a) Compute $\dfrac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+\beta} = \dfrac{2 \times 3}{2 + 3} = \dfrac{6}{5}$. Then $S = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{6}{5}\right)T_{\min}^2 = 1500 \implies T_{\min} = \mathbf{50\text{ s}}$ and $v_p = \frac{6}{5}(50) = \mathbf{60\text{ m/s}}$.

(b) Use the one-line trapezoid time formula: $$T = \frac{S}{v_{\max}} + \frac{v_{\max}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta}\right) = \frac{1500}{30} + \frac{30}{2}\left(\frac{5}{6}\right) = 50 + 12.5 = \mathbf{62.5\text{ s}}$$ Then $v_{\text{avg}} / v_{\max} = \dfrac{S / T}{v_{\max}} = \dfrac{50}{62.5} = \mathbf{0.8}$.

Level 3 Calculus Kinematics Bridge ($a(x)$) Worked Example 9: Position-Dependent Acceleration via $a = v\dfrac{dv}{dx}$
A particle starts from the origin ($x = 0$) with an initial velocity $u = 3\text{ m/s}$ and moves along the $+x$-axis under a position-dependent acceleration $a(x) = (4 - x)\text{ m/s}^2$, where $x$ is in metres. Find:
(a) the velocity $v$ as a function of position $x$,
(b) the maximum velocity $v_{\max}$ attained and the coordinate where it occurs, and
(c) the maximum positive coordinate $x_{\max}$ reached before the particle reverses direction.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Calculus Integration):

Because acceleration is given as a function of $x$ (NOT $t$), standard UAM equations are completely invalid. Use the chain-rule identity $a = v\dfrac{dv}{dx}$:

$$v\frac{dv}{dx} = 4 - x \implies v\,dv = (4 - x)\,dx$$

(a) Velocity as a function of $x$: Integrate from the initial state $(x = 0, v = 3)$ to an arbitrary state $(x, v)$:

$$\int_3^v v\,dv = \int_0^x (4 - x)\,dx \implies \left[\frac{v^2}{2}\right]_3^v = \left[4x - \frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^x$$ $$\frac{v^2 - 9}{2} = 4x - \frac{x^2}{2} \implies v^2 = 9 + 8x - x^2 \implies \mathbf{v(x) = \sqrt{9 + 8x - x^2}}$$

(b) Maximum velocity $v_{\max}$: Velocity peaks when $\dfrac{dv}{dx} = 0$, which (for $v > 0$) occurs precisely when $a(x) = 0$:

$$4 - x = 0 \implies \mathbf{x = 4\text{ m}}$$

Substitute $x = 4\text{ m}$ into $v^2 = 9 + 8x - x^2$:

$$v_{\max}^2 = 9 + 8(4) - 4^2 = 9 + 32 - 16 = 25 \implies \mathbf{v_{\max} = 5\text{ m/s}}$$

(c) Maximum coordinate $x_{\max}$ (turning point where $v = 0$):

$$9 + 8x - x^2 = 0 \implies x^2 - 8x - 9 = 0 \implies (x - 9)(x + 1) = 0$$

Since the particle starts at $x = 0$ moving in the $+x$ direction, it first stops and reverses at $\mathbf{x_{\max} = 9\text{ m}}$.

30-Second BEST Exam Shortcut: Signed Area Under the $a\text{-}x$ Graph

Recall $\dfrac{v^2 - u^2}{2} = \text{Signed Area under } a\text{-}x$. Plot the straight line $a(x) = 4 - x$ (intercepts at $a = 4$ and $x = 4$, slope $-1$):

1) Peak velocity occurs at the $x$-intercept ($x = 4\text{ m}$). The positive triangle from $x = 0$ to $4$ has area $A_+ = \frac{1}{2}(4)(4) = +8\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2$. Hence $v_{\max}^2 = u^2 + 2A_+ = 3^2 + 2(8) = 25 \implies \mathbf{v_{\max} = 5\text{ m/s}}$.

2) Turning point ($v = 0$) requires total net area $= \frac{0 - 3^2}{2} = -4.5$. Thus the negative triangle beyond $x = 4$ (with base $b = x_{\max} - 4$ and height $b$) must have area $\frac{1}{2}b^2 = 8 + 4.5 = 12.5 \implies b^2 = 25 \implies b = 5 \implies \mathbf{x_{\max} = 4 + 5 = 9\text{ m}}$.
