Trap & Error Forensics — The 5 Deadliest Chapter 1 Killers

In BEST Paper 1, top-5% students rarely lose marks because they do not know a theorem—they lose marks to five recurring structural traps engineered directly into MCQ distractors and NAT grids. Audit every solution you write against this checklist.

Trap Forensics #1: Forgetting to Restore the Cancelled Common Factor (Q22)

The Fatal Mistake: When computing $\operatorname{Rem}\!\left(\dfrac{14^{99} + 21^{99}}{539}\right)$, students rightly cancel $49$ from numerator and denominator to evaluate $\operatorname{Rem}\!\left(\dfrac{2^{99}\cdot 7^{97} + 3^{99}\cdot 7^{97}}{11}\right) = 5$, and then immediately bubble 005 into the NAT box—forgetting to multiply back by $49$ to get 245.

Diagnostic Cure: Whenever you cancel a factor $k$ out of a remainder problem, immediately draw a bold boxed reminder “$\times\, k$ AT END!” next to your final answer slot before simplifying a single exponent. Better yet, use the Zero-Remainder CRT split ($N = 49k$ directly), which structurally prevents dropping $49$.

Trap Forensics #2: Blindly Applying Euler’s Theorem to Non-Coprime Bases (Q11, Q22)

The Fatal Mistake: Computing $2^{2026} \pmod{100}$ by writing “$\phi(100) = 40$, so $2^{40} \equiv 1 \pmod{100}$, hence $2^{2026} \equiv 2^{26} \pmod{100}$.” That is catastrophically false because $\gcd(2, 100) = 2 \neq 1$: in fact $2^{40} \equiv 76 \not\equiv 1 \pmod{100}$, and while $2^{26} \equiv 64$ happens to agree for $m \ge 2$ only because $76 \cdot 2^m \equiv 2^m$, the same false logic predicts $2^{2000} \equiv 2^0 = 1 \pmod{100}$ when the true value is $76$!

Diagnostic Cure: Before invoking $a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$, perform a mandatory 1-second coprimality check: “Is $\gcd(a, n) = 1$?” If NO, immediately split $n$ into coprime prime-power factors via CRT (e.g., $100 = 4 \times 25$, where $2^k \equiv 0 \pmod 4$ and $\gcd(2, 25) = 1$ allows Euler’s Theorem safely modulo $25$).

Trap Forensics #3: Ordered vs. Unordered Pairs & Boundary Constraints (Q5, Q20)

The Fatal Mistake: In Q5, students compute $\operatorname{LCM}(6, 9, 15) + 4 = 94$ and enter 094, missing the single adjective “smallest $3$-digit positive integer” ($184$). In Q20, students compute $2^k = 2^3 = 8$ for $xy = 42$ and select Option (D), ignoring the strict ordering condition $a \lt b$ that cuts $8$ ordered pairs down to $4$.

Diagnostic Cure: Underline three high-risk constraint words during your first reading of every problem: digit count ($3$-digit / $4$-digit), ordering ($a \lt b$ vs ordered pairs), and sign/positivity (smallest positive vs non-negative).

Trap Forensics #4: Dropping Absolute Value Bars When Denesting Radicals (Q19)

The Fatal Mistake: Writing $\sqrt{13 - 4\sqrt{3}} = \sqrt{(1 - 2\sqrt{3})^2} = 1 - 2\sqrt{3}$ because $1$ was listed before $12$. Adding $(4 - 2\sqrt{3}) + (1 - 2\sqrt{3})$ produces the exact distractor Option (B): $5 - 4\sqrt{3}$! Remember that the principal square root $\sqrt{u}$ is always non-negative, whereas $1 - 2\sqrt{3} \approx -2.464 \lt 0$.

Diagnostic Cure: Always write the larger term first inside $\sqrt{A - 2\sqrt{B}} = \sqrt{x} - \sqrt{y}$ (enforce $x \ge y$ by comparing squares $x$ and $y$, never by eyeballing coefficients).

Trap Forensics #5: The $a^b$ vs $b^a$ Direction Reversal & Osculator Remainder Misuse (Q2, Q28)

The Fatal Mistake: (a) Students test $2^3 = 8 \lt 3^2 = 9$, conclude “larger base wins,” and mistakenly mark $e^\pi \lt \pi^e$ as TRUE in Q28—forgetting that $2 \lt e$ sits on the increasing side of $f(x) = x^{1/x}$, whereas every $x \ge e$ sits on the decreasing side where the smaller base (larger exponent) wins. (b) In divisibility problems, students use an osculator (like $q + 2u$ for $19$) to find a non-zero remainder, forgetting that $q + 2u \equiv 2N \pmod{19}$, not $N \pmod{19}$.

Diagnostic Cure: Anchor $e \approx 2.72$ mentally as the summit of $x^{1/x}$: to the right of $e$ (all integers $\ge 3$, plus $\pi$), smaller base always beats larger base ($e^\pi \gt \pi^e$, $3^4 \gt 4^3$, $2026^{2027} \gt 2027^{2026}$). Restrict osculators strictly to testing zero remainder (exact divisibility); for non-zero remainders, always use block rules ($1001, 999, 99$) or direct modular arithmetic.
