25.1 Physical Properties & High-Yield Exceptions

Of the 118 known elements, roughly $78\%$ are metals, positioned on the left and center of the periodic table, while non-metals occupy the upper-right block separated by the diagonal metalloid staircase ($\text{B}, \text{Si}, \text{Ge}, \text{As}, \text{Sb}, \text{Te}, \text{Po}$). In competitive exams like BEST and NSEJS, examiners rarely ask for the textbook rule — they target the exceptions that break the rule and test the underlying atomic bonding responsible for each anomaly.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Why Metals Are Malleable Yet Ionic Crystals Shatter

In a metal, positively charged atomic kernels sit embedded in a delocalized sea of valence electrons. Because metallic bonding is non-directional, sliding one plane of atoms over another merely shifts the electron sea around the new positions — the metallic bond never breaks, yielding malleability and ductility. By contrast, graphite is soft and slippery because weak van der Waals forces separate its flat $sp^2$ sheets, whereas diamond's rigid, directional $sp^3$ tetrahedral network makes it both ultra-hard and an electrical insulator.

25.2 Chemical Properties, Amphoteric Oxides & Acid Traps

Metals are electropositive (electron donors forming cations: $\text{M} \to \text{M}^{n+} + ne^-$) and act as reducing agents. Non-metals are electronegative (electron acceptors forming anions: $\text{X} + ne^- \to \text{X}^{n-}$) and act as oxidizing agents. Every chemical reaction in this chapter is ultimately governed by how readily a metal loses electrons.

A. Reaction with Oxygen & The Four Classes of Oxides

Heating metals in air yields metal oxides ($\text{Metal} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{Metal Oxide}$), with vigor tracking reactivity:

  • $\text{K}$ and $\text{Na}$: Catch fire violently in open air; hence stored immersed in kerosene oil (non-polar, oxygen-free barrier).
  • $\text{Mg}, \text{Al}, \text{Zn}, \text{Pb}$: At ordinary temperatures, their surfaces rapidly develop a thin, adherent oxide layer that prevents further deep oxidation (self-passivation). Heating $\text{Mg}$ ribbon burns with a dazzling white flame forming $\text{MgO}$.
  • $\text{Fe}$: Solid iron bars do not burn in a Bunsen flame, but fine iron filings burn vigorously with bright sparks (huge surface-area-to-volume ratio).
  • $\text{Cu}$: Does not burn, but coats with a black layer of copper(II) oxide: $2\text{Cu}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{CuO}(s)$.
  • $\text{Ag}$ and $\text{Au}$: Do not react with oxygen even at very high temperatures.
Master Classification of Oxides (Zero-Confusion Matrix)
  1. Basic Oxides (Most Metal Oxides): React with acids to give salt + water. Soluble ones (alkalis) dissolve in water to give $\text{OH}^-$ ions: $$\text{Na}_2\text{O}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \to 2\text{NaOH}(aq), \qquad \text{K}_2\text{O}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \to 2\text{KOH}(aq), \qquad \text{CaO}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \to \text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq)$$
  2. Acidic Oxides (Most Non-Metal Oxides): Dissolve in water to form oxyacids and neutralize bases: $$\text{SO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \to \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3(aq), \qquad \text{CO}_2(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \to \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3(aq), \qquad \text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}(s) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \to 4\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4(aq)$$
  3. Neutral Oxides (3 Mandatory Non-Metal Oxides): $\text{CO}$, $\text{NO}$, and $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ (plus $\text{H}_2\text{O}$). They show neither acidic nor basic behavior and do not affect litmus.
  4. Amphoteric Oxides (Exam Goldmine): Metal oxides that react with both acids and strong bases to produce salt and water. Core four: $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $\text{ZnO}$, $\text{PbO}$, $\text{SnO}$ (plus $\text{BeO}, \text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3, \text{Sb}_2\text{O}_3$ at Olympiad level). $$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 6\text{HCl}(aq) \to 2\text{AlCl}_3(aq) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$ $$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \to \underbrace{2\text{NaAlO}_2(aq)}_{\text{Sodium aluminate}} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$ $$\text{ZnO}(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \to \text{ZnCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$ $$\text{ZnO}(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \to \underbrace{\text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2(aq)}_{\text{Sodium zincate}} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$ $$\text{PbO}(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \to \underbrace{\text{Na}_2\text{PbO}_2(aq)}_{\text{Sodium plumbite}} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Oxidation State Rule for Transition Metal Oxides

Not all metal oxides are basic! As the oxidation state of a transition metal increases, its cation becomes smaller and more polarizing (Fajan's Rules), shifting oxide character from basic → amphoteric → acidic:

  • Chromium: $\text{CrO}$ ($+2$, Basic) $\longrightarrow$ $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$ ($+3$, Amphoteric) $\longrightarrow$ $\text{CrO}_3$ ($+6$, Acidic — forms chromic acid $\text{H}_2\text{CrO}_4$).
  • Manganese: $\text{MnO}$ ($+2$, Basic) $\longrightarrow$ $\text{MnO}_2$ ($+4$, Amphoteric) $\longrightarrow$ $\text{Mn}_2\text{O}_7$ ($+7$, Strongly Acidic — forms $\text{HMnO}_4$).

Also note that amphoteric metals themselves dissolve directly in hot aqueous $\text{NaOH}$ releasing $\text{H}_2$: $$2\text{Al} + 2\text{NaOH} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{NaAlO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\uparrow, \qquad \text{Zn} + 2\text{NaOH} \to \text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2 + \text{H}_2\uparrow$$

C. Reaction with Acids & The $\text{HNO}_3$ / Passivation / Aqua Regia Triad

With non-oxidizing dilute acids ($\text{HCl}$ and dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), metals above hydrogen displace $\text{H}_2$ gas: $$\text{Metal} + \text{Dilute Acid} \to \text{Metal Salt} + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow$$ In a standard classroom calorimetry test comparing equal masses of $\text{Mg}, \text{Al}, \text{Zn}, \text{Fe}$ with dilute $\text{HCl}$, both the rate of effervescence and the temperature rise strictly follow $\text{Mg} \gt \text{Al} \gt \text{Zn} \gt \text{Fe}$. Copper shows zero bubbles and zero temperature rise.

Olympiad Trap #1 — Why Nitric Acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) Usually Refuses to Liberate $\text{H}_2$

The Core Mechanism: Nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) is a powerful oxidizing agent (nitrogen is in its maximum $+5$ oxidation state). Instead of letting $\text{H}_2$ escape, $\text{HNO}_3$ immediately oxidizes nascent hydrogen to $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ while reducing itself to nitrogen oxides ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$, $\text{NO}$, or $\text{NO}_2$) depending on acid concentration:

$$3\text{Cu} + 8\text{HNO}_3(\text{dilute}) \to 3\text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{NO}(g) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$$ $$\text{Cu} + 4\text{HNO}_3(\text{conc.}) \to \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{NO}_2(g)\text{ (brown fumes)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

The Mandatory Two-Metal Exception: Very dilute ($\approx 1\text{--}2\%$) $\text{HNO}_3$ reacts with Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) and Manganese ($\text{Mn}$) to liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas because its oxidizing power at extreme dilution is too weak to intercept the fast $\text{H}_2$ evolution:

$$\text{Mg}(s) + 2\text{HNO}_3(\text{very dil.}) \to \text{Mg(NO}_3)_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow$$ $$\text{Mn}(s) + 2\text{HNO}_3(\text{very dil.}) \to \text{Mn(NO}_3)_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow$$

Passivation: Concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ renders $\text{Al}$, $\text{Cr}$, and $\text{Fe}$ completely passive by oxidizing their surface into an ultra-thin, impervious, non-porous protective oxide barrier ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3, \text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3, \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$). Consequently, concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ can be safely stored and transported in aluminium tankers!

Aqua Regia ("Royal Water") — Dissolving Gold & Platinum

Composition: Freshly prepared mixture of 3 parts concentrated $\text{HCl}$ : 1 part concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ by volume. Neither acid alone can dissolve gold ($\text{Au}$) or platinum ($\text{Pt}$), yet together they succeed through a dual redox + complexation tandem:

$$\text{HNO}_3 + 3\text{HCl} \to \text{NOCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2[\text{Cl}]\text{ (nascent chlorine)}$$

First, $\text{HNO}_3$ oxidizes trace $\text{Au}$ atoms to $\text{Au}^{3+}$; immediately, high-concentration $\text{Cl}^-$ from $\text{HCl}$ complexes $\text{Au}^{3+}$ into the ultra-stable soluble tetrachloroaurate(III) ion $[\text{AuCl}_4]^-$, pulling the equilibrium completely forward:

$$\text{Au} + 4\text{HCl} + \text{HNO}_3 \to \underbrace{\text{HAuCl}_4}_{\text{Chloroauric acid}} + \text{NO}\uparrow + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$ $$3\text{Pt} + 18\text{HCl} + 4\text{HNO}_3 \to \underbrace{3\text{H}_2\text{PtCl}_6}_{\text{Chloroplatinic acid}} + 4\text{NO}\uparrow + 8\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Standard Reduction Potential ($E^\circ$) Quantitative Rule

In physical electrochemistry, the Reactivity Series is simply the Electrochemical Series sorted by increasing Standard Reduction Potential $E^\circ(\text{M}^{n+}/\text{M})$ relative to the Standard Hydrogen Electrode ($E^\circ_{2\text{H}^+/\text{H}_2} = 0.00\text{ V}$):

  • More negative $E^\circ$ $\implies$ Ion resists reduction $\implies$ Metal oxidizes effortlessly (stronger reducing agent, higher reactivity):
    $\text{Li}^+/\text{Li} = -3.05\text{ V}$, $\text{K}^+/\text{K} = -2.93\text{ V}$, $\text{Na}^+/\text{Na} = -2.71\text{ V}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}/\text{Mg} = -2.37\text{ V}$, $\text{Al}^{3+}/\text{Al} = -1.66\text{ V}$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn} = -0.76\text{ V}$, $\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe} = -0.44\text{ V}$, $\text{Sn}^{2+}/\text{Sn} = -0.14\text{ V}$, $\text{Pb}^{2+}/\text{Pb} = -0.13\text{ V}$.
  • More positive $E^\circ$ $\implies$ Ion reduces eagerly $\implies$ Noble metal resists oxidation:
    $\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu} = +0.34\text{ V}$, $\text{Hg}^{2+}/\text{Hg} = +0.85\text{ V}$, $\text{Ag}^+/\text{Ag} = +0.80\text{ V}$, $\text{Au}^{3+}/\text{Au} = +1.50\text{ V}$.

Spontaneity Criterion: A displacement reaction $\text{A}(s) + \text{B}^{n+}(aq) \to \text{A}^{n+}(aq) + \text{B}(s)$ is thermodynamically spontaneous if and only if:

$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode }(\text{B}^{n+}/\text{B})} - E^\circ_{\text{anode }(\text{A}^{n+}/\text{A})} \gt 0 \iff E^\circ_{\text{A}} \lt E^\circ_{\text{B}}$$

Olympiad Nuance: Why is $\text{Li}$ ($-3.05\text{ V}$) a stronger reducing agent in water than $\text{K}$ ($-2.93\text{ V}$) despite having a higher ionization energy? Because the tiny $\text{Li}^+$ ion releases an enormous hydration enthalpy in water, more than compensating for its ionization energy!

25.4 Ionic (Electrovalent) Compounds: Bonding & Lattice Physics

When a metal atom (low ionization enthalpy) meets a non-metal atom (high electron affinity), valence electrons transfer completely so both species achieve stable noble-gas octets (or duplet for $\text{Li}^+$). The resulting oppositely charged ions pack into a repeating three-dimensional crystal lattice bound by powerful, non-directional Coulombic electrostatic forces.

Electron-Transfer Schemes (Mandatory NCERT & Foundation Models)
  • Sodium Chloride ($\text{NaCl}$): $$\text{Na }(2,8,1) \to \text{Na}^+\,(2,8) + e^-, \qquad \text{Cl }(2,8,7) + e^- \to \text{Cl}^-\,(2,8,8)$$ $$\text{Na}\cdot + :\ddot{\text{Cl}}\cdot \longrightarrow [\text{Na}]^+[\,:\ddot{\text{Cl}}:\,]^-$$
  • Magnesium Chloride ($\text{MgCl}_2$): One $\text{Mg}$ atom donates 2 electrons — one to each of two $\text{Cl}$ atoms: $$\text{Mg }(2,8,2) \to \text{Mg}^{2+}\,(2,8) + 2e^-, \qquad 2\text{Cl }(2,8,7) + 2e^- \to 2\text{Cl}^-\,(2,8,8)$$ $$:\text{Mg} + 2\,:\ddot{\text{Cl}}\cdot \longrightarrow [\text{Mg}]^{2+}\,2[\,:\ddot{\text{Cl}}:\,]^-$$
  • Aluminium Oxide ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$): Two $\text{Al}$ atoms ($2,8,3$) lose $6e^-$ total; three $\text{O}$ atoms ($2,6$) gain $6e^-$ total: $$2\text{Al} \to 2\text{Al}^{3+} + 6e^-, \qquad 3\text{O} + 6e^- \to 3\text{O}^{2-} \implies 2[\text{Al}]^{3+}\,3[\,:\ddot{\text{O}}:\,]^{2-}$$

Four Signature Properties Explained from First Principles

  1. Crystalline Solids with High MP & BP: Breaking an ionic crystal requires overcoming strong omnidirectional electrostatic attractions across the entire 3D lattice. By Coulomb's law, the Lattice Energy ($U$) scales as: $$U \propto \frac{|q_+ \cdot q_-|}{r_+ + r_-}$$ Higher ionic charges ($q_+, q_-$) and smaller interionic distance ($r_+ + r_-$) dramatically raise melting point: $\text{MgO}$ ($+2/-2$, $\text{MP} = 2852^\circ\text{C}$) $\gg$ $\text{NaCl}$ ($+1/-1$, $\text{MP} = 801^\circ\text{C}$).
  2. Hard Yet Brittle (Shear Repulsion): Ionic crystals resist scratching, but a sharp hammer blow shifts one lattice plane by a single ion spacing. Instantly, cations align directly opposite cations ($+$ facing $+$) and anions face anions ($-$ facing $-$). Massive electrostatic repulsion cleaves the crystal cleanly along that plane.
  3. Polar Solubility ("Like Dissolves Like"): Ionic solids dissolve in polar solvents like water (high dielectric constant $\varepsilon \approx 80$), which weakens interionic attraction by a factor of $80$ and stabilizes separated ions via exothermic hydration ($\Delta H_{\text{hydration}} \gt U$). Non-polar organic solvents like kerosene and petrol ($\varepsilon \approx 2$) cannot solvate ions, leaving ionic salts completely insoluble.
  4. State-Dependent Electrical Conductivity:
    • Solid state: Non-conductor — ions are rigidly locked at lattice nodes and cannot migrate.
    • Molten (fused) or aqueous state: Strong conductor — thermal energy or hydration frees cations and anions to drift toward opposite electrodes under a potential difference.

25.5 Metallurgy: From Raw Ore to 99.99% Pure Metal

A mineral is any naturally occurring element or compound in the Earth's crust. An ore is a mineral from which a metal can be extracted conveniently and profitably. Thus, all ores are minerals, but not all minerals are ores (e.g., clay $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\cdot 2\text{SiO}_2\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is an aluminium mineral, whereas bauxite $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is its chief ore). Unwanted earthy impurities (sand, clay, rocky silica) mixed with mined ore are called gangue (or matrix).

Step 1: Concentration (Enrichment) of Ore

  • Hydraulic Washing (Gravity Separation): Used for heavy oxide ores ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$, $\text{SnO}_2$). A running stream of water washes away lighter silica gangue while dense ore particles settle.
  • Magnetic Separation: Used when either the ore (magnetite $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$, chromite $\text{FeCr}_2\text{O}_4$) or the gangue (magnetic wolframite $\text{FeWO}_4$ inside non-magnetic tin ore $\text{SnO}_2$) is attracted by a magnetic roller.
  • Froth Floatation Process (Sulfide Ores Only — $\text{ZnS}, \text{PbS}, \text{Cu}_2\text{S}, \text{CuFeS}_2$): Based on differential surface wetting. Crushed sulfide ore is agitated in water mixed with a frother (pine oil / eucalyptus oil) and a collector (sodium ethyl xanthate). Sulfide particles become hydrophobic (oil-wetted), attach to rising air bubbles, and float into a skimmed froth; hydrophilic gangue gets water-wetted and sinks.
    Olympiad Bridge (Depressants): To separate a mixture of $\text{ZnS}$ and $\text{PbS}$, adding $\text{NaCN}$ acts as a selective depressant: it reacts with $\text{ZnS}$ to form a water-soluble complex $\text{Na}_2[\text{Zn(CN)}_4]$, stopping $\text{ZnS}$ from frothing while pure $\text{PbS}$ rises into the froth!
  • Chemical Leaching (Bayer's Process for Bauxite): Exploits the amphoteric nature of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$. Red bauxite (contaminated with basic $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ and $\text{TiO}_2$) is digested in hot conc. $\text{NaOH}$: $$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \xrightarrow{473\text{--}523\text{ K}} 2\text{NaAlO}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$ Insoluble $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ is filtered off. Passing $\text{CO}_2$ precipitates pure $\text{Al(OH)}_3$, which is calcined at $1470\text{ K}$ to yield pure white alumina ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$).

Step 3: Reduction by Reactivity Tier

  1. Low Reactivity Tier ($\text{Hg}, \text{Cu}$) — Thermal & Auto-Reduction:
    • Mercury from Cinnabar ($\text{HgS}$): Simply heating in air oxidizes $\text{HgS}$ to $\text{HgO}$, which thermally decomposes at $\approx 300^\circ\text{C}$ with no external reducing agent: $$2\text{HgS}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{HgO}(s) + 2\text{SO}_2(g), \qquad 2\text{HgO}(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Hg}(l) + \text{O}_2(g)$$
    • Copper from Copper Glance ($\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$): Partial roasting converts two-thirds of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$ to $\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$; shutting off air allows the remaining sulfide to auto-reduce the oxide: $$2\text{Cu}_2\text{S}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Cu}_2\text{O}(s) + 2\text{SO}_2(g)$$ $$2\text{Cu}_2\text{O}(s) + \text{Cu}_2\text{S}(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 6\text{Cu}(s) + \text{SO}_2(g)$$ Escaping $\text{SO}_2$ bubbles leave surface blisters on solidifying copper, earning it the name blister copper ($\approx 98\%$ pure).
  2. Medium Reactivity Tier ($\text{Zn}, \text{Fe}, \text{Pb}, \text{Mn}$) — Smelting & Thermite:
    • Carbon / $\text{CO}$ Smelting: Coke reduces oxides at high furnace temperatures: $$\text{ZnO}(s) + \text{C}(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Zn}(s) + \text{CO}(g), \qquad \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 3\text{CO}(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Fe}(l) + 3\text{CO}_2(g)$$ In the blast furnace, limestone ($\text{CaCO}_3 \to \text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2$) supplies basic flux ($\text{CaO}$) that binds acidic silica gangue ($\text{SiO}_2$) into fusible slag ($\text{CaSiO}_3$): $\text{CaO} + \text{SiO}_2 \to \text{CaSiO}_3(l)$.
    • Thermite Reaction (Aluminothermy): Reactive metals like $\text{Al}$ displace medium-reactivity metals from their oxides with massive exothermicity ($\Delta H \approx -850\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$), producing molten iron used to weld cracked railway tracks and heavy machinery: $$\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{Al}(s) \longrightarrow 2\text{Fe}(l) + \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + \text{Heat}$$ $$3\text{MnO}_2(s) + 4\text{Al}(s) \longrightarrow 3\text{Mn}(l) + 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + \text{Heat}$$
  3. High Reactivity Tier ($\text{K}, \text{Na}, \text{Ca}, \text{Mg}, \text{Al}$) — Electrolytic Reduction of Molten Salts:
    • Why Carbon Fails: $\text{Na}, \text{Mg}, \text{Al}$ have far higher thermodynamic affinity for oxygen than carbon does (their $\Delta G^\circ_f$ curves lie far below $\text{CO}/\text{CO}_2$ on an Ellingham diagram).
    • Why Molten, NEVER Aqueous: Electrolyzing aqueous $\text{NaCl}$ reduces $\text{H}^+$ ($E^\circ = 0.00\text{ V}$) at the cathode instead of $\text{Na}^+$ ($E^\circ = -2.71\text{ V}$), yielding $\text{H}_2$ gas and $\text{NaOH}$ instead of metallic $\text{Na}$! Using molten $\text{NaCl}$ (Downs process) eliminates water: $$\text{Cathode } (-): \quad \text{Na}^+ + e^- \to \text{Na}(l) \qquad\qquad \text{Anode } (+): \quad 2\text{Cl}^- \to \text{Cl}_2(g) + 2e^-$$
    • Hall–Héroult Process for Aluminium: Pure $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ melts at $2050^\circ\text{C}$ and conducts electricity poorly. Dissolving alumina in molten Cryolite ($\text{Na}_3\text{AlF}_6$) and Fluorspar ($\text{CaF}_2$) serves two critical exam functions: (1) lowers the melting point to $\approx 900\text{--}950^\circ\text{C}$, and (2) sharply increases electrical conductivity. Molten $\text{Al}$ collects at the steel cathode lining while $\text{O}_2$ liberated at the graphite anodes gradually burns them into $\text{CO}/\text{CO}_2$, requiring periodic anode replacement.

Step 4: Electrolytic Refining (Copper, Zinc, Tin, Nickel, Silver, Gold)

Crude metals obtained by reduction still contain $1\text{--}5\%$ metallic impurities. In Electrolytic Refining:

  • Anode ($+$): Thick slab of impure metal (oxidizes and shrinks: $\text{Cu}(s) \to \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-$).
  • Cathode ($-$): Thin strip of pure metal (gains mass as pure metal plates out: $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \to \text{Cu}(s)$).
  • Electrolyte: Acidified aqueous salt solution of the metal (e.g., $\text{CuSO}_4(aq) + \text{dil. H}_2\text{SO}_4$).
  • Fate of Impurities (Major Olympiad Trap!):
    • More reactive impurities ($\text{Zn}, \text{Fe}$): Oxidize into solution ($\text{Zn} \to \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2e^-$) and remain dissolved in the electrolyte because their reduction potentials are far more negative than $\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}$.
    • Less reactive / noble impurities ($\text{Ag}, \text{Au}, \text{Pt}$): Cannot oxidize at copper's anode potential; as surrounding copper dissolves away, they fall straight to the bottom directly beneath the anode as Anode Mud.

25.6 Corrosion, Sacrificial Galvanization & Alloys

Corrosion is the slow electrochemical destruction of a metal surface by atmospheric gases and moisture.

  • Rusting of Iron: Requires both $\text{O}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ simultaneously (verified by the classic three-test-tube experiment: anhydrous $\text{CaCl}_2$ removes moisture → no rust; boiled distilled water sealed with oil removes dissolved $\text{O}_2$ → no rust; open moist air → heavy rust). Rust is flaky, porous hydrated iron(III) oxide: $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Dissolved salts ($\text{NaCl}$) and acidic gases ($\text{CO}_2, \text{SO}_2$) accelerate rusting by increasing water's ionic conductivity.
  • Green Patina on Copper: Copper reacts slowly with moist atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ to form green basic copper carbonate: $$2\text{Cu}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \underbrace{\text{CuCO}_3\cdot\text{Cu(OH)}_2(s)}_{\text{Green basic copper carbonate}}$$
  • Black Tarnishing of Silver: Silver does not react with oxygen, but reacts with trace atmospheric hydrogen sulfide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$) to form black silver sulfide: $$4\text{Ag}(s) + 2\text{H}_2\text{S}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \underbrace{2\text{Ag}_2\text{S}(s)}_{\text{Black}} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

Corrosion Prevention: Why Galvanization Beats Tin-Plating

Simple barrier methods (painting, oiling, greasing, chrome-plating) work only while intact. Anodizing protects aluminium by electrolyzing dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ with a clean aluminium article as the anode; oxygen gas evolved at the anode thickens the natural $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ layer into a tough, corrosion-proof coat that readily absorbs dyes.

Galvanization (coating steel/iron with a thin layer of Zinc) is uniquely superior because it provides cathodic (sacrificial) protection even after the coating is deeply scratched! Because $\text{Zn}$ lies above $\text{Fe}$ in the reactivity series ($E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.76\text{ V} \lt E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}} = -0.44\text{ V}$), zinc preferentially oxidizes as the sacrificial anode and pumps electrons into the exposed iron, forcing iron to act as an uncorroded cathode. Conversely, Tin ($\text{Sn}$, $E^\circ = -0.14\text{ V}$) sits below iron — if a tin-plated can is scratched, iron becomes the anode and rusts even faster than bare iron!

Worked Example 1 — Amphoteric Oxide & Thermite Identification Level 1 Conceptual
An element $X$ forms an oxide $X_2\text{O}_3$ which reacts with both dilute $\text{HCl}$ and aqueous $\text{NaOH}$ to yield salt and water. Furthermore, powdered $X$ reduces $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ in a highly exothermic reaction used to weld fractured railway tracks. Identify $X$, write balanced chemical equations for the reaction of $X_2\text{O}_3$ with $\text{HCl}$ and $\text{NaOH}$, and write the welding equation.

Step 1 (Identify $X$): The formula $X_2\text{O}_3$ indicates a $+3$ valency. An amphoteric oxide of formula $X_2\text{O}_3$ whose parent metal reduces $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ exothermically to join railway tracks is unmistakably Aluminium ($\text{Al}$), and its oxide is $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$.

Step 2 (Amphoteric Reactions of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$):

  • Acting as a base against acid ($\text{HCl}$): $$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 6\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3(aq) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
  • Acting as an acid against alkali ($\text{NaOH}$): $$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \longrightarrow \underbrace{2\text{NaAlO}_2(aq)}_{\text{Sodium aluminate}} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

Step 3 (Industrial Welding Reaction — Thermite Process):

$$\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{Al}(s) \longrightarrow 2\text{Fe}(l) + \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s) + \text{Heat}$$

The heat liberated exceeds the melting point of iron ($1538^\circ\text{C}$), pouring molten iron directly into the rail crack.

Worked Example 2 — Reactivity Deduction from a Displacement Matrix Level 1 Logical Deduction
Four metals $P, Q, R,$ and $S$ are tested separately in aqueous solutions of $\text{FeSO}_4$, $\text{CuSO}_4$, $\text{ZnSO}_4$, and $\text{AgNO}_3$:
  • $P$ displaces $\text{Cu}$ from $\text{CuSO}_4$, but shows no reaction with $\text{FeSO}_4$.
  • $Q$ displaces $\text{Fe}$ from $\text{FeSO}_4$, but shows no reaction with $\text{ZnSO}_4$.
  • $R$ displaces $\text{Ag}$ from $\text{AgNO}_3$, but fails to displace $\text{Cu}, \text{Fe},$ or $\text{Zn}$.
  • $S$ shows no reaction with any of the four salt solutions.
Arrange $P, Q, R, S$ in decreasing order of reactivity and predict what is observed when a strip of $Q$ is dipped in aqueous $\text{CuSO}_4$.

Step 1 (Anchor the Reference Salts): From the Reactivity Series, the four reference metals strictly satisfy $\text{Zn} \gt \text{Fe} \gt \text{Cu} \gt \text{Ag}$.

Step 2 (Bracket Each Unknown Metal):

  • $Q$ displaces $\text{Fe}$ but not $\text{Zn} \implies \text{Fe} \lt Q \le \text{Zn}$.
  • $P$ displaces $\text{Cu}$ but not $\text{Fe} \implies \text{Cu} \lt P \le \text{Fe}$.
  • $R$ displaces $\text{Ag}$ but not $\text{Cu} \implies \text{Ag} \lt R \le \text{Cu}$.
  • $S$ cannot displace even $\text{Ag} \implies S \le \text{Ag}$.

Step 3 (Final Order): Chaining the strict inequalities gives:

$$Q \gt P \gt R \gt S$$

Step 4 (Dipping $Q$ into $\text{CuSO}_4$): Since $Q \gt \text{Fe} \gt \text{Cu}$, metal $Q$ vigorously displaces copper from $\text{CuSO}_4(aq)$. The characteristic blue color fades (turning colorless if $Q = \text{Zn}$) and a reddish-brown deposit of copper coats the strip of $Q$.

Worked Example 3 — Calcination vs. Roasting of Zinc Ores Level 1 Metallurgy
Two zinc ores, Ore $A$ and Ore $B$, undergo preliminary testing. Ore $A$ produces brisk effervescence with dilute $\text{HCl}$ liberating a colorless gas that turns lime water milky. Ore $B$, when heated strongly in excess air, evolves a pungent gas smelling of burning sulfur. Identify Ores $A$ and $B$, name the thermal conversion step required for each, and write all balanced equations leading to metallic zinc.

Step 1 (Ore Identification):

  • Gas turning lime water milky is $\text{CO}_2$, confirming a carbonate ore → Ore $A$ is Calamine ($\text{ZnCO}_3$).
  • Gas smelling of burning sulfur is $\text{SO}_2$, confirming a sulfide ore → Ore $B$ is Zinc Blende ($\text{ZnS}$).

Step 2 (Conversion to Zinc Oxide):

  • For Calamine ($\text{ZnCO}_3$) — Calcination (strong heating in limited/absent air): $$\text{ZnCO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{ZnO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)\uparrow$$
  • For Zinc Blende ($\text{ZnS}$) — Roasting (strong heating in excess air): $$2\text{ZnS}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{ZnO}(s) + 2\text{SO}_2(g)\uparrow$$

Step 3 (Reduction to Metallic Zinc): Because $\text{Zn}$ is a medium-reactivity metal, $\text{ZnO}$ is reduced by heating with coke (carbon):

$$\text{ZnO}(s) + \text{C}(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Zn}(s) + \text{CO}(g)\uparrow$$

Worked Example 4 — Multi-Clue Identification ($\text{HNO}_3$, Steam & Aqua Regia) Level 2 Multi-Concept Trap
Three metals $M_1, M_2,$ and $M_3$ exhibit the following behaviors:
  1. $M_1$ ignores cold water, reacts with hot water while floating on its surface, and liberates a gas burning with a pop sound when treated with very dilute ($1\%$) $\text{HNO}_3$.
  2. $M_2$ reacts only with steam in a reversible reaction to yield a magnetic black oxide $X$, and becomes completely passive in concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$.
  3. $M_3$ is a yellow noble metal used in 22-carat jewellery that dissolves in a $3:1$ volume mixture of conc. $\text{HCl}$ and conc. $\text{HNO}_3$, and is recovered from the anode mud during copper refining.
Identify $M_1, M_2, M_3$, and oxide $X$, and write balanced equations for (i) $M_1$ with very dilute $\text{HNO}_3$, and (ii) $M_2$ with steam.

Step 1 (Decode $M_1$): Only two metals evolve $\text{H}_2$ (pop sound) with very dilute $\text{HNO}_3$: $\text{Mg}$ and $\text{Mn}$. Of these, Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) uniquely reacts with hot water (not cold water) and floats due to adhering $\text{H}_2$ bubbles. Hence $M_1 = \text{Mg}$.

$$\text{Mg}(s) + 2\text{HNO}_3(\text{very dil.}) \longrightarrow \text{Mg(NO}_3)_2(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow$$

Step 2 (Decode $M_2$ and $X$): Among steam-only metals ($\text{Al}, \text{Zn}, \text{Fe}$), Iron ($\text{Fe}$) undergoes a reversible reaction with steam to form magnetic black ferrosoferric oxide ($\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$) and is rendered passive by conc. $\text{HNO}_3$. Hence $M_2 = \text{Fe}$ and $X = \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$.

$$3\text{Fe}(s) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4(s) + 4\text{H}_2(g)$$

Step 3 (Decode $M_3$): The $3:1$ mixture of conc. $\text{HCl}$ and conc. $\text{HNO}_3$ is Aqua Regia. The yellow noble metal used in 22-carat jewellery and recovered from copper anode mud is Gold ($\text{Au}$). Hence $M_3 = \text{Au}$.

Worked Example 5 — Limiting Reagent Stoichiometry in Thermite Welding Level 2 Quantitative / NAT
During a railway repair, $320\text{ g}$ of pure haematite ($\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) is ignited with $135\text{ g}$ of aluminium powder ($\text{Al}$). Assuming $100\%$ reaction completion, determine: (a) the limiting reagent, (b) the exact mass of molten iron ($\text{Fe}$) produced in grams, and (c) the mass of excess reagent remaining unreacted. (Atomic masses: $\text{Fe} = 56$, $\text{O} = 16$, $\text{Al} = 27$).

Step 1 (Balanced Thermite Equation):

$$\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3(s) + 2\text{Al}(s) \longrightarrow 2\text{Fe}(l) + \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3(s)$$

Step 2 (Convert Masses to Moles):

$$M(\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3) = 2(56) + 3(16) = 112 + 48 = 160\text{ g mol}^{-1}$$ $$n(\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3) = \frac{320}{160} = 2.0\text{ mol}, \qquad n(\text{Al}) = \frac{135}{27} = 5.0\text{ mol}$$

Step 3 (Identify Limiting Reagent via Mole-to-Coefficient Ratio):

$$\frac{n(\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3)}{1} = 2.0 \quad \lt \quad \frac{n(\text{Al})}{2} = 2.5$$

Since $2.0 \lt 2.5$, $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ is the limiting reagent.

Step 4 (Calculate Molten Iron Produced & Excess Aluminium):

  • From stoichiometry, $1\text{ mol }\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ yields $2\text{ mol Fe}$. Thus $2.0\text{ mol }\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ yields $4.0\text{ mol Fe}$: $$m(\text{Fe}) = 4.0 \times 56 = \mathbf{224\text{ g}}$$
  • $\text{Al}$ consumed $= 2 \times 2.0 = 4.0\text{ mol}$. Unreacted $\text{Al} = 5.0 - 4.0 = 1.0\text{ mol}$: $$m(\text{Al excess}) = 1.0 \times 27 = \mathbf{27\text{ g}}$$

Conservation Check: Total reactants $= 320 + 135 = 455\text{ g}$. Products + Excess $= 224\text{ g Fe} + 2(102)\text{ g Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 27\text{ g Al} = 224 + 204 + 27 = 455\text{ g}$.

Worked Example 6 — Lattice Energy Comparison & Scratched Can Forensics Level 2 Analytical / Electrochemistry
(a) Using lattice electrostatics, explain why $\text{MgO}$ ($\text{MP} = 2852^\circ\text{C}$) melts at over triple the temperature of $\text{NaCl}$ ($\text{MP} = 801^\circ\text{C}$), and why both are electrical insulators as solids yet shatter under shear stress.
(b) Two identical steel food cans — Can X coated with Zinc and Can Y coated with Tin — suffer a deep scratch exposing iron to moist air. Predict which can corrodes faster at the scratch using $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.76\text{ V}$, $E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}} = -0.44\text{ V}$, and $E^\circ_{\text{Sn}^{2+}/\text{Sn}} = -0.14\text{ V}$.

Part (a) — Lattice Electrostatics & Mechanical Behavior:

  • Lattice energy obeys $U \propto \dfrac{|q_+ q_-|}{r_+ + r_-}$. In $\text{MgO}$, the charge product is $|(+2)(-2)| = 4$, compared to $|(+1)(-1)| = 1$ in $\text{NaCl}$. Moreover, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ have smaller ionic radii than $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$. Together, these factors make the $\text{MgO}$ lattice roughly four times stronger, demanding far greater thermal energy to melt.
  • In the solid state, ions are locked in rigid lattice positions (zero mobile charge carriers $\implies$ insulator). Under hammer shear, crystal layers slip by one ion diameter, aligning like charges ($+$ facing $+$, $-$ facing $-$); intense electrostatic repulsion immediately cleaves the crystal (brittleness).

Part (b) — Galvanic Corrosion at the Scratch:

  • Can X ($\text{Zn}$-coated): Since $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}} (-0.76\text{ V}) \lt E^\circ_{\text{Fe}} (-0.44\text{ V})$, zinc has the higher oxidation tendency and serves as the sacrificial anode ($\text{Zn} \to \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2e^-$). Electrons flow into the exposed iron, making iron the cathode where oxygen reduces to $\text{OH}^-$. Iron stays uncorroded.
  • Can Y ($\text{Sn}$-coated): Since $E^\circ_{\text{Fe}} (-0.44\text{ V}) \lt E^\circ_{\text{Sn}} (-0.14\text{ V})$, exposed iron becomes the anode ($\text{Fe} \to \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^-$) while the large tin surface acts as an efficient cathode, accelerating rusting at the scratch.

Verdict: Can Y (tin-plated) corrodes far faster at the scratch.

Worked Example 7 — Displacement Strip Mass-Change Stoichiometry Level 3 Olympiad NAT
A clean zinc strip weighing $50.0\text{ g}$ is immersed in $200\text{ mL}$ of an aqueous $\text{AgNO}_3$ solution. When the reaction stops completely, the strip (with all deposited silver adhering firmly) is dried and re-weighed; its new mass is $65.1\text{ g}$. Calculate (a) the mass of silver deposited (in grams) and (b) the initial molarity of the $\text{AgNO}_3$ solution (in $\text{mol L}^{-1}$). (Atomic masses: $\text{Zn} = 65$, $\text{Ag} = 108$).

First-Principles Derivation:

Since $\text{Zn}$ lies above $\text{Ag}$ in the reactivity series, zinc dissolves into solution while silver plates onto the strip:

$$\text{Zn}(s) + 2\text{AgNO}_3(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Zn(NO}_3)_2(aq) + 2\text{Ag}(s)$$

Let $x$ moles of $\text{Zn}$ dissolve. By stoichiometry, $2x$ moles of $\text{AgNO}_3$ react and $2x$ moles of $\text{Ag}$ deposit on the strip.

  • Mass lost by strip (dissolved $\text{Zn}$) $= 65x\text{ g}$.
  • Mass gained by strip (deposited $\text{Ag}$) $= 2x \times 108 = 216x\text{ g}$.
  • Net mass increase of strip: $$\Delta m = m_{\text{final}} - m_{\text{initial}} = 216x - 65x = 151x\text{ g}$$

Equating to the observed mass increase $\Delta m = 65.1 - 50.0 = 15.1\text{ g}$:

$$151x = 15.1 \implies x = 0.1\text{ mol of Zn dissolved}$$

Limiting-reagent check: Initial zinc was $\frac{50.0}{65} \approx 0.77\text{ mol} \gg 0.1\text{ mol}$, confirming $\text{AgNO}_3$ was completely consumed.

  • (a) Mass of $\text{Ag}$ deposited: $n(\text{Ag}) = 2x = 0.2\text{ mol} \implies m(\text{Ag}) = 0.2 \times 108 = \mathbf{21.6\text{ g}}$.
  • (b) Initial molarity of $\text{AgNO}_3$: $n(\text{AgNO}_3) = 2x = 0.2\text{ mol}$ in $V = 200\text{ mL} = 0.2\text{ L}$: $$M = \frac{0.2\text{ mol}}{0.2\text{ L}} = \mathbf{1.0\text{ mol L}^{-1}\text{ (1.0 M)}}$$
30-Second Exam Shortcut (Direct Mass-Change Quotient): For any displacement $a\,\text{M}_{\text{diss}} + b\,\text{M}_{\text{dep}}^{n+} \to \dots$, jump straight to the reaction extent $x$ in one line: $$x = \frac{\Delta m_{\text{strip}}}{b M_{\text{dep}} - a M_{\text{diss}}} = \frac{65.1 - 50.0}{2(108) - 1(65)} = \frac{15.1}{151} = 0.1\text{ mol}$$ Instantly, $n(\text{Ag}) = 2x = 0.2\text{ mol} \implies 21.6\text{ g}$ and $M = 0.2/0.2 = 1.0\text{ M}$!
Worked Example 8 — Quantitative Alloy Composition via Selective Acid Dissolution Level 3 Olympiad Quantitative
A $10.0\text{ g}$ sample of a ternary $\text{Mg}\text{--}\text{Al}\text{--}\text{Cu}$ alloy is treated with excess dilute $\text{HCl}$. After effervescence stops completely, an insoluble reddish-brown residue weighing $2.2\text{ g}$ remains, and $8.96\text{ L}$ of $\text{H}_2$ gas (measured at STP, where $1\text{ mol gas} = 22.4\text{ L}$) is collected. Calculate the mass percentage of $\text{Al}$, $\text{Mg}$, and $\text{Cu}$ in the alloy. (Atomic masses: $\text{Mg} = 24$, $\text{Al} = 27$, $\text{Cu} = 63.5$).

First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 (Isolate Copper via Reactivity Series): Copper lies below $[\text{H}]$ and does not react with dilute $\text{HCl}$. Therefore, the entire reddish-brown insoluble residue is pure copper:

$$m(\text{Cu}) = 2.2\text{ g} \implies \%\text{Cu} = \frac{2.2}{10.0} \times 100 = \mathbf{22\%}$$

Consequently, the combined mass of the active metals is $m(\text{Al}) + m(\text{Mg}) = 10.0 - 2.2 = 7.8\text{ g}$.

Step 2 (Set Up $\text{H}_2$ Stoichiometry):

$$2\text{Al} + 6\text{HCl} \to 2\text{AlCl}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\uparrow \qquad\qquad \text{Mg} + 2\text{HCl} \to \text{MgCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\uparrow$$

Total moles of $\text{H}_2$ evolved $= \dfrac{8.96}{22.4} = 0.40\text{ mol}$. Let $m(\text{Al}) = x\text{ g}$, so $m(\text{Mg}) = (7.8 - x)\text{ g}$.

  • Moles of $\text{H}_2$ from $\text{Al} = \dfrac{3}{2} \cdot \dfrac{x}{27} = \dfrac{x}{18}\text{ mol}$.
  • Moles of $\text{H}_2$ from $\text{Mg} = 1 \cdot \dfrac{7.8 - x}{24} = \dfrac{7.8 - x}{24}\text{ mol}$.

Step 3 (Solve the Linear System):

$$\frac{x}{18} + \frac{7.8 - x}{24} = 0.40$$

Multiply through by $\text{LCM}(18, 24) = 72$:

$$4x + 3(7.8 - x) = 0.40 \times 72 = 28.8$$ $$4x + 23.4 - 3x = 28.8 \implies x = 5.4\text{ g}$$

Therefore:

  • $m(\text{Al}) = 5.4\text{ g} \implies \%\text{Al} = \mathbf{54\%}$
  • $m(\text{Mg}) = 7.8 - 5.4 = 2.4\text{ g} \implies \%\text{Mg} = \mathbf{24\%}$
  • $m(\text{Cu}) = 2.2\text{ g} \implies \%\text{Cu} = \mathbf{22\%}$

Bonus Verification: If an identical $10.0\text{ g}$ sample were instead heated with excess $\text{NaOH}(aq)$, ONLY amphoteric $\text{Al}$ ($5.4\text{ g} = 0.2\text{ mol}$) would dissolve, leaving $\text{Mg} + \text{Cu} = 4.6\text{ g}$ undissolved!

30-Second Exam Shortcut ("All-One-Metal" Swap Method): Note the $\text{H}_2$ yield per gram: $\text{Al}$ gives $\frac{1}{18}\text{ mol g}^{-1}$; $\text{Mg}$ gives $\frac{1}{24}\text{ mol g}^{-1}$.
1. Assume all $7.8\text{ g}$ is $\text{Mg}$: $\text{H}_2 = \frac{7.8}{24} = 0.325\text{ mol}$.
2. Excess $\text{H}_2$ actually observed $= 0.400 - 0.325 = 0.075\text{ mol}$.
3. Swapping $1\text{ g Mg} \to 1\text{ g Al}$ adds $\left(\frac{1}{18} - \frac{1}{24}\right) = \frac{1}{72}\text{ mol H}_2$.
4. Hence $m(\text{Al}) = 0.075 \times 72 = \mathbf{5.4\text{ g}}$ ($54\%$) in four seconds flat!
Worked Example 9 — Copper Glance Auto-Reduction & Anode Mud Forensics Level 3 Metallurgy + Refining
(a) A $200\text{ g}$ ore sample contains $79.5\%$ pure copper glance ($\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$) by mass, with the rest being inert silica gangue. Assuming $100\%$ recovery during froth floatation followed by partial roasting and complete auto-reduction, calculate the maximum theoretical mass of copper obtained and the volume of $\text{SO}_2$ released at STP ($1\text{ mol} = 22.4\text{ L}$). (Atomic masses: $\text{Cu} = 63.5$, $\text{S} = 32$, $\text{O} = 16$).
(b) In an industrial electrolytic refining tank using acidified $\text{CuSO}_4(aq)$, a $250\text{ g}$ impure copper anode containing $96.0\%$ $\text{Cu}$, $2.4\%$ $\text{Zn}$, $0.8\%$ $\text{Ag}$, and $0.8\%$ $\text{Au}$ is electrolyzed until it dissolves completely. Find the exact mass of pure copper deposited at the cathode and the exact mass of anode mud collected.

Part (a) — Full Multi-Step Derivation:

Mass of pure $\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$ in $200\text{ g}$ ore $= 200 \times 0.795 = 159\text{ g}$.
Molar mass of $\text{Cu}_2\text{S} = 2(63.5) + 32 = 159\text{ g mol}^{-1} \implies n(\text{Cu}_2\text{S}) = \dfrac{159}{159} = 1.0\text{ mol}$.

Stepwise equations for partial roasting and auto-reduction:

$$2\text{Cu}_2\text{S} + 3\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Cu}_2\text{O} + 2\text{SO}_2 \qquad \text{(Two-thirds of }\text{Cu}_2\text{S roasted)}$$ $$2\text{Cu}_2\text{O} + \text{Cu}_2\text{S} \xrightarrow{\Delta} 6\text{Cu} + \text{SO}_2 \qquad \text{(Auto-reduction with remaining one-third)}$$

Adding both equations cancels the intermediate $2\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$ and gives $3\text{Cu}_2\text{S} + 3\text{O}_2 \to 6\text{Cu} + 3\text{SO}_2$, which divides by $3$ to:

$$\text{Cu}_2\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{Cu} + \text{SO}_2$$

Hence $1.0\text{ mol }\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$ yields $2.0\text{ mol Cu}$ ($= 2.0 \times 63.5 = \mathbf{127\text{ g Cu}}$) and $1.0\text{ mol SO}_2$ ($= \mathbf{22.4\text{ L at STP}}$).

Part (b) — Anode Mud Reactivity Filter:

  • Copper ($96.0\%$): Oxidizes at anode to $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ and deposits cleanly at the cathode: $$m(\text{pure Cu at cathode}) = 0.960 \times 250 = \mathbf{240.0\text{ g}}$$
  • Zinc ($2.4\% = 6.0\text{ g}$): Because $\text{Zn}$ is more reactive than $\text{Cu}$ ($E^\circ = -0.76\text{ V} \lt +0.34\text{ V}$), zinc oxidizes into $\text{Zn}^{2+}(aq)$ and stays dissolved in the electrolyte. It does not deposit at the cathode and does not enter the anode mud!
  • Silver ($0.8\% = 2.0\text{ g}$) & Gold ($0.8\% = 2.0\text{ g}$): Both lie below $\text{Cu}$ in the reactivity series, cannot oxidize at the anode, and settle directly beneath it as Anode Mud: $$m(\text{Anode Mud}) = 2.0 + 2.0 = \mathbf{4.0\text{ g}}$$
30-Second Exam Shortcut (POAC + Reactivity Cut): Never waste time balancing multi-step roasting/auto-reduction for yield questions — apply Principle of Atom Conservation (POAC) directly on $\text{Cu}$ and $\text{S}$: $$n(\text{Cu}) = 2 \times n(\text{Cu}_2\text{S}) = 2 \times \frac{159}{159} = 2\text{ mol} \implies \mathbf{127\text{ g}}; \qquad n(\text{SO}_2) = 1 \times n(\text{Cu}_2\text{S}) = 1\text{ mol} \implies \mathbf{22.4\text{ L}}$$ For Anode Mud, ignore every metal above $\text{Cu}$ ($\text{Zn}, \text{Fe}$) and sum ONLY the noble percentages below $\text{Cu}$: $(0.8\% + 0.8\%) \times 250 = 1.6\% \times 250 = \mathbf{4.0\text{ g}}$.
