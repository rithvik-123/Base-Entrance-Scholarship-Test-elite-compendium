1. Chemical Equations, Conservation Laws & Qualitative Diagnostics

First-Principles Foundation: What a Balanced Equation Guarantees

A chemical reaction rearranges atoms by breaking existing chemical bonds and forming new ones; no atoms are created or destroyed (Law of Conservation of Mass, Lavoisier). In ionic systems, total electric charge is simultaneously conserved (Law of Conservation of Charge). Therefore, a correctly balanced chemical equation encodes four exact quantitative ratios:

  1. Stoichiometric Mole Ratio: Coefficients give the exact ratio of moles (and molecules/formula units) consumed and formed.
  2. Mass Ratio: Total mass of reactants equals total mass of products ($\sum m_{\text{reactants}} = \sum m_{\text{products}}$).
  3. Gaseous Volume Ratio (Avogadro / Gay-Lussac Law): For gaseous species measured at identical temperature $T$ and pressure $P$, the volume ratio equals the stoichiometric mole ratio ($V \propto n$).
  4. Net Ionic Charge Balance: In any ionic equation, $\sum q_{\text{LHS}} = \sum q_{\text{RHS}}$.

The Algebraic Coefficient Method (Zero-Guesswork Balancing)

Hit-and-trial balancing wastes precious exam minutes on multi-product equations. For any skeletal equation, assign literal coefficients $a, b, c, d, \dots$, enforce atom conservation for every element, set one convenient variable to $1$, solve the linear system, and clear denominators to obtain the smallest coprime integers.

Protocol Demonstration: Red-Hot Iron with Steam

Consider $a\,\text{Fe}(s) + b\,\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \longrightarrow c\,\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4(s) + d\,\text{H}_2(g)$.

  • Iron ($\text{Fe}$) balance: $a = 3c$
  • Oxygen ($\text{O}$) balance: $b = 4c$
  • Hydrogen ($\text{H}$) balance: $2b = 2d \implies d = b = 4c$

Set $c = 1 \implies a = 3,\; b = 4,\; d = 4$. The unique minimal integer equation is:

$$3\text{Fe}(s) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \longrightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4(s) + 4\text{H}_2(g)$$

Verification: Left has $3\text{ Fe},\, 8\text{ H},\, 4\text{ O}$; Right has $3\text{ Fe},\, 8\text{ H},\, 4\text{ O}$. Notice that iron reacts only with steam ($\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$), not cold or hot liquid water, yielding magnetic iron oxide ($\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$), not $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$.

2. Systematic Classification of Chemical Reactions & Energetics

2.1 Combination & Decomposition Reactions

  • Combination ($A + B \longrightarrow AB$): Two or more reactants merge into a single product. Most combinations are exothermic (bond formation dominates). Examples: slaking of quicklime, $\text{CaO}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq) + \text{Heat}$ (non-redox); burning of magnesium ribbon, $2\text{Mg}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{MgO}(s)$ (redox). Note that whitewashing walls relies on atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ reacting slowly with slaked lime over 2–3 days to form shiny $\text{CaCO}_3(s)$.
  • Decomposition ($AB \longrightarrow A + B$): A single compound breaks into two or more simpler substances. Because bonds must be broken, decomposition reactions are endothermic and require an external energy driver:
    • Thermal Decomposition (Thermolysis):
      • Ferrous sulfate heptahydrate: Pale green $\text{FeSO}_4\cdot 7\text{H}_2\text{O}(s)$ first loses water of crystallization to become white anhydrous $\text{FeSO}_4(s)$, then decomposes on strong heating: $$2\text{FeSO}_4(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3(s)\text{ (reddish-brown)} + \text{SO}_2(g) + \text{SO}_3(g)$$ Notice $\text{Fe}$ goes $+2 \to +3$ while half the $\text{S}$ goes $+6 \to +4$; hence it is a redox thermal decomposition.
      • Lead(II) nitrate: White crystals decrepitate (crackle), giving yellow-when-cold (reddish-brown-when-hot) litharge $\text{PbO}$, brown $\text{NO}_2$ fumes, and $\text{O}_2$: $$2\text{Pb(NO}_3)_2(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{PbO}(s) + 4\text{NO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g)$$
      • Limestone (Calcium carbonate): $\text{CaCO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)$ (strictly non-redox; oxidation states remain $\text{Ca}=+2,\; \text{C}=+4,\; \text{O}=-2$).
    • Electrolytic Decomposition (Electrolysis): Acidulated water splits under DC current: $$2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \xrightarrow{\text{Electricity}} 2\text{H}_2(g)\text{ (cathode)} + \text{O}_2(g)\text{ (anode)}$$ At the same $T, P$, the volume ratio is $V_{\text{H}_2} : V_{\text{O}_2} = 2 : 1$, while the mass ratio is $m_{\text{H}_2} : m_{\text{O}_2} = (2 \times 2) : (1 \times 32) = 1 : 8$.
    • Photolytic Decomposition (Photolysis): Silver halides decompose in sunlight (basis of black-and-white photography): $$2\text{AgCl}(s)\text{ (white)} \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2\text{Ag}(s)\text{ (grey)} + \text{Cl}_2(g), \qquad 2\text{AgBr}(s)\text{ (pale yellow)} \xrightarrow{h\nu} 2\text{Ag}(s)\text{ (grey)} + \text{Br}_2(g)$$

2.2 Single Displacement & Reactivity Hierarchies

A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its aqueous salt or acid. Every single-displacement reaction is necessarily a redox reaction.

  • Metal Reactivity Series (Decreasing Ease of Oxidation / Electron Loss): $$\text{K} \gt \text{Na} \gt \text{Ca} \gt \text{Mg} \gt \text{Al} \gt \text{Zn} \gt \text{Fe} \gt \text{Pb} \gt [\text{H}] \gt \text{Cu} \gt \text{Hg} \gt \text{Ag} \gt \text{Au} \gt \text{Pt}$$ Example: $\text{Fe}(s) + \text{CuSO}_4(aq)\text{ (blue)} \longrightarrow \text{FeSO}_4(aq)\text{ (pale green)} + \text{Cu}(s)\text{ (reddish-brown deposit)}$. Conversely, $\text{Cu}(s) + \text{FeSO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow \text{No Reaction}$.
  • Halogen Reactivity Series (Decreasing Oxidizing Power / Electron Gain): $$\text{F}_2 \gt \text{Cl}_2 \gt \text{Br}_2 \gt \text{I}_2$$ Example: $\text{Cl}_2(aq) + 2\text{KBr}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{KCl}(aq) + \text{Br}_2(aq)$ (solution turns orange-brown), and $\text{Cl}_2(aq) + 2\text{KI}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{KCl}(aq) + \text{I}_2(aq)$ (brown solution; turns starch blue-black).

2.3 Double Displacement & Precipitate Color Reference

2.4 Endothermic vs. Exothermic Reactions & Reaction-Coordinate Profiles

The enthalpy change of a reaction is defined as $\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}} = \sum E_{\text{bonds broken}} - \sum E_{\text{bonds formed}}$.

  • Exothermic Reactions ($\Delta H \lt 0$): More energy is released forming product bonds than absorbed breaking reactant bonds; net heat flows to the surroundings (temperature rises). Key examples: combustion of fuels, cellular respiration ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{Energy}$), slaking of quicklime ($\text{CaO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$), composting of vegetable matter, acid-base neutralization, and dilution of conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.
  • Endothermic Reactions ($\Delta H \gt 0$): Net energy is absorbed from the surroundings (temperature drops). Key examples: photosynthesis, all thermal/electrolytic/photolytic decompositions, dissolution of $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ in water, and the classic cold-pack reaction between barium hydroxide octahydrate and ammonium chloride: $$\text{Ba(OH)}_2\cdot 8\text{H}_2\text{O}(s) + 2\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}(s) \longrightarrow \text{BaCl}_2(aq) + 2\text{NH}_3(g) + 10\text{H}_2\text{O}(l), \qquad \Delta H \gt 0$$ This reaction absorbs so much thermal energy that the beaker temperature plunges below $0^\circ\text{C}$.

3. Electronic Redox, Oxidation Number Rules & Structural Paradoxes

Classical vs. Modern Electronic Concept of Redox
  • Classical Definition: Oxidation = addition of $\text{O}$ (or electronegative element) or removal of $\text{H}$ (or electropositive element). Reduction = reverse.
  • Electronic Definition (OIL RIG): Oxidation Is Loss of electrons (oxidation number increases); Reduction Is Gain of electrons (oxidation number decreases).
  • Agents: The Oxidizing Agent (Oxidant) oxidizes the other species by accepting electrons and thus gets reduced itself. The Reducing Agent (Reductant) reduces the other species by donating electrons and thus gets oxidized itself.

Hierarchical Rules for Assigning Oxidation States (OS)

Oxidation state represents the hypothetical charge an atom would carry if all its heteronuclear bonds were $100\%$ ionic (shared electrons assigned entirely to the more electronegative atom; homonuclear bonds split $50:50$, contributing $0$). Apply these rules in strict priority order:

  1. Free / Elemental State: OS $= 0$ for every atom in an uncombined element ($\text{Fe},\, \text{O}_2,\, \text{O}_3,\, \text{P}_4,\, \text{S}_8$).
  2. Monoatomic Ions: OS equals the ionic charge ($\text{Fe}^{3+} = +3,\; \text{S}^{2-} = -2,\; \text{N}^{3-} = -3$).
  3. Fluorine ($\text{F}$): Always $-1$ in every compound (highest electronegativity, $4.0$).
  4. Alkali & Alkaline Earth Metals: Group 1 ($\text{Li, Na, K}$) $= +1$; Group 2 ($\text{Mg, Ca, Ba}$) $= +2$; $\text{Al} = +3$ in compounds.
  5. Hydrogen ($\text{H}$): $+1$ with non-metals ($\text{H}_2\text{O},\, \text{NH}_3,\, \text{HCl}$), but $-1$ in binary/complex metal hydrides ($\text{NaH},\, \text{CaH}_2,\, \text{LiAlH}_4,\, \text{NaBH}_4$).
  6. Oxygen ($\text{O}$): Normally $-2$, with five critical exceptions every Olympiad student must memorize:
    • Peroxides ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2,\, \text{Na}_2\text{O}_2,\, \text{BaO}_2$): $\text{O} = \mathbf{-1}$ (contains an $\text{-O-O-}$ bond).
    • Superoxides ($\text{KO}_2,\, \text{RbO}_2,\, \text{CsO}_2$): $\text{O} = \mathbf{-\frac{1}{2}}$ (contains $\text{O}_2^-$ ion).
    • Ozonides ($\text{KO}_3$): $\text{O} = \mathbf{-\frac{1}{3}}$ (contains $\text{O}_3^-$ ion).
    • Oxygen difluoride ($\text{OF}_2$): $\text{O} = \mathbf{+2}$ (since $\text{F} = -1$).
    • Dioxygen difluoride ($\text{O}_2\text{F}_2$): $\text{O} = \mathbf{+1}$.
  7. Charge Conservation Rule: $\sum (\text{OS of all atoms}) = 0$ for a neutral molecule, and $=$ net ionic charge for a polyatomic ion.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Structural Paradoxes & Average Oxidation States

Golden Paradox Rule: An element's true oxidation state can NEVER exceed its maximum group valence (e.g., $\text{S} \le +6$, $\text{Cr} \le +6$, $\text{N} \le +5$, $\text{Mn} \le +7$, $\text{Cl} \le +7$). Whenever the algebraic formula gives a value above this ceiling, the molecule contains peroxide ($\text{-O-O-}$) linkages where each peroxo oxygen is $-1$, not $-2$.

  • Chromium(VI) Peroxide ($\text{CrO}_5$): Naive formula gives $x + 5(-2) = 0 \implies x = +10$ (impossible, since $\text{Cr}$ has only 6 valence electrons: $3d^5 4s^1$). Its actual butterfly structure has one $\text{Cr=O}$ oxo bond ($-2$) and two peroxo rings (four $\text{O}$ atoms at $-1$): $$x + 1(-2) + 4(-1) = 0 \implies x = +6$$
  • Caro's Acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_5$, Peroxymonosulfuric acid): Naive formula gives $+8$ (impossible, $\text{S}_{\max} = +6$). Structure $\text{HO-SO}_2\text{-O-O-H}$ has one peroxo linkage (two $\text{O}$ at $-1$) and three normal $\text{O}$ ($-2$): $$2(+1) + x + 3(-2) + 2(-1) = 0 \implies x = +6$$ Bonus analog: Marshall's acid ($\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8$, peroxydisulfuric acid) has one central $\text{-O-O-}$ bridge ($\text{HO}_3\text{S-O-O-SO}_3\text{H}$), giving $\text{S} = +6$, not $+7$.
  • Magnetite ($\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$): Algebraic average gives $3x + 4(-2) = 0 \implies x_{\text{avg}} = +\frac{8}{3}$. Physically, $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$ is a mixed stoichiometric oxide $\text{FeO}\cdot \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$, containing one $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ and two $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ions per formula unit: $\frac{(+2) + 2(+3)}{3} = +\frac{8}{3}$.
  • Bleaching Powder ($\text{CaOCl}_2$): Algebraic average gives $\text{Cl}_{\text{avg}} = 0$. Structurally, it is $\text{Ca}^{2+}(\text{OCl}^-)(\text{Cl}^-)$ (calcium hypochlorite chloride): the hypochlorite chlorine is $+1$ and the chloride ion is $-1$.
  • Sodium Tetrathionate ($\text{Na}_2\text{S}_4\text{O}_6$): Algebraic average gives $4x = +10 \implies x_{\text{avg}} = +2.5$. In its linear chain $\text{O}_3\text{S-S-S-SO}_3^{2-}$, the two central $\text{S}$ atoms bond exclusively to sulfur (homonuclear bonds $\implies \text{OS} = \mathbf{0}$), while each terminal $\text{S}$ bonds to three $\text{O}$ atoms ($-2$ contribution each via two $\text{S=O}$ and one $\text{S-O}^-$) $\implies \text{OS} = \mathbf{+5}$. Individual states: $+5,\; 0,\; 0,\; +5$.

4. Types of Redox Reactions & Oxidant / Reductant Behavior

4.1 Five Structural Classes of Redox Reactions

  1. Combination Redox: At least one reactant must be in elemental state ($\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{CO}_2$; $3\text{Mg} + \text{N}_2 \longrightarrow \text{Mg}_3\text{N}_2$).
  2. Decomposition Redox: Either an elemental product forms ($2\text{KClO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{KCl} + 3\text{O}_2$) or an intramolecular electron transfer occurs ($2\text{FeSO}_4 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + \text{SO}_2 + \text{SO}_3$; $\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3 \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{N}_2\text{O} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
  3. Displacement Redox: Metal or non-metal displacement ($\text{Zn} + 2\text{HCl} \longrightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2 + \text{H}_2$).
  4. Disproportionation (Auto-Redox): A single element in an intermediate oxidation state is simultaneously oxidized to a higher state and reduced to a lower state: $$\overset{0}{\text{Cl}}_2 + 2\text{OH}^-\text{ (cold, dil.)} \longrightarrow \overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}^- + \overset{+1}{\text{ClO}}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$ $$3\overset{0}{\text{Cl}}_2 + 6\text{OH}^-\text{ (hot, conc.)} \longrightarrow 5\overset{-1}{\text{Cl}}^- + \overset{+5}{\text{ClO}}_3^- + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$ $$\overset{0}{\text{P}}_4 + 3\text{OH}^- + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \overset{-3}{\text{PH}}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\overset{+1}{\text{P}}\text{O}_2^- \qquad \text{and} \qquad 2\text{H}_2\overset{-1}{\text{O}}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\overset{-2}{\text{O}} + \overset{0}{\text{O}}_2$$ Olympiad Rule: Fluorine ($\text{F}_2$) cannot disproportionate because it never exhibits a positive oxidation state.
  5. Comproportionation (Symproportionation): The exact reverse of disproportionation—two species containing the same element in a high and a low oxidation state react to yield a common intermediate state: $$\overset{+5}{\text{IO}}_3^- + 5\overset{-1}{\text{I}}^- + 6\text{H}^+ \longrightarrow 3\overset{0}{\text{I}}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}, \qquad 2\text{H}_2\overset{-2}{\text{S}} + \overset{+4}{\text{S}}\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 3\overset{0}{\text{S}} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

5. Out-of-Portion Bridge: Systematic Redox Balancing & $n$-Factor Theory

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Two Bulletproof Balancing Algorithms

Method 1: Oxidation Number Change Method

  1. Assign oxidation numbers to every atom and identify the oxidized and reduced elements.
  2. Compute total increase in OS per formula unit of RA ($\Delta_{\text{ox}}$) and total decrease in OS per formula unit of OA ($\Delta_{\text{red}}$).
  3. Cross-multiply the OA and RA by minimal integers so that $\text{Total OS Increase} = \text{Total OS Decrease}$.
  4. Balance all atoms other than $\text{H}$ and $\text{O}$ by inspection.
  5. Balance $\text{O}$ by adding $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, then balance $\text{H}$ using $\text{H}^+$ (in acidic medium) or adjust with $\text{OH}^-/\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (in basic medium). Verify net charge on both sides.

Method 2: Ion-Electron (Half-Reaction) Method (Gold Standard for Acidic & Basic Media)

  1. Split the net ionic skeleton into separate Oxidation and Reduction half-reactions.
  2. In each half-reaction, balance all atoms other than $\text{O}$ and $\text{H}$ first.
  3. Balance $\text{O}$ atoms by adding $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules to the oxygen-deficient side.
  4. Balance $\text{H}$ atoms by adding $\text{H}^+$ ions to the hydrogen-deficient side.
  5. Balance electric charge by adding electrons ($e^-$) to the more positive side.
  6. Basic Medium Conversion Rule (Zero-Error Protocol): If the medium is basic, neutralize every $\text{H}^+$ by adding an equal number of $\text{OH}^-$ ions to BOTH sides of the half-reaction, replace $\text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^-$ with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, and cancel excess $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
  7. Multiply the two half-reactions by smallest integers so electrons lost $=$ electrons gained, add them together, and cancel common terms.

First-Principles Derivation of Redox $n$-Factor & Law of Equivalence

In any balanced redox reaction, electrons are neither created nor destroyed. Define the redox $n$-factor ($n_f$) of a species as the exact number of moles of electrons lost or gained by 1 mole of that species:

$$n_f = (\text{Number of redox-active atoms per formula unit}) \times |\text{OS}_{\text{final}} - \text{OS}_{\text{initial}}|$$

If $n_{\text{OA}}$ moles of oxidizing agent react completely with $n_{\text{RA}}$ moles of reducing agent, conservation of electrons dictates:

$$\text{Moles of } e^- \text{ gained} = \text{Moles of } e^- \text{ lost} \implies n_{\text{OA}} \cdot (n_f)_{\text{OA}} = n_{\text{RA}} \cdot (n_f)_{\text{RA}}$$

This identity is the Law of Chemical Equivalence ($\text{Equivalents of OA} = \text{Equivalents of RA}$), and the redox Equivalent Weight is $E = \dfrac{\text{Molar Mass } M}{n_f}$. It allows you to compute exact stoichiometric mole ratios in 15 seconds without balancing the full equation.

First-Principles Proof: Disproportionation $n$-Factor Formula

Suppose $1\text{ mole}$ of a species $A$ disproportionates: a fraction $x\text{ moles}$ undergoes oxidation (losing $n_1$ electrons per mole oxidized) and the remaining $y\text{ moles}$ undergoes reduction (gaining $n_2$ electrons per mole reduced), so $x + y = 1$.

By conservation of electrons within the disproportionation:

$$x \cdot n_1 = y \cdot n_2 \implies y = x \frac{n_1}{n_2}$$

Substituting $y$ into $x + y = 1$:

$$x\left(1 + \frac{n_1}{n_2}\right) = 1 \implies x = \frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$

The total number of moles of electrons transferred per mole of $A$ is therefore $n_f = x \cdot n_1 = \dfrac{n_1 n_2}{n_1 + n_2}$, which takes the memorable harmonic form:

$$\frac{1}{n_f} = \frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}$$

Sanity Check on $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2$: Per mole of $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ (2 O atoms at $-1$), oxidation to $\text{O}_2(0)$ gives $n_1 = 2 \times 1 = 2$, and reduction to $2\text{H}_2\text{O}(-2)$ gives $n_2 = 2 \times 1 = 2$. Thus $n_f = \frac{2 \times 2}{2 + 2} = 1$. Directly: $2\text{ mol H}_2\text{O}_2$ transfers $2\text{ mol }e^- \implies 1\text{ mol }e^-$ per mole of $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$. Exact match!

6. Everyday Oxidation Effects: Corrosion & Rancidity

Corrosion: Slow Electrochemical Surface Oxidation of Metals

When reactive metals are exposed to atmospheric moisture, oxygen, acidic gases ($\text{CO}_2,\, \text{SO}_2$), or hydrogen sulfide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$), surface atoms undergo redox degradation:

  • Rusting of Iron: Requires both $\text{O}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (accelerated by dissolved electrolytes like $\text{NaCl}$ or acidic $\text{CO}_2$). Anodic iron oxidation ($\text{Fe} \to \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^-$) followed by aerial oxidation produces flaky reddish-brown hydrated ferric oxide: $$4\text{Fe}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) + 2x\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}(s)$$ Prevention: Painting, greasing, alloying (stainless steel: $\text{Fe + Cr + Ni}$), and galvanization (zinc coating acts as a sacrificial anode—even when scratched, $\text{Zn}$ oxidizes preferentially over $\text{Fe}$ because $\text{Zn}$ sits above $\text{Fe}$ in the reactivity series).
  • Green Patina on Copper: Moist air containing $\text{CO}_2$ forms a protective green layer of basic copper(II) carbonate: $$2\text{Cu}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{Cu(OH)}_2\cdot\text{CuCO}_3(s)\text{ (green)}$$
  • Black Tarnish on Silver: Trace atmospheric $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ (assisted by $\text{O}_2$) converts bright silver into black silver sulfide: $$4\text{Ag}(s) + 2\text{H}_2\text{S}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{Ag}_2\text{S}(s)\text{ (black)} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
Rancidity: Aerial Oxidation of Fats & Oils

Unsaturated fatty acids in oils and lipid-rich foods react slowly with atmospheric $\text{O}_2$ via free-radical oxidation, producing volatile short-chain aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids that cause foul odor and taste (rancidity).

  • Chemical Antioxidants (Reducing Agents): BHA (Butylated Hydroxyanisole) and BHT (Butylated Hydroxytoluene) intercept oxidizing radicals before they attack lipids.
  • Inert Gas Flushing: Chip and snack packets are flushed with chemically inert nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$) to displace $\text{O}_2$.
  • Physical Barriers: Airtight containers, opaque/amber packaging (blocks UV photolysis), and refrigeration (lowers kinetic energy below $E_a$).

Calculate the exact number of moles of (a) $\text{KMnO}_4$, and (b) $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$, required separately in acidic medium to oxidize this entire mixture completely to $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ and $\text{CO}_2$.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Watch the Ferric Ion Trap!):

Dissect the oxidation states of every ion in the mixture:

  • From $6\text{ mol FeSO}_4$: we obtain $6\text{ mol Fe}^{2+}$, which oxidizes via $\text{Fe}^{2+} \longrightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + 1e^-$, releasing $6 \times 1 = 6\text{ mol }e^-$.
  • From $4\text{ mol Fe}_2(\text{C}_2\text{O}_4)_3$: iron is already in the $+3$ state ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$) and undergoes zero oxidation! Only the $4 \times 3 = 12\text{ mol}$ of oxalate ions ($\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-}$) oxidize via $\text{C}_2\text{O}_4^{2-} \longrightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 2e^-$, releasing $12 \times 2 = 24\text{ mol }e^-$.

Total electrons released by the mixture: $6 + 24 = \mathbf{30\text{ mol }e^-}$.

Using the separate balanced acidic equations:

  • (a) For $\text{KMnO}_4$: $\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 5e^- \longrightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$ absorbs $5\text{ mol }e^-$ per mole of $\text{KMnO}_4$: $$n(\text{KMnO}_4) = \frac{30\text{ mol }e^-}{5\text{ mol }e^-/\text{mol}} = \mathbf{6\text{ moles}}$$
  • (b) For $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$: $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 14\text{H}^+ + 6e^- \longrightarrow 2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 7\text{H}_2\text{O}$ absorbs $6\text{ mol }e^-$ per mole of $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$: $$n(\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7) = \frac{30\text{ mol }e^-}{6\text{ mol }e^-/\text{mol}} = \mathbf{5\text{ moles}}$$
30-Second Equivalence Shortcut: Sum reducing equivalents directly: $n_f(\text{FeSO}_4) = 1$ and $n_f(\text{Fe}_2(\text{C}_2\text{O}_4)_3) = 0\text{ (Fe}^{3+}\text{ inert)} + 3 \times 2 = 6$. Total equivalents $= 6(1) + 4(6) = 30$. Divide by $n_f(\text{KMnO}_4) = 5 \implies \mathbf{6\text{ mol}}$; divide by $n_f(\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7) = 6 \implies \mathbf{5\text{ mol}}$.
