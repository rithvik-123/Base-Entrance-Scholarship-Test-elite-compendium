1. Acid–Base Theories & Oxyacid Basicity Structures

First-Principles Evolution of Acid–Base Models

Chemistry defines acidity and basicity across three progressively broader frameworks, forming a strict hierarchy: Arrhenius $\subset$ Brønsted–Lowry $\subset$ Lewis. Every Arrhenius acid is a Brønsted–Lowry acid, and every Brønsted–Lowry acid is a Lewis acid—but the converses do not hold.

1.1 Arrhenius Theory (Aqueous Ionization Model)

  • Arrhenius Acid: A substance that dissociates in water to furnish hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$), which immediately hydrate to form hydronium ions ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$): $$\text{HCl}(g) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq) + \text{Cl}^-(aq)$$
  • Arrhenius Base (Alkali): A water-soluble substance that dissociates in water to furnish hydroxide ions ($\text{OH}^-$): $$\text{NaOH}(s) \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{Na}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq)$$ Note the critical vocabulary distinction: all alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis (insoluble bases like $\text{CuO},\; \text{Fe(OH)}_3$ neutralize acids without dissolving in water).
Critical NCERT Exemplar Trap: Dry $\text{HCl}$ Gas & $\text{HCl}$ in Benzene

Dry hydrogen chloride gas ($\text{HCl}(g)$) and solutions of $\text{HCl}$ in non-polar organic solvents (such as dry benzene, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$, or toluene) exist exclusively as neutral covalent molecules. Without polar water molecules to pull the $\text{H-Cl}$ bond apart and stabilize ions via hydration, no $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ ions form. Consequently, dry $\text{HCl}$ gas neither conducts electricity nor turns dry blue litmus paper red! Only when litmus paper is moist (providing $\text{H}_2\text{O}$) does $\text{HCl}$ ionize and turn it red.

1.2 Brønsted–Lowry Theory (Proton-Transfer Model — Foundation Bridge)

  • Brønsted–Lowry Acid: Any species capable of donating a proton ($\text{H}^+$).
  • Brønsted–Lowry Base: Any species capable of accepting a proton ($\text{H}^+$).
  • Conjugate Acid–Base Pairs: Two species that differ by strictly ONE proton ($\text{H}^+$): $$\text{Acid} \underset{+\text{H}^+}{\overset{-\text{H}^+}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Conjugate Base}, \qquad \text{Base} \underset{-\text{H}^+}{\overset{+\text{H}^+}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{Conjugate Acid}$$ Example: $\underset{\text{Acid}_1}{\text{HCl}} + \underset{\text{Base}_2}{\text{NH}_3} \rightleftharpoons \underset{\text{Conjugate Acid}_2}{\text{NH}_4^+} + \underset{\text{Conjugate Base}_1}{\text{Cl}^-}$. Notice $\text{NH}_3$ acts as a base even in the absence of $\text{OH}^-$ ions!
  • Strength Inversion Law: A strong acid always has a very weak conjugate base ($\text{HClO}_4 \to \text{ClO}_4^-$), and a weak acid has a strong conjugate base ($\text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{OH}^-$).
  • Amphiprotic (Amphoteric) Species: Species that can both donate and accept a proton—e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (conjugate acid $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$, conjugate base $\text{OH}^-$), $\text{HCO}_3^-$ ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$ / $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$), $\text{HSO}_4^-$ ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ / $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$), and $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-$ ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$ / $\text{HPO}_4^{2-}$).

1.3 Lewis Theory (Electron-Pair Model — Olympiad Bridge)

  • Lewis Acid: An electron-pair acceptor (electrophile with an incomplete octet, empty $d$-orbitals, or positive charge): $\text{BF}_3,\; \text{AlCl}_3,\; \text{FeCl}_3,\; \text{H}^+,\; \text{Ag}^+,\; \text{Cu}^{2+},\; \text{CO}_2$.
  • Lewis Base: An electron-pair donor (nucleophile bearing at least one lone pair): $\text{NH}_3,\; \text{H}_2\text{O},\; \text{OH}^-,\; \text{Cl}^-,\; \text{CN}^-$.
  • Coordinate Adduct Formation: $\text{H}_3\text{N:}\text{ (Lewis base)} + \text{BF}_3\text{ (Lewis acid)} \longrightarrow \text{H}_3\text{N}\!\rightarrow\!\text{BF}_3$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: The Phosphorus Oxyacid Basicity Trap

Basicity of an acid is NOT the total number of hydrogen atoms in its molecular formula—it is the exact number of replaceable (ionizable) $\text{H}^+$ ions per molecule. In oxyacids, only hydrogen atoms bonded directly to highly electronegative oxygen ($\text{-O-H}$) are polar enough to ionize as $\text{H}^+$. Hydrogen atoms bonded directly to phosphorus ($\text{P-H}$, where $\chi_{\text{P}} \approx 2.19 \approx \chi_{\text{H}} \approx 2.20$) are nearly non-polar and cannot ionize, though they impart strong reducing power!

  • Orthophosphoric acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$): One $\text{P=O}$ + three $\text{P-OH}$ bonds $\implies$ Tribasic ($n_f = 3$). Forms three salt series ($\text{NaH}_2\text{PO}_4,\; \text{Na}_2\text{HPO}_4,\; \text{Na}_3\text{PO}_4$).
  • Phosphorous acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3$): One $\text{P=O}$ + two $\text{P-OH}$ + one $\text{P-H}$ bond $\implies$ Dibasic ($n_f = 2$). Forms only two salt series ($\text{NaH}_2\text{PO}_3,\; \text{Na}_2\text{HPO}_3$); $\text{Na}_3\text{PO}_3$ does not exist!
  • Hypophosphorous acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_2$): One $\text{P=O}$ + one $\text{P-OH}$ + two $\text{P-H}$ bonds $\implies$ Monobasic ($n_f = 1$). Forms only $\text{NaH}_2\text{PO}_2$, and is a potent reducing agent (reduces $\text{AgNO}_3 \to \text{Ag}$).
  • Bonus Check — Boric acid ($\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3$): Contains $3\text{ H}$ atoms, yet is a weak monobasic Lewis acid (not a proton donor!); it accepts one $\text{OH}^-$ from water: $\text{B(OH)}_3 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons [\text{B(OH)}_4]^- + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+$.

2. Core Chemical Properties, Indicators & Dilution Safety

2.1 Signature Reactions of Acids & Bases

  1. Acid + Active Metal (above $\text{H}$): $\text{Metal} + \text{Dil. Acid} \longrightarrow \text{Salt} + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow$. Example: $\text{Zn}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4(aq) + \text{H}_2(g)$.
    Nitric Acid Exception: $\text{HNO}_3$ is a strong oxidant that oxidizes liberated $\text{H}_2$ to $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (reducing itself to $\text{NO},\, \text{NO}_2,$ or $\text{N}_2\text{O}$). Only $\text{Mg}$ and $\text{Mn}$ evolve $\text{H}_2$ with very dilute ($1\%$) $\text{HNO}_3$.
  2. Acid + Metal Carbonate / Hydrogencarbonate: Always evolves brisk effervescence of $\text{CO}_2$: $$\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)\uparrow$$ $$\text{NaHCO}_3(s) + \text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)\uparrow$$
  3. Acid + Basic Metal Oxide: Produces salt and water. Classic color diagnostic: black copper(II) oxide dissolves in dilute $\text{HCl}$ to form a blue-green solution: $$\text{CuO}(s)\text{ (black)} + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{CuCl}_2(aq)\text{ (blue-green)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
  4. Strong Base + Amphoteric Metals ($\text{Zn},\; \text{Al}$): Unlike most metals, amphoteric metals dissolve in hot concentrated alkali, liberating $\text{H}_2$ gas: $$\text{Zn}(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2(aq)\text{ (sodium zincate)} + \text{H}_2(g)\uparrow$$ $$2\text{Al}(s) + 2\text{NaOH}(aq) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaAlO}_2(aq)\text{ (sodium aluminate)} + 3\text{H}_2(g)\uparrow$$
  5. Base + Acidic Non-Metal Oxide: $\text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq) + \text{CO}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{CaCO}_3(s)\downarrow + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ (proves non-metallic oxides are acidic).

Dilution Safety Law: Acid into Water (A → W), NEVER Water into Acid

Hydration of concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ or $\text{HNO}_3$ is fiercely exothermic. Always add acid dropwise along the beaker wall into a large volume of water with continuous stirring so the high specific heat capacity of water ($4.18\text{ J g}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}$) safely absorbs the heat. Pouring water onto concentrated acid causes localized superheating, violent steam-splashing of corrosive acid, and thermal shock cracking of the glassware. Note that diluting an acid decreases $[\text{H}_3\text{O}^+]$ per unit volume (increasing $\text{pH}$ toward $7$).

3. Logarithmic pH Scale & Ionic Equilibrium Bridge

3.1 Self-Ionization of Water ($K_w$) & The Temperature Trap

Pure water undergoes slight endothermic auto-ionization ($\Delta H \approx +57.3\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$):

$$2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_3\text{O}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq), \qquad K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-]$$

At $25^\circ\text{C}$ ($298\text{ K}$), $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}\text{ M}^2$. Taking $-\log_{10}$ of both sides yields the master identities:

$$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+], \qquad \text{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\text{OH}^-], \qquad \text{pH} + \text{pOH} = \text{p}K_w = 14\;\; (\text{at }25^\circ\text{C})$$
Olympiad Temperature Trap: Neutral Water Is NOT Always $\text{pH} = 7$!

Because water auto-ionization is endothermic, raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium forward (Le Chatelier's Principle), increasing $K_w$! At $60^\circ\text{C}$, $K_w \approx 10^{-13}\text{ M}^2$, so pure water has $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-6.5}\text{ M}$ and $\text{pH}_{\text{neutral}} = 6.5 \lt 7$! Remember the true universal criterion of neutrality at any temperature: $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$ (i.e., $\text{pH} = \frac{1}{2}\text{p}K_w$), NOT $\text{pH} = 7$.

3.2 Exact pH Calculation Cases (Strong Acids, Diprotic Normality, Mixing & Ultra-Dilute Trap)

  1. Strong Monoprotic & Polyprotic Acids / Bases (Normality Factor): For complete dissociation, multiply molarity $M$ by basicity/acidity $n_f$: $$[\text{H}^+] = n_a M_a \quad (\text{e.g., }n_a = 2\text{ for }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4), \qquad [\text{OH}^-] = n_b M_b \quad (\text{e.g., }n_b = 2\text{ for }\text{Ba(OH)}_2,\, \text{Ca(OH)}_2)$$
  2. Mixing Strong Acids & Strong Bases: Compute total milliequivalents (millimoles of $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$): $$N_a V_a = \sum n_{a,i} M_{a,i} V_{a,i}, \qquad N_b V_b = \sum n_{b,j} M_{b,j} V_{b,j}$$
    • If $N_a V_a \gt N_b V_b$: acidic, $[\text{H}^+] = \dfrac{N_a V_a - N_b V_b}{V_{\text{total}}}$.
    • If $N_b V_b \gt N_a V_a$: basic, $[\text{OH}^-] = \dfrac{N_b V_b - N_a V_a}{V_{\text{total}}}$, then $\text{pH} = 14 - \text{pOH}$.
    • If $N_a V_a = N_b V_b$: complete neutralization, $\text{pH} = 7.0$ (at $25^\circ\text{C}$).
  3. The Ultra-Dilute Strong Acid/Base Trap ($c \le 10^{-6}\text{ M}$): Never ignore water's own $\text{H}^+$ contribution! For a strong monoprotic acid of molarity $c$, charge neutrality demands $[\text{H}^+] = c + [\text{OH}^-] = c + \dfrac{K_w}{[\text{H}^+]}$, yielding the exact quadratic equation: $$[\text{H}^+]^2 - c[\text{H}^+] - K_w = 0 \implies [\text{H}^+] = \frac{c + \sqrt{c^2 + 4K_w}}{2}$$ For $10^{-8}\text{ M HCl}$, this yields $[\text{H}^+] = 1.051 \times 10^{-7}\text{ M} \implies \mathbf{\text{pH} = 6.98}$ (strictly acidic, NEVER $8$!). Symmetrically, $10^{-8}\text{ M NaOH}$ yields $[\text{OH}^-] = 1.051 \times 10^{-7}\text{ M} \implies \text{pOH} = 6.98 \implies \mathbf{\text{pH} = 7.02}$ (strictly basic, NEVER $6$!).

3.3 High-Yield Everyday pH Applications

  • Tooth Decay: Tooth enamel is calcium hydroxyapatite, $\text{Ca}_5(\text{PO}_4)_3\text{OH}$ (hardest substance in the human body). Mouth bacteria ferment sugars into acids; when mouth $\text{pH}$ falls below $5.5$, enamel corrodes. Basic toothpastes neutralize this acid.
  • Acid Rain: Normal rainwater is slightly acidic ($\text{pH} \approx 5.6$) due to dissolved $\text{CO}_2$ ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$). When atmospheric $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{NO}_2$ drop rainwater $\text{pH}$ below $5.6$, it becomes acid rain, harming aquatic life.
  • Digestive Antacids & Stings: Excess gastric $\text{HCl}$ is relieved by mild bases like Milk of Magnesia ($\text{Mg(OH)}_2$) or baking soda ($\text{NaHCO}_3$). Honeybee stings and stinging nettle leaves inject methanoic (formic) acid ($\text{HCOOH}$), neutralized by rubbing mild $\text{NaHCO}_3$ (or basic dock plant leaves).

4. Salt Hydrolysis & Five Commercial Chemical Processes

4.1 Predicting Salt Solution pH via Parent Acid & Parent Base

A salt is neutral only when neither of its ions hydrolyzes water. Ions originating from a weak acid or weak base react reversibly with water (salt hydrolysis) to shift pH:

Salt Class (Parent Acid + Parent Base) Hydrolyzing Ion Aqueous pH ($25^\circ\text{C}$) Exam Benchmark Salts
Strong Acid + Strong Base None (no hydrolysis) Neutral ($\text{pH} = 7$) $\text{NaCl},\; \text{KNO}_3,\; \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4,\; \text{BaCl}_2$
Strong Acid + Weak Base Cation ($\text{BH}^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{B} + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+$) Acidic ($\text{pH} \lt 7$) $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl},\; (\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4,\; \text{CuSO}_4,\; \text{AlCl}_3,\; \text{FeCl}_3$

4.2 Master Reference of Commercial Compounds from Common Salt & Gypsum

  1. Chlor-Alkali Process (Caustic Soda, $\text{NaOH}$): Electrolysis of concentrated aqueous $\text{NaCl}$ (brine) in a cation-exchange membrane cell yields three industrial feedstocks simultaneously: $$2\text{NaCl}(aq) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \xrightarrow{\text{Electricity}} 2\text{NaOH}(aq)\text{ (near cathode)} + \text{Cl}_2(g)\text{ (anode)} + \text{H}_2(g)\text{ (cathode)}$$ Uses: $\text{Cl}_2$ (water disinfection, PVC, CFCs, bleaching powder); $\text{H}_2$ (rocket fuel, margarine hydrogenation, $\text{NH}_3$ synthesis); $\text{NaOH}$ (degreasing metals, soaps/detergents, paper, artificial fibers).
  2. Bleaching Powder ($\text{CaOCl}_2$, Calcium Oxychloride): Prepared by passing chlorine gas over dry slaked lime: $$\text{Ca(OH)}_2(s) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{CaOCl}_2(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$ Uses: Bleaching cotton/linen and wood pulp, oxidizing agent in chemical industries, and disinfecting drinking water (releases available $\text{Cl}_2$ upon reacting with dilute acids/atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$).
  3. Baking Soda ($\text{NaHCO}_3$) vs. Baking Powder: Manufactured via the Solvay ammonia-soda reaction: $$\text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2 + \text{NH}_3 \longrightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{Cl} + \text{NaHCO}_3\downarrow$$ On heating during cooking: $2\text{NaHCO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(g) + \text{CO}_2(g)\uparrow$. Because $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ tastes bitter and soapy, baking powder blends $\text{NaHCO}_3$ with a mild edible solid acid (tartaric acid): $\text{NaHCO}_3 + \text{H}^+\text{ (acid)} \longrightarrow \text{CO}_2\uparrow + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{sodium tartrate}$ (tasteless!). Also used in soda-acid fire extinguishers and antacids.
  4. Washing Soda ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$): Recrystallizing soda ash ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$) obtained from heating $\text{NaHCO}_3$: $$\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3(s) + 10\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3\cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}(s)$$ Uses: Removing permanent hardness of water (precipitates $\text{Ca}^{2+}/\text{Mg}^{2+}$ as insoluble carbonates), glass/soap/paper industries, and manufacturing borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
  5. Plaster of Paris ($\text{CaSO}_4\cdot \frac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}$) vs. Gypsum ($\text{CaSO}_4\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$): Controlled heating of gypsum at exactly $373\text{ K}$ ($100^\circ\text{C}$) drives off three-quarters of its water of crystallization: $$\text{CaSO}_4\cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(s) \xrightarrow{373\text{ K}} \text{CaSO}_4\cdot \frac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}(s) + 1\frac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$$ The fractional $\frac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}$ simply means two formula units of $\text{CaSO}_4$ share one $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecule ($2\text{CaSO}_4\cdot\text{H}_2\text{O}$). Mixing POP with water reverses the equation exothermically into a hard interlocking gypsum mass (surgical casts, statues). Dead-Burnt Plaster Trap: Heating gypsum above $473\text{ K}$ ($200^\circ\text{C}$) expels all water, yielding anhydrous $\text{CaSO}_4$ (dead-burnt plaster), which completely loses its setting property. Store POP in moisture-proof containers!

5. Out-of-Portion Bridge: Electrolytes, Preferential Discharge & Faraday's Law

5.1 Electrolyte Strength & Electrolytic Cell Polarity

  • Strong Electrolytes ($\alpha \approx 100\%$): Completely dissociated into mobile ions ($\text{HCl},\; \text{HNO}_3,\; \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4,\; \text{NaOH},\; \text{KOH}$, almost all soluble ionic salts).
  • Weak Electrolytes ($\alpha \ll 100\%$): Partially ionized in dynamic equilibrium ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH},\; \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3,\; \text{NH}_4\text{OH},\; \text{HF}$).
  • Non-Electrolytes ($\alpha = 0$): Exist purely as neutral covalent molecules in solution; do not conduct electricity (glucose, sucrose, urea, ethanol, distilled water's negligible auto-ionization).
  • Electrolytic Cell Sign Lock ("AN OX / RED CAT"): Driven by an external DC battery:
    • Anode = Positive ($+$) electrode: Attracts negative anions; Oxidation (electron loss) ALWAYS occurs here.
    • Cathode = Negative ($-$) electrode: Attracts positive cations; Reduction (electron gain) ALWAYS occurs here.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Preferential Discharge Theory

In an aqueous electrolyte, water introduces competing $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$ ions alongside the salt's cation and anion. Which ion discharges at each inert electrode is governed by its ease of discharge (standard electrode potential $E^\circ$, modulated by concentration/overpotential):

1. Cation Discharge Order at Cathode (Increasing Ease of Reduction → Rightmost Wins):

$$\text{K}^+ \lt \text{Na}^+ \lt \text{Ca}^{2+} \lt \text{Mg}^{2+} \lt \text{Al}^{3+} \lt \text{Zn}^{2+} \lt \text{Fe}^{2+} \lt \mathbf{\text{H}^+} \lt \text{Cu}^{2+} \lt \text{Ag}^+$$

Consequence: Active metals ($\text{K}, \text{Na}, \text{Ca}, \text{Mg}, \text{Al}$) can NEVER be electroplated from aqueous solutions because $\text{H}^+$ (from $\text{H}_2\text{O}$) reduces far more easily to $\text{H}_2(g)$! They must be extracted from their molten halides/oxides.

2. Anion Discharge Order at Inert Anode (Increasing Ease of Oxidation → Rightmost Wins):

$$\text{SO}_4^{2-} \lt \text{NO}_3^- \lt \mathbf{\text{OH}^-} \lt \text{Cl}^- \lt \text{Br}^- \lt \text{I}^-$$

Concentration & Overpotential Nuance: In concentrated aqueous $\text{NaCl}$ (brine), $\text{Cl}^-$ discharges over $\text{OH}^-$ because oxygen evolution carries a high kinetic overpotential on electrode surfaces and $[\text{Cl}^-] \gg [\text{OH}^-]$. In very dilute $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{OH}^-$ wins and $\text{O}_2(g)$ evolves instead. Sulfate ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$) and nitrate ($\text{NO}_3^-$) never discharge in dilute aqueous media—$\text{OH}^-$ always oxidizes to $\text{O}_2$.

5.3 Faraday's First Law of Electrolysis (First-Principles Derivation)

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Quantitative Electrolysis from $1\text{ Faraday}$

What is the total electric charge carried by $1\text{ mole}$ of electrons? Multiply Avogadro's number by the elementary charge:

$$1\text{ Faraday (F)} = N_A \cdot e = (6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1})(1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) \approx 96485\text{ C mol}^{-1} \approx \mathbf{96500\text{ C mol}^{-1}}$$

Consider depositing a metal ion $M^{n+} + n\,e^- \longrightarrow M(s)$:

  • Depositing $1\text{ mole}$ ($M\text{ grams}$) requires $n\text{ moles}$ of electrons $= n \times 96500\text{ C}$.
  • Therefore, passing exactly $1\text{ F} = 96500\text{ C}$ deposits $\dfrac{M}{n} = E$ grams (exactly $1$ gram-equivalent of ANY substance!).
  • Passing a steady current $I$ (amperes) for time $t$ (seconds) supplies total charge $Q = I \cdot t$ coulombs, which equals $\dfrac{I \cdot t}{96500}$ Faradays (equivalents). Hence Faraday's First Law:
$$m = Z \cdot Q = Z \cdot I \cdot t = \frac{E \cdot I \cdot t}{96500} = \frac{M \cdot I \cdot t}{n \cdot 96500}$$

where $Z = \dfrac{E}{96500}\text{ g C}^{-1}$ is the electrochemical equivalent (ECE). Corollary (Faraday's Second Law): When the same charge $Q$ passes through multiple cells connected in series, the masses deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights: $\dfrac{m_1}{E_1} = \dfrac{m_2}{E_2} = \dfrac{Q}{96500}$.

Chapter 24 Master Formula & Benchmark Vault
  • Water Auto-Ionization ($25^\circ\text{C}$): $K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-14}$; $\;\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14$. As $T \uparrow$, $K_w \uparrow$ and $\text{pH}_{\text{neutral}} = \frac{1}{2}\text{p}K_w \lt 7$.
  • Strong Acid/Base Normality: $[\text{H}^+] = n_a M_a$, $[\text{OH}^-] = n_b M_b$; $\;\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$.
  • Mixing Strong Acid + Base: Excess concentration $= \dfrac{|N_a V_a - N_b V_b|}{V_a + V_b}$.
  • Ultra-Dilute Strong Acid ($c \le 10^{-6}\text{ M}$): $[\text{H}^+] = \dfrac{c + \sqrt{c^2 + 4K_w}}{2}$ ($10^{-8}\text{ M HCl} \implies \text{pH} = 6.98$; $10^{-8}\text{ M NaOH} \implies \text{pH} = 7.02$).
  • Oxyacid Basicities: $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4 \to 3$, $\;\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3 \to 2$, $\;\text{H}_3\text{PO}_2 \to 1$, $\;\text{H}_3\text{BO}_3 \to 1$ (Lewis acid).
  • Faraday's First Law: $n(e^-) = \dfrac{I \cdot t}{96500}\text{ F}$; $\;m = \dfrac{E \cdot I \cdot t}{96500} = \dfrac{M \cdot I \cdot t}{n \cdot 96500}$. At STP ($22.4\text{ L mol}^{-1}$): $1\text{ F}$ liberates $11.2\text{ L H}_2$, $11.2\text{ L Cl}_2$, or $5.6\text{ L O}_2$.

Level 1 Logarithmic pH & Normality Worked Example 3: Exact pH of Strong Diprotic Acid & Diacidic Base
Assuming $100\%$ dissociation at $25^\circ\text{C}$, calculate the exact $\text{pH}$ of: (a) $0.005\text{ M }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, and (b) $0.0005\text{ M }\text{Ba(OH)}_2$.

(a) $0.005\text{ M }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$: Sulfuric acid is diprotic ($n_a = 2$): $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{H}^+(aq) + \text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq)$.

$$[\text{H}^+] = 2 \times 0.005\text{ M} = 0.01\text{ M} = 1.0 \times 10^{-2}\text{ M}$$ $$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(1.0 \times 10^{-2}) = \mathbf{2.0}$$

(b) $0.0005\text{ M }\text{Ba(OH)}_2$: Barium hydroxide is diacidic ($n_b = 2$): $\text{Ba(OH)}_2(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Ba}^{2+}(aq) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq)$.

$$[\text{OH}^-] = 2 \times 0.0005\text{ M} = 0.001\text{ M} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ M}$$ $$\text{pOH} = -\log_{10}(1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 3.0 \implies \text{pH} = 14 - \text{pOH} = 14 - 3.0 = \mathbf{11.0}$$

Level 2 Acid–Base Mixing pH Worked Example 4: Mixing Strong Diprotic Acid with Strong Monoacidic Base
$200\text{ mL}$ of $0.10\text{ M }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is mixed with $300\text{ mL}$ of $0.10\text{ M }\text{NaOH}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$. Calculate the exact $\text{pH}$ of the resulting mixture. (Given: $\log_{10} 2 = 0.301$.)

Step 1 (Compute Milliequivalents of $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$):

  • From $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($n_a = 2$): $n(\text{H}^+) = 2 \times 0.10\text{ M} \times 200\text{ mL} = 40\text{ mmol}$.
  • From $\text{NaOH}$ ($n_b = 1$): $n(\text{OH}^-) = 1 \times 0.10\text{ M} \times 300\text{ mL} = 30\text{ mmol}$.

Step 2 (Neutralization & Excess Ion Concentration): The $30\text{ mmol OH}^-$ neutralizes $30\text{ mmol H}^+$, leaving excess acid:

$$n_{\text{excess}}(\text{H}^+) = 40 - 30 = 10\text{ mmol}, \qquad V_{\text{total}} = 200 + 300 = 500\text{ mL}$$ $$[\text{H}^+] = \frac{10\text{ mmol}}{500\text{ mL}} = 0.02\text{ M} = 2 \times 10^{-2}\text{ M}$$

Step 3 (Logarithmic pH):

$$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(2 \times 10^{-2}) = 2 - \log_{10} 2 = 2 - 0.301 = \mathbf{1.699 \approx 1.70}$$
Level 2 Ionic Equilibrium & Temperature Worked Example 5: Neutrality & Basicity Above Room Temperature ($60^\circ\text{C}$)
At $60^\circ\text{C}$, the ionic product of water is $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-13}\text{ M}^2$. (a) Calculate the $\text{pH}$ of pure neutral water at $60^\circ\text{C}$. (b) If an aqueous solution at $60^\circ\text{C}$ has $\text{pH} = 6.8$, determine quantitatively whether it is acidic, basic, or neutral.

(a) Neutral Water at $60^\circ\text{C}$: In pure water, $[\text{H}^+] = [\text{OH}^-]$. From $K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-13}$:

$$[\text{H}^+]^2 = 10^{-13} \implies [\text{H}^+] = 10^{-6.5}\text{ M} \implies \text{pH}_{\text{neutral}} = -\log_{10}(10^{-6.5}) = \mathbf{6.5}$$

(b) Classifying $\text{pH} = 6.8$ at $60^\circ\text{C}$: At $60^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{p}K_w = -\log_{10}(10^{-13}) = 13$, so $\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 13$:

$$[\text{H}^+] = 10^{-6.8}\text{ M}, \qquad \text{pOH} = 13 - 6.8 = 6.2 \implies [\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-6.2}\text{ M}$$

Comparing the two concentrations: $\dfrac{[\text{OH}^-]}{[\text{H}^+]} = \dfrac{10^{-6.2}}{10^{-6.8}} = 10^{0.6} \approx 3.98 \gt 1$. Because $[\text{OH}^-] \gt [\text{H}^+]$ (and $\text{pH } 6.8 \gt \text{pH}_{\text{neutral}}\; 6.5$), the solution is strictly BASIC despite having $\text{pH} \lt 7$!

Level 2 Preferential Discharge Worked Example 6: Aqueous $\text{CuSO}_4$ Electrolysis — Inert Pt vs. Active Cu Electrodes
A blue aqueous solution of $\text{CuSO}_4$ is electrolyzed in two independent cells: Cell A uses inert platinum ($\text{Pt}$) electrodes, while Cell B uses pure copper ($\text{Cu}$) electrodes. Write the balanced cathode and anode half-reactions for both cells and deduce how (i) blue color intensity and (ii) solution $\text{pH}$ evolve in each cell.

Competing Ions Present: $\text{Cu}^{2+},\; \text{SO}_4^{2-}$ (from $\text{CuSO}_4$) and $\text{H}^+,\; \text{OH}^-$ (from $\text{H}_2\text{O}$).

Cell A (Inert Pt Electrodes):

  • Cathode ($-$): $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ lies to the right of $\text{H}^+$ in the preferential discharge series $\implies \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Cu}(s)$ (reddish-brown copper plates onto cathode).
  • Anode ($+$): $\text{OH}^-$ discharges preferentially over $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ $\implies 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{O}_2(g)\uparrow + 4\text{H}^+(aq) + 4e^-$.
  • Overall Reaction: $2\text{CuSO}_4(aq) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow 2\text{Cu}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) + 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq)$.
  • Observable Changes: (i) Blue color fades steadily as $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions are depleted; (ii) $\text{pH}$ decreases sharply as $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ accumulates.

Cell B (Active Cu Electrodes):

  • Cathode ($-$): $\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Cu}(s)$ (cathode gains mass).
  • Anode ($+$): Metallic copper oxidizes more readily than $\text{OH}^-$ or $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ $\implies \text{Cu}(s) \longrightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-$ (anode corrodes/loses equal mass).
  • Observable Changes: For every $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ion deposited at the cathode, exactly one $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ion enters from the anode; hence (i) blue color intensity stays constant and (ii) solution $\text{pH}$ remains unchanged.
LEVEL 3: RANK-BOOSTER & OLYMPIAD BRIDGE (Full First-Principles Proof + 30-Second Exam Shortcut)
Level 3 Ultra-Dilute Ionic Equilibrium Worked Example 7: Exact pH of $1.0 \times 10^{-8}\text{ M HCl}$ (The Classic Olympiad Trap)
Calculate the exact $\text{pH}$ of $1.0 \times 10^{-8}\text{ M HCl}$ aqueous solution at $25^\circ\text{C}$, and prove mathematically why $\text{pH} = 8$ is impossible. (Given: $\sqrt{401} = 20.025$ and $\log_{10}(1.051) = 0.022$.)

Full First-Principles Derivation (Charge Balance + Common-Ion Effect):

Adding an acid ($\text{HCl}$) to pure neutral water ($\text{pH} = 7.0$) can never yield an alkaline solution ($\text{pH} = 8.0$). When acid concentration $c = 1.0 \times 10^{-8}\text{ M}$ is comparable to or smaller than $10^{-7}\text{ M}$, $\text{H}^+$ from water auto-ionization cannot be neglected. Let total $[\text{H}^+] = x$:

  • Complete dissociation of $\text{HCl}$ gives $[\text{Cl}^-] = c = 10^{-8}\text{ M}$.
  • Water equilibrium demands $[\text{OH}^-] = \dfrac{K_w}{x} = \dfrac{10^{-14}}{x}$ (note that water ionization is slightly suppressed below $10^{-7}\text{ M}$ by the common-ion $\text{H}^+$!).
  • By electroneutrality ($\sum\text{positive charges} = \sum\text{negative charges}$): $$[\text{H}^+] = [\text{Cl}^-] + [\text{OH}^-] \implies x = c + \frac{K_w}{x} \implies x^2 - cx - K_w = 0$$

Apply the quadratic formula (retaining the positive root $x \gt 0$):

$$x = \frac{c + \sqrt{c^2 + 4K_w}}{2} = \frac{10^{-8} + \sqrt{10^{-16} + 400 \times 10^{-16}}}{2} = \frac{1 + \sqrt{401}}{2} \times 10^{-8}$$

Substituting $\sqrt{401} = 20.025$:

$$x = \frac{1 + 20.025}{2} \times 10^{-8} = 10.5125 \times 10^{-8} = 1.051 \times 10^{-7}\text{ M}$$ $$\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(1.051 \times 10^{-7}) = 7 - \log_{10}(1.051) = 7 - 0.022 = \mathbf{6.978 \approx 6.98}$$
30-Second Ultra-Dilute Shortcut: For any strong monoprotic acid with $c \ll 10^{-7}\text{ M}$, $\sqrt{c^2 + 4K_w} \approx 2\sqrt{K_w} = 2 \times 10^{-7}$, giving the razor-accurate formula $[\text{H}^+] \approx 10^{-7} + \dfrac{c}{2}$! Here $[\text{H}^+] \approx 10^{-7} + 0.05 \times 10^{-7} = 1.05 \times 10^{-7}\text{ M} \implies \text{pH} = 7 - \log_{10}(1.05) = \mathbf{6.98}$ (error $\lt 0.001$ pH units!). Memorize the benchmark trio: $10^{-7}\text{ M HCl} \to \mathbf{6.79}$, $10^{-8}\text{ M HCl} \to \mathbf{6.98}$, $10^{-8}\text{ M NaOH} \to \mathbf{7.02}$.
Level 3 Faraday's First Law & Gas STP Volume Worked Example 8: Simultaneous Copper Plating, Oxygen Evolution & Acid Generation
A steady current of $9.65\text{ A}$ is passed for $1000\text{ s}$ through aqueous $\text{CuSO}_4$ using inert platinum electrodes. Calculate: (a) the mass of copper deposited at the cathode, (b) the STP volume (in litres, $V_m = 22.4\text{ L mol}^{-1}$) of $\text{O}_2(g)$ evolved at the anode, and (c) the moles of $\text{H}^+$ ions generated in the electrolyte. (Atomic mass: $\text{Cu} = 63.5\text{ u}$; $1\text{ F} = 96500\text{ C}$.)

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 (Total Charge & Moles of Electrons):

$$Q = I \cdot t = 9.65\text{ A} \times 1000\text{ s} = 9650\text{ C}, \qquad n(e^-) = \frac{Q}{96500} = \frac{9650}{96500} = \mathbf{0.10\text{ mol }e^-}\;\; (0.10\text{ F})$$

Step 2 (Cathode — Copper Mass): From $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{Cu}(s)$, $2\text{ mol }e^-$ deposits $1\text{ mol Cu}$:

$$n(\text{Cu}) = \frac{0.10}{2} = 0.05\text{ mol} \implies m(\text{Cu}) = 0.05\text{ mol} \times 63.5\text{ g mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{3.175\text{ g}}$$

Step 3 (Anode — Oxygen Volume & $\text{H}^+$ Generated): From $2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \text{O}_2(g) + 4\text{H}^+(aq) + 4e^-$:

  • $4\text{ mol }e^-$ yields $1\text{ mol O}_2 \implies n(\text{O}_2) = \dfrac{0.10}{4} = 0.025\text{ mol} \implies V(\text{O}_2) = 0.025 \times 22.4 = \mathbf{0.56\text{ L}}$ ($560\text{ mL}$).
  • $4\text{ mol }e^-$ generates $4\text{ mol H}^+ \implies n(\text{H}^+) = \mathbf{0.10\text{ mol}}$.
30-Second Equivalent-Volume Shortcut: Once you compute $F = \dfrac{I \cdot t}{96500} = 0.10\text{ eq}$, every product equals $0.10\text{ equivalents}$: $m(\text{Cu}) = 0.10 \times \dfrac{63.5}{2} = \mathbf{3.175\text{ g}}$; $V(\text{O}_2) = 0.10 \times 5.6\text{ L eq}^{-1} = \mathbf{0.56\text{ L}}$ (memorize: $1\text{ eq O}_2 = 5.6\text{ L}$, $1\text{ eq H}_2 = 11.2\text{ L}$ at STP); $n(\text{H}^+) = 0.10\text{ eq} = \mathbf{0.10\text{ mol}}$!
Level 3 Coupled Electrolysis + pH Shift Worked Example 9: Calculating Final pH After Electrolyzing Aqueous $\text{NaCl}$
$500\text{ mL}$ of a $1.0\text{ M}$ aqueous $\text{NaCl}$ solution (initially neutral, $\text{pH} = 7.0$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$) is electrolyzed between inert platinum electrodes with a steady current of $1.93\text{ A}$ for $2500\text{ s}$. Assuming constant solution volume and $100\%$ current efficiency, determine the exact final $\text{pH}$ of the solution. ($1\text{ F} = 96500\text{ C}$.)

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 (Faradays Passed & Limiting-Reactant Check):

$$Q = I \cdot t = 1.93\text{ A} \times 2500\text{ s} = 4825\text{ C} \implies n(e^-) = \frac{4825}{96500} = 0.050\text{ mol }e^-$$

Initial $\text{NaCl}$ present $= 1.0\text{ M} \times 0.500\text{ L} = 0.50\text{ mol} \gg 0.050\text{ mol}$, confirming brine stays concentrated throughout.

Step 2 (Electrode Reactions & $\text{OH}^-$ Yield):

  • Anode ($+$): $2\text{Cl}^-(aq) \longrightarrow \text{Cl}_2(g) + 2e^-$
  • Cathode ($-$): $2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + 2e^- \longrightarrow \text{H}_2(g) + 2\text{OH}^-(aq)$

Since $2\text{ mol }e^-$ produces $2\text{ mol OH}^-$, the stoichiometry is $1:1$, so $n(\text{OH}^-) = n(e^-) = 0.050\text{ mol}$.

Step 3 (Final $[\text{OH}^-]$, $\text{pOH}$, and $\text{pH}$):

$$[\text{OH}^-] = \frac{0.050\text{ mol}}{0.500\text{ L}} = 0.10\text{ M} = 10^{-1}\text{ M}$$ $$\text{pOH} = -\log_{10}(10^{-1}) = 1.0 \implies \text{pH} = 14 - 1.0 = \mathbf{13.0}$$
30-Second Electrolysis-pH Shortcut: Whenever aqueous $\text{NaCl}$ is electrolyzed (with excess $\text{NaCl}$), every $1\text{ F}$ generates $1\text{ mol OH}^-$, so directly plug into: $$[\text{OH}^-] = \frac{I \cdot t}{96500 \cdot V_{\text{L}}} = \frac{1.93 \times 2500}{96500 \times 0.5} = 0.1\text{ M} \implies \text{pOH} = 1 \implies \text{pH} = \mathbf{13.0}$$ (Notice the sweet arithmetic factor: $\frac{1.93}{96500} = 2 \times 10^{-5}\text{ F s}^{-1}$!)
