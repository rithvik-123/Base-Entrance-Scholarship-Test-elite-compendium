3.1 The Fundamental Rate Law & Proportionality Traps

Both Time–Speed–Distance (TSD) and Time & Work obey the exact same mathematical conservation law: an output quantity equals a rate multiplied by duration. In TSD, $D = S \times T$; in Time & Work, $W = \eta \times T$ (where $\eta$ is efficiency, or work per unit time). Recognizing this structural isomorphism lets you transfer proportionality shortcuts seamlessly across both halves of this chapter.

Core Principle · Three-Way Proportionality & Unit Calibration

From the fundamental equation $D = S \times T$, locking any one variable creates an exact ratio law between the remaining two:

  • Constant Distance ($D_1 = D_2$): Speed and Time are inversely proportional: $$S \propto \frac{1}{T} \implies \frac{S_1}{S_2} = \frac{T_2}{T_1}$$ Crucial Inversion: If speed changes by a fraction $\frac{a}{b}$, time changes by $\frac{b}{a}$. A speed reduction of $25\%$ (factor $\frac{3}{4}$) does not increase time by $25\%$; it multiplies time by $\frac{4}{3}$, an increase of $33\frac{1}{3}\%$.
  • Constant Time ($T_1 = T_2$): Distance and Speed are directly proportional: $$D \propto S \implies \frac{D_1}{D_2} = \frac{S_1}{S_2}$$ Whenever two bodies start simultaneously and meet at the same instant, the ratio of distances they cover equals the ratio of their speeds.
  • Constant Speed ($S_1 = S_2$): Distance and Time are directly proportional: $$D \propto T \implies \frac{D_1}{D_2} = \frac{T_1}{T_2}$$
  • Exact Unit Conversion: $$1\text{ km/h} = \frac{1000\text{ m}}{3600\text{ s}} = \frac{5}{18}\text{ m/s}, \qquad 1\text{ m/s} = \frac{18}{5}\text{ km/h}$$

Average Speed: Harmonic Mean vs. Arithmetic Mean

By definition, average speed is never the average of speeds—it is always the ratio of total distance covered to total time elapsed:

$$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{D_{\text{total}}}{T_{\text{total}}} = \frac{d_1 + d_2 + \dots + d_n}{t_1 + t_2 + \dots + t_n}$$
Condition Derivation Resulting Mean Exam Trap
Equal Time Intervals ($t_1 = t_2 = \dots = t_n = t$) $v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{v_1 t + v_2 t + \dots + v_n t}{nt}$ Arithmetic Mean (AM):
$\frac{v_1 + v_2 + \dots + v_n}{n}$		 Assuming AM applies when distances are equal.
Equal Distances ($d_1 = d_2 = d$) $v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2d}{\frac{d}{v_1} + \frac{d}{v_2}} = \frac{2}{\frac{1}{v_1} + \frac{1}{v_2}}$ Harmonic Mean (HM):
$\frac{2 v_1 v_2}{v_1 + v_2}$		 For $n$ equal legs: $\frac{n}{\frac{1}{v_1} + \frac{1}{v_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{v_n}}$ (not $\frac{3v_1v_2v_3}{v_1+v_2+v_3}$!).
Weighted Distances ($d_1 : d_2 = w_1 : w_2$) $v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{w_1 + w_2}{\frac{w_1}{v_1} + \frac{w_2}{v_2}}$ Weighted Harmonic Mean Since $\text{HM} \le \text{AM}$ (strictly less if $v_1 \ne v_2$), round-trip average speed is always strictly less than the arithmetic mean of the two speeds.

Stoppages & Speed-Change Equations

1. Stoppage Time per Hour: Suppose a vehicle's average speed excluding stoppages (its actual cruising speed) is $S_{\text{run}}$, and its average speed including stoppages is $S_{\text{stop}}$ ($S_{\text{stop}} < S_{\text{run}}$). In 1 clock hour, the vehicle covers a distance of $S_{\text{stop}}$ km. At its cruising speed $S_{\text{run}}$, the time actually spent moving is $\frac{S_{\text{stop}}}{S_{\text{run}}}$ hours. Therefore, the idle stoppage time per hour is:

$$t_{\text{stop/hr}} = 1 - \frac{S_{\text{stop}}}{S_{\text{run}}} = \frac{S_{\text{run}} - S_{\text{stop}}}{S_{\text{run}}}\text{ hours} = \left(\frac{S_{\text{run}} - S_{\text{stop}}}{S_{\text{run}}}\right) \times 60\text{ minutes/hour}$$

Trap Check: The denominator is always the faster speed ($S_{\text{run}}$, excluding stoppages), never $S_{\text{stop}}$.

2. Fixed-Distance Late/Early Speed-Change Equation: When a commuter covers a fixed distance $D$ at speed $S_1$ and then at speed $S_2$, causing a total time difference $\Delta T$ (add minutes if one is late and the other is early; subtract if both are late or both are early):

$$\Delta T = \left|\frac{D}{S_1} - \frac{D}{S_2}\right| = D \left|\frac{S_2 - S_1}{S_1 S_2}\right| \implies D = \frac{S_1 S_2}{|S_1 - S_2|} \cdot \Delta T$$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge · Differential Isolation in Speed-Change & Breakdown Problems

Consider a vehicle whose speed increases by $\Delta v_1$ saving time $\Delta t_1$, or decreases by $\Delta v_2$ losing time $\Delta t_2$ over the same distance $D$. Setting $D = v t$:

$$(v + \Delta v_1)(t - \Delta t_1) = vt \implies t\,\Delta v_1 - v\,\Delta t_1 = \Delta v_1 \Delta t_1$$ $$(v - \Delta v_2)(t + \Delta t_2) = vt \implies v\,\Delta t_2 - t\,\Delta v_2 = \Delta v_2 \Delta t_2$$

Notice that the quadratic $vt$ term cancels completely, leaving a clean linear system in $v$ and $t$! Similarly, in engine-breakdown problems where a fault occurs $x\text{ km}$ further down the track and reduces the total delay by $\Delta T$, the entire journey outside that $x\text{ km}$ window is identical in both scenarios. Isolating solely that $x\text{ km}$ strip yields $\frac{x}{v_{\text{slow}}} - \frac{x}{v_{\text{orig}}} = \Delta T$ instantaneously, giving $v_{\text{orig}}$ in one step without solving simultaneous equations for $D$.

3.2 Relative Motion: Trains, Crossing Geometry & After-Meeting Theorem

Relative velocity transforms a two-body moving problem into a single-body stationary problem by shifting the coordinate origin onto one of the moving objects:

  • Opposite directions (approaching or receding): $v_{\text{rel}} = v_1 + v_2$.
  • Same direction (chasing or overtaking): $v_{\text{rel}} = |v_1 - v_2|$.

Exact Crossing Distance Rules for Trains

A train of length $L_T$ completely crosses an object when its front engine aligns with the object's entry point and its last guard van clears the object's exit point. Thus, the effective distance covered is the sum of the two lengths:

  • Stationary point object (pole, standing man, signal post; length $\approx 0$): $t = \dfrac{L_T}{v_T}$.
  • Stationary extended object (platform, bridge, tunnel of length $L_P$): $t = \dfrac{L_T + L_P}{v_T}$.
  • Moving point object (running man, or a seated passenger inside another train): $t = \dfrac{L_T}{v_T \pm v_m}$. Classic BEST Trap: If Train A crosses a man sitting inside Train B, the length of Train B is completely irrelevant—only $L_A$ is crossed at relative speed $v_A \pm v_B$!
  • Two trains crossing each other (lengths $L_1, L_2$): $t = \dfrac{L_1 + L_2}{v_1 \pm v_2}$ ($+$ for opposite, $-$ for same direction).

The After-Meeting Theorem (First-Principles Derivation)

Suppose body $A$ starts from station $P$ toward $Q$ at constant speed $v_A$, and simultaneously body $B$ starts from $Q$ toward $P$ at constant speed $v_B$. They meet at an intermediate point $M$ after time $t$. After passing $M$, $A$ takes time $t_1$ to reach $Q$, and $B$ takes time $t_2$ to reach $P$.

Figure 3.1: Equal segment lengths $PM$ and $QM$ link pre-meeting time $t$ directly to post-meeting times $t_1$ and $t_2$.

Equating the two expressions for each segment in Figure 3.1:

$$PM = v_A t = v_B t_2 \implies \frac{v_A}{v_B} = \frac{t_2}{t} \qquad\text{and}\qquad QM = v_B t = v_A t_1 \implies \frac{v_A}{v_B} = \frac{t}{t_1}$$

Equating the right-hand sides gives $\dfrac{t_2}{t} = \dfrac{t}{t_1} \implies t^2 = t_1 t_2$, from which all three master identities follow immediately:

$$t_{\text{meet}} = \sqrt{t_1 t_2}, \qquad \frac{v_A}{v_B} = \sqrt{\frac{t_2}{t_1}}, \qquad \frac{T_A}{T_B} = \frac{t + t_1}{t + t_2} = \frac{v_B}{v_A} = \sqrt{\frac{t_1}{t_2}}$$

Validity Guardrail: This theorem holds only if both bodies start at the exact same instant. If $A$ starts $\Delta t$ hours earlier than $B$, first advance $A$ to its position at the instant $B$ starts, and apply the theorem only to the remaining simultaneous journey!

3.3 Boats & Streams

Let $u$ be the speed of the boat in still water and $v$ be the speed of the river current (with $u > v$ so the boat can make upstream progress).

  • Downstream speed (with current): $D_s = u + v$.
  • Upstream speed (against current): $U_s = u - v$.
  • Inversion formulas: Adding and subtracting the two equations yields: $$u = \frac{D_s + U_s}{2}, \qquad v = \frac{D_s - U_s}{2}$$
  • Round-Trip Time & Distance Formula: To row distance $d$ downstream and return $d$ upstream in total time $T$: $$T = \frac{d}{u + v} + \frac{d}{u - v} = \frac{d(u - v) + d(u + v)}{u^2 - v^2} = \frac{2du}{u^2 - v^2} \implies d = \frac{T(u^2 - v^2)}{2u}$$
  • Round-Trip Average Speed: $$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2d}{T} = \frac{u^2 - v^2}{u} = u - \frac{v^2}{u} < u$$ Counterintuitive Fact: A river current always slows down a round trip compared to still water, because the boat spends strictly more time fighting the current upstream than enjoying its boost downstream.
  • Time Difference Formula: If upstream takes $\Delta T$ hours longer than downstream over the same distance $d$: $$\Delta T = \frac{d}{u - v} - \frac{d}{u + v} = \frac{2dv}{u^2 - v^2} \implies d = \frac{\Delta T(u^2 - v^2)}{2v}$$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge · The Drifting-Object (River Frame) Invariant

Classic IOQM / NSEJS Trap: A rower loses his hat in a flowing river while rowing upstream. He notices the loss $t_0$ minutes later, immediately turns around, and retrieves the hat at a point $x\text{ km}$ downstream from where it fell. Find the speed of the river $v$.

Galilean Frame Solution: Switch to the reference frame moving with the river water. In this frame, the water is completely stationary, the hat does not move at all, and the rower moves away from the hat at speed $u$ for $t_0$ minutes, then returns to the hat at the exact same speed $u$. Therefore, the return time is also $t_0$ minutes—completely independent of $u$! Total elapsed time $= 2t_0$. During those $2t_0$ minutes, the hat simply drifted downstream with the current by distance $x$, so $v = \dfrac{x}{2t_0}$.

Critical Fraction-LCM Rule & Proof of Distinct Meeting Points

1. LCM of Fractions: Always reduce every fraction $\frac{x_i}{y_i}$ to lowest terms first, then apply: $$\text{LCM}\!\left(\frac{x_1}{y_1}, \frac{x_2}{y_2}, \dots\right) = \frac{\text{LCM}(x_1, x_2, \dots)}{\text{HCF}(y_1, y_2, \dots)}$$ Forgetting to reduce fractions first produces false common factors in the denominator and destroys the answer.

2. Why $|p \pm q|$ Distinct Points? Suppose $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{p}{q}$ in lowest terms. By the time both return to Start together, $A$ has completed exactly $p$ full laps and $B$ has completed $q$ full laps (their first meeting at Start happens after $T_{\text{start}} = \frac{pL}{a} = \frac{qL}{b}$). In the same direction, $A$ gains $p - q$ full laps on $B$ before returning to Start, creating $p - q$ meetings at fractions $\frac{k}{p-q}$ of the track ($k = 1, 2, \dots, p-q$)—all distinct because the cycle repeats verbatim after $T_{\text{start}}$. In opposite directions, their combined distance is $(p + q)L$, creating $p + q$ equally spaced meeting points.

Linear Races: Distance vs. Time Head-Starts

Decode standard race terminology strictly into distance–speed ratios over equal time intervals:

  • "$A$ beats $B$ by $x\text{ m}$ in a $L\text{-m}$ race": When $A$ runs $L$, $B$ runs $L - x$ in the exact same time $\implies \dfrac{v_A}{v_B} = \dfrac{L}{L - x}$.
  • "$A$ beats $B$ by $t$ seconds": $A$ reaches the finish $t$ seconds earlier, meaning $B$ needs $t$ more seconds to cover the remaining distance $x \implies v_B = \dfrac{x}{t}$.
  • "$A$ gives $B$ a start of $s\text{ m}$ and still beats $B$ by $x\text{ m}$": $B$ starts at the $s\text{-m}$ mark and ends $x\text{ m}$ short of $L$, so in the time $A$ covers $L$, $B$ covers $L - s - x \implies \dfrac{v_A}{v_B} = \dfrac{L}{L - s - x}$.
  • "Dead heat": Both runners cross the finish line simultaneously ($x = 0$). If $A$ gives $B$ a start of $s\text{ m}$ for a dead heat, $\dfrac{v_A}{v_B} = \dfrac{L}{L - s}$.
  • Three-Runner Transitivity Trap: If $A$ beats $B$ by $100\text{ m}$ and $B$ beats $C$ by $100\text{ m}$ in a $1000\text{ m}$ race, $A$ does not beat $C$ by $200\text{ m}$! Multiply the speed ratios: $$\frac{v_A}{v_C} = \frac{v_A}{v_B} \times \frac{v_B}{v_C} = \frac{1000}{900} \times \frac{1000}{900} = \frac{1000}{810} \implies A\text{ beats }C\text{ by }1000 - 810 = 190\text{ m}$$

3.5 Escalators: Moving-Frame Step Conservation

An escalator is simply a 1D river whose "distance" is measured in visible steps $N$ (the fixed number of steps visible on the escalator when it is switched off). Let $p$ be the person's walking speed (steps/second) and $e$ be the escalator's speed (steps/second).

Figure 3.3: Visible step conservation—steps walked by the person ($n = pt$) combine additively or subtractively with steps moved by the escalator ($et$).

The master conservation equation for any escalator trip taking time $t$ and $n$ physical steps walked by the person ($n = pt$) is:

$$N = n \pm e t = n\left(1 \pm \frac{e}{p}\right)$$

where $+$ applies when walking with the escalator (escalator helps you, so $n < N$) and $-$ applies when walking against the escalator (escalator pushes you back, so $n > N$).

Special Case · Harmonic Mean Shortcut (And Its Deadly Trap!)

If the same person walks at the EXACT SAME speed $p$ once with the escalator (taking $n_1$ steps) and once against it (taking $n_2$ steps), then:

$$\frac{N}{n_1} = 1 + \frac{e}{p} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{N}{n_2} = 1 - \frac{e}{p}$$

Adding both equations eliminates $\frac{e}{p}$ cleanly:

$$\frac{N}{n_1} + \frac{N}{n_2} = 2 \implies N = \frac{2 n_1 n_2}{n_1 + n_2}$$

DEADLY TRAP: If the person changes walking speed between trips (e.g., walks twice as fast on the second trip, $p_2 = 2p_1$), the harmonic mean formula FAILS! Always fall back to the universal conservation form $N = n_1 \pm e t_1 = n_2 \pm e t_2$ using the exact ratio $t_1 : t_2 = \frac{n_1}{p_1} : \frac{n_2}{p_2}$.

3.6 Time & Work: LCM Efficiency Method, MDH Chain Rule & Mid-Way Shifts

Fraction-based work equations ($\frac{1}{12} + \frac{1}{15} - \frac{1}{20}$) invite arithmetic errors under exam pressure. Instead, standardize every Time & Work problem into integer units using the LCM Efficiency Method:

  1. Assign Total Work: Set $W = \text{LCM}(\text{all given solo completion times})$.
  2. Compute Integer Efficiencies: Each worker's daily output is $\eta_i = \dfrac{W}{T_i}$ units/day.
  3. Combine Additively: Efficiencies add directly: $\eta_{A+B} = \eta_A + \eta_B$, so $T_{A+B} = \dfrac{W}{\eta_A + \eta_B}$.

Pairwise Cyclic Groups ($A+B$, $B+C$, $C+A$)

When solo times aren't given, but paired times $T_{AB}, T_{BC}, T_{CA}$ are known, set $W = \text{LCM}(T_{AB}, T_{BC}, T_{CA})$. Adding the three pair efficiencies counts each worker twice:

$$\eta_{A+B} + \eta_{B+C} + \eta_{C+A} = 2(\eta_A + \eta_B + \eta_C) \implies \eta_{A+B+C} = \frac{\eta_{A+B} + \eta_{B+C} + \eta_{C+A}}{2}$$

Individual efficiencies then follow by subtraction: $\eta_A = \eta_{A+B+C} - \eta_{B+C}$, and so forth.

The MDH Chain Rule (Compound Proportionality)

When $M$ identical workers of individual efficiency $E$ work $D$ days at $H$ hours/day to accomplish $W$ units of work, total man-hour-efficiency effort is directly proportional to work done:

$$\frac{M_1 D_1 H_1 E_1}{W_1} = \frac{M_2 D_2 H_2 E_2}{W_2}$$

Mini-Illustration (Man-Woman-Child Conversion): If $3\text{ men}$ or $5\text{ women}$ can finish a job in $16\text{ days}$, then $3\eta_M = 5\eta_W \implies \eta_M : \eta_W = 5 : 3$. Setting $\eta_M = 5$ and $\eta_W = 3$, total work $W = 3(5)(16) = 240\text{ units}$. Thus $6\text{ men}$ and $5\text{ women}$ have combined rate $6(5) + 5(3) = 45\text{ u/day}$ and finish in $\frac{240}{45} = 5\frac{1}{3}\text{ days}$.

Joining & Leaving Mid-Way: The Phantom-Work Technique

  • Case A (Leaves $k$ days AFTER start): We know the exact duration $k$ that worker $A$ worked. Simply subtract $A$'s output ($k \cdot \eta_A$) from total work $W$ and let the remaining workers handle $W - k\eta_A$.
  • Case B (Leaves $k$ days BEFORE completion): Let total project duration be $T$. Since $A$ works $T - k$ days and $B$ works $T$ days: $$(T - k)\eta_A + T\eta_B = W \implies T(\eta_A + \eta_B) = W + k\eta_A \implies T = \frac{W + k\eta_A}{\eta_A + \eta_B}$$ Intuition: Do not let $A$ leave! Force $A$ to stay for those final $k$ days—that adds $k\eta_A$ "phantom units" to the workload, allowing everyone to work from Day 1 to Day $T$ together!
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge · The $\sqrt{ab}$ Time-Work Isomorphism

If $A$ alone takes $a$ days more than $(A + B)$ working together, and $B$ alone takes $b$ days more than $(A + B)$ working together, let $T = T_{A+B}$. Then $T_A = T + a$ and $T_B = T + b$. Using $\frac{1}{T_A} + \frac{1}{T_B} = \frac{1}{T}$:

$$\frac{1}{T + a} + \frac{1}{T + b} = \frac{1}{T} \implies \frac{2T + a + b}{T^2 + (a + b)T + ab} = \frac{1}{T}$$ $$2T^2 + (a + b)T = T^2 + (a + b)T + ab \implies T^2 = ab \implies T_{A+B} = \sqrt{ab}$$

Observe the deep mathematical unity: this is algebraically identical to the TSD After-Meeting Theorem ($t_{\text{meet}} = \sqrt{t_1 t_2}$)! Moreover, $A$'s efficiency to $B$'s efficiency is $\frac{\eta_A}{\eta_B} = \frac{T + b}{T + a} = \frac{\sqrt{ab} + b}{\sqrt{ab} + a} = \frac{\sqrt{b}(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})}{\sqrt{a}(\sqrt{b} + \sqrt{a})} = \sqrt{\frac{b}{a}}$.

3.7 Alternating Days & Negative Work / Pipes & Cisterns

In Pipes & Cisterns, an inlet pipe performs positive work ($+\eta$) while an outlet/leak pipe performs negative work ($-\eta$). When workers or pipes operate on alternating intervals, group one complete sequence of turns into a single Cycle.

1. All-Positive Alternating Work: Suppose $A$ (rate $\eta_A$) and $B$ (rate $\eta_B$) work on alternate days starting with $A$. One 2-day cycle produces $w_c = \eta_A + \eta_B$. Compute the maximum number of full cycles $k = \lfloor W / w_c \rfloor$, consuming $2k$ days and leaving remainder $R = W - k w_c$. Simulate the remaining $R$ units strictly day-by-day in the exact turn order ($A$, then $B$).

2. Negative-Work Alternating Cycles (The Critical Last-Cycle Trap!): Suppose an inlet pipe $A$ fills $+a$ units in Hour 1, and an outlet pipe $B$ drains $-b$ units in Hour 2 (with $a > b$, net cycle gain $g = a - b$ per 2 hours). Why does dividing $W$ by $g$ fail catastrophically? Because once the tank hits full capacity $W$ during an inlet stroke, the process terminates immediately—the outlet pipe never gets to drain it back down!

Figure 3.4: Sawtooth trajectory of alternating fill ($+a$) and drain ($-b$). Full cycles stop as soon as the trough reaches or exceeds $W - a$; the very next fill stroke hits $W$.
Exact Algorithm · Solving Negative-Work Alternating Cycles

Given capacity $W$, positive fill stroke $+a$ (1 hr), and negative drain stroke $-b$ (1 hr) with $a > b$ and $W > a$:

  1. Compute the Safety Ceiling: $W_{\text{safe}} = W - a$.
  2. Find Full Cycles via Ceiling Function: Calculate the smallest integer $k$ such that after $k$ complete 2-hour cycles, the water level is at least $W - a$: $$k = \left\lceil \frac{W - a}{a - b} \right\rceil$$ Why Ceiling ($\lceil \cdot \rceil$) and NOT Floor? If you used floor when $(W - a)$ isn't divisible by $(a - b)$, the level after $k$ cycles would be strictly less than $W - a$; the next $+a$ stroke would still fall short of $W$, forcing an extra $-b$ drop anyway!
  3. Compute Final Partial Fill Time: After $2k$ hours, the tank holds $k(a - b)$ units. The remaining deficit $R = W - k(a - b)$ satisfies $0 < R \le a$, which Pipe $A$ fills in $\dfrac{R}{a}$ hours: $$T_{\text{total}} = 2k + \frac{W - k(a - b)}{a}$$

3.8 Work & Wages: The Work-Share Axiom

Wages obey one inviolable principle: Money is ALWAYS divided strictly in the ratio of actual work done, never by time spent alone.

$$\text{Wage}_i = \left(\frac{W_i}{W_{\text{total}}}\right) \times \text{Total Contract Payment} = \left(\frac{\eta_i \cdot t_i}{W_{\text{total}}}\right) \times \text{Total Contract Payment}$$
  • Uniform Duration Case ($t_1 = t_2 = \dots = t_n$): When all workers work together from start to finish without leaving, $t_i$ cancels out, so $\text{Wage}_1 : \text{Wage}_2 : \dots = \eta_1 : \eta_2 : \dots = \frac{1}{T_1} : \frac{1}{T_2} : \dots$.
  • Variable Duration Case ($t_1 \ne t_2$): Always compute each worker's individual units $W_i = \eta_i t_i$ (or fractional share $\frac{t_i}{T_i}$). Even a slower worker earns more if they stay on the job longer and complete more units!
  • Helper / Assistant Share: If $A$ and $B$ (solo times $T_A, T_B$) hire a helper $C$ and finish the entire job together in $T$ days, $A$'s work fraction is $\frac{T}{T_A}$, $B$'s is $\frac{T}{T_B}$, and $C$'s share is simply the residual $1 - \frac{T}{T_A} - \frac{T}{T_B}$.

  • Boats & Streams: $D_s = u + v$, $U_s = u - v$; $u = \frac{D_s+U_s}{2}$, $v = \frac{D_s-U_s}{2}$; Round trip $d = \frac{T(u^2-v^2)}{2u}$, $v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{u^2-v^2}{u}$.
  • Circular Track ($L$, $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{p}{q}$ reduced): First meet $= \frac{L}{a \mp b}$; At start $= \text{LCM}\!\left(\frac{L}{a}, \frac{L}{b}\right)$; Distinct points $= |p \mp q|$.
  • Escalators: $N = n \pm et = n\!\left(1 \pm \frac{e}{p}\right)$; Same walking speed with & against $\Rightarrow N = \frac{2n_1 n_2}{n_1 + n_2}$.
  • Time & Work: $W = \text{LCM}(T_i)$, $\eta_i = \frac{W}{T_i}$; $\frac{M_1 D_1 H_1 E_1}{W_1} = \frac{M_2 D_2 H_2 E_2}{W_2}$; $T_{A+B} = \sqrt{ab}$ (extra-days theorem).
  • Negative Alternating Cycles: $k = \left\lceil \frac{W - a}{a - b} \right\rceil$,   $T = 2k + \frac{W - k(a - b)}{a}$.
  • Work & Wages: $\text{Wage}_i \propto W_i = \eta_i t_i$ (proportional to efficiency only when $t_i$ is identical for all).

LEVEL 1 · WARMUP & FOUNDATION CALIBRATION (EXAMPLES 1–3)
Level 1 Average Speed & Stoppages Worked Example 1: Round-Trip Harmonic Mean & Idle Stoppage Rate

An intercity bus travels from Bengaluru to Mysuru ($150\text{ km}$) at a uniform speed of $60\text{ km/h}$ without stoppages, and returns along the same route at $40\text{ km/h}$ without stoppages. (a) Find its average speed for the entire round trip. (b) On another day, due to intermediate passenger stops, the bus's average speed on the onward $150\text{ km}$ journey drops from $60\text{ km/h}$ to $48\text{ km/h}$. How many minutes per hour does the bus stop on average?

Part (a): Average Speed for the Round Trip

From first principles, compute the time for each leg separately:

$$t_{\text{onward}} = \frac{150}{60} = 2.5\text{ hours}, \qquad t_{\text{return}} = \frac{150}{40} = 3.75\text{ hours}$$

Total distance $= 150 + 150 = 300\text{ km}$; total time $= 2.5 + 3.75 = 6.25\text{ hours}$. Hence:

$$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{300}{6.25} = \frac{300}{25/4} = 48\text{ km/h}$$

Harmonic Mean Verification: Since both legs have equal distance ($150\text{ km}$), $v_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{2(60)(40)}{60 + 40} = \dfrac{4800}{100} = 48\text{ km/h}$. Notice this is strictly less than the arithmetic mean $\frac{60+40}{2} = 50\text{ km/h}$.

Part (b): Stoppage Time per Hour

Including stoppages, the bus covers $48\text{ km}$ in $1\text{ clock hour}$ ($60\text{ minutes}$). At its actual running speed of $60\text{ km/h}$, the time required to cover $48\text{ km}$ is:

$$t_{\text{moving}} = \frac{48\text{ km}}{60\text{ km/h}} = 0.8\text{ hours} = 48\text{ minutes}$$

Therefore, in every $60\text{ minutes}$ of elapsed clock time, the bus stands still for:

$$t_{\text{stop}} = 60 - 48 = \mathbf{12\text{ minutes per hour}}$$

Formula Check: $\dfrac{S_{\text{run}} - S_{\text{stop}}}{S_{\text{run}}} \times 60 = \dfrac{60 - 48}{60} \times 60 = 12\text{ min/hr}$.

Level 1 Boats & Streams Worked Example 2: Round-Trip River Distance & Effective Speed

A motorboat travels at $12\text{ km/h}$ in still water. In a river flowing at $3\text{ km/h}$, the boat takes a total of $4\text{ hours}$ to travel downstream to a dock and immediately return to its starting jetty. Find (a) the distance between the jetty and the dock, and (b) the boat's average speed for the round trip.

Step 1: Determine Downstream and Upstream Speeds

Given $u = 12\text{ km/h}$ and $v = 3\text{ km/h}$:

$$D_s = u + v = 12 + 3 = 15\text{ km/h}, \qquad U_s = u - v = 12 - 3 = 9\text{ km/h}$$

Step 2: Solve for Distance $d$

Total round-trip time equals the sum of downstream time and upstream time:

$$\frac{d}{15} + \frac{d}{9} = 4 \implies \frac{3d + 5d}{45} = 4 \implies \frac{8d}{45} = 4 \implies d = \frac{180}{8} = \mathbf{22.5\text{ km}}$$

Sanity Check: Downstream time $= \frac{22.5}{15} = 1.5\text{ h}$; upstream time $= \frac{22.5}{9} = 2.5\text{ h}$; sum $= 4.0\text{ h}$.

Step 3: Round-Trip Average Speed

$$v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}} = \frac{2 \times 22.5}{4} = \frac{45}{4} = \mathbf{11.25\text{ km/h}}$$

Formula Check: $v_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{u^2 - v^2}{u} = \dfrac{144 - 9}{12} = \dfrac{135}{12} = 11.25\text{ km/h}$ (strictly slower than still-water speed $12\text{ km/h}$).

Level 1 Time & Work · Wages Worked Example 3: LCM Efficiency Method & Helper Wage Allocation

Ananya can complete a design project in $12\text{ days}$ and Bhavesh can complete it in $15\text{ days}$. With the assistance of Charan, all three finish the entire project working together in $5\text{ days}$. If the total contract fee is ₹$3,600$, find Charan's solo completion time and each person's exact wage share.

Step 1: Assign Total Work via LCM

Let Total Work $W = \text{LCM}(12, 15, 5) = 60\text{ units}$.

Step 2: Compute Individual Daily Efficiencies

$$\eta_A = \frac{60}{12} = 5\text{ units/day}, \qquad \eta_B = \frac{60}{15} = 4\text{ units/day}, \qquad \eta_{A+B+C} = \frac{60}{5} = 12\text{ units/day}$$

Subtracting $\eta_A + \eta_B$ from the combined efficiency gives Charan's rate:

$$\eta_C = 12 - (5 + 4) = 3\text{ units/day}$$

Therefore, Charan working alone would take:

$$T_C = \frac{W}{\eta_C} = \frac{60}{3} = \mathbf{20\text{ days}}$$

Step 3: Distribute Wages Strictly by Work Done

Over the $5\text{ days}$, actual units completed are $W_A = 5 \times 5 = 25$, $W_B = 4 \times 5 = 20$, and $W_C = 3 \times 5 = 15$. Because all three worked for the exact same duration, the work ratio reduces directly to the efficiency ratio $5 : 4 : 3$ (sum $= 12$ parts):

$$\text{Value of } 1\text{ part} = \frac{₹3,600}{12} = ₹300$$
  • Ananya's share: $5 \times ₹300 = \mathbf{₹1,500}$
  • Bhavesh's share: $4 \times ₹300 = \mathbf{₹1,200}$
  • Charan's share: $3 \times ₹300 = \mathbf{₹900}$
LEVEL 2 · BEST EXAM-REAL APPLICATION (EXAMPLES 4–6)
Level 2 Relative Motion · Trains Worked Example 4: After-Meeting Theorem Coupled with Train Crossing Geometry

Two express trains $T_1$ (length $180\text{ m}$) and $T_2$ (length $220\text{ m}$) depart simultaneously from stations $P$ and $Q$ toward each other on parallel tracks. After their engines pass each other at point $M$, $T_1$ takes $4\text{ hours}$ to reach $Q$ while $T_2$ takes $9\text{ hours}$ to reach $P$. If the speed of $T_1$ is $72\text{ km/h}$, find: (a) the speed of $T_2$, (b) the distance between stations $P$ and $Q$, and (c) the time in seconds taken by the two trains to completely clear each other from the instant their engines meet.

Part (a): Speed of $T_2$ via the After-Meeting Theorem

Both trains start simultaneously, with post-meeting times $t_1 = 4\text{ h}$ (for $T_1$) and $t_2 = 9\text{ h}$ (for $T_2$). Applying the theorem:

$$\frac{v_1}{v_2} = \sqrt{\frac{t_2}{t_1}} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{4}} = \frac{3}{2}$$

Substituting $v_1 = 72\text{ km/h}$:

$$v_2 = 72 \times \frac{2}{3} = \mathbf{48\text{ km/h}}$$

Part (b): Distance Between Stations $P$ and $Q$

First compute the time elapsed before the engines meet at $M$:

$$t_{\text{meet}} = \sqrt{t_1 t_2} = \sqrt{4 \times 9} = 6\text{ hours}$$

Total journey time for $T_1$ from $P$ to $Q$ is $T_1 = t_{\text{meet}} + t_1 = 6 + 4 = 10\text{ hours}$. Therefore:

$$PQ = v_1 \times T_1 = 72 \times 10 = \mathbf{720\text{ km}}$$

Cross-Check via $T_2$: $T_2 = 6 + 9 = 15\text{ h} \implies 48 \times 15 = 720\text{ km}$. Cross-Check via Relative Speed: $(72 + 48) \times 6 = 720\text{ km}$.

Part (c): Complete Crossing Time

Because the trains move in opposite directions, their relative speed is $v_{\text{rel}} = 72 + 48 = 120\text{ km/h}$. Convert to $\text{m/s}$:

$$v_{\text{rel}} = 120 \times \frac{5}{18} = \frac{100}{3}\text{ m/s}$$

Total distance to clear completely is $L_1 + L_2 = 180 + 220 = 400\text{ m}$. Hence:

$$t_{\text{cross}} = \frac{400}{100/3} = \mathbf{12\text{ seconds}}$$
Level 2 Circular Tracks · 3 Runners Worked Example 5: Three-Runner Meeting Times & Distinct Meeting Nodes

Three athletes $A$, $B$, and $C$ start simultaneously from the same starting line on a $1200\text{ m}$ circular track. $A$ and $B$ run clockwise at $15\text{ m/s}$ and $9\text{ m/s}$ respectively, while $C$ runs anticlockwise at $3\text{ m/s}$. Find: (a) when all three will meet for the first time anywhere on the track, (b) when all three will meet for the first time at the starting point, and (c) the number of distinct points on the track where $A$ and $C$ meet.

Part (a): First Meeting of All Three Anywhere on the Track

Pair $A$ with $B$ (same direction) and $A$ with $C$ (opposite direction). If $A$ coincides with $B$ and simultaneously coincides with $C$, then all three coincide at the exact same instant and position:

$$t_{AB} = \frac{1200}{15 - 9} = \frac{1200}{6} = 200\text{ s}, \qquad t_{AC} = \frac{1200}{15 + 3} = \frac{1200}{18} = \frac{200}{3}\text{ s}$$

Take the LCM of these reduced intervals:

$$t_{\text{all}} = \text{LCM}\!\left(\frac{200}{1},\, \frac{200}{3}\right) = \frac{\text{LCM}(200, 200)}{\text{HCF}(1, 3)} = \mathbf{200\text{ seconds}}$$

Position Verification at $t = 200\text{ s}$: $A$ covers $15(200) = 3000\text{ m} = 2(1200) + 600\text{ m}$ CW; $B$ covers $9(200) = 1800\text{ m} = 1(1200) + 600\text{ m}$ CW; $C$ covers $3(200) = 600\text{ m}$ ACW (which is identical to $600\text{ m}$ CW!). All three meet at the point diametrically opposite the start—proving that meeting anywhere can occur strictly earlier than meeting at the start.

Part (b): First Meeting of All Three at the Starting Point

Compute each athlete's single-lap period in reduced fractional form:

$$T_A = \frac{1200}{15} = 80\text{ s}, \qquad T_B = \frac{1200}{9} = \frac{400}{3}\text{ s}, \qquad T_C = \frac{1200}{3} = 400\text{ s}$$

Take the LCM of all three periods:

$$T_{\text{start}} = \text{LCM}\!\left(\frac{80}{1},\, \frac{400}{3},\, \frac{400}{1}\right) = \frac{\text{LCM}(80, 400, 400)}{\text{HCF}(1, 3, 1)} = \mathbf{400\text{ seconds}}$$

Part (c): Distinct Meeting Points of $A$ and $C$

Reduce their speed ratio to lowest terms: $\dfrac{v_A}{v_C} = \dfrac{15}{3} = \dfrac{5}{1}$ (so $p = 5, q = 1$). Because they run in opposite directions, the number of distinct meeting points is:

$$p + q = 5 + 1 = \mathbf{6\text{ distinct points}}$$
Level 2 Pipes & Cisterns · Negative Work Worked Example 6: Alternating Inlet–Outlet Cycle & The Non-Divisible Ceiling Trap

Inlet Pipe $A$ can fill an empty reservoir in $12\text{ hours}$, while outlet Pipe $B$ can completely drain the full reservoir in $20\text{ hours}$. Starting with an empty reservoir, the pipes are opened alternately for $1\text{ hour}$ each, beginning with Pipe $A$ in Hour 1, Pipe $B$ in Hour 2, Pipe $A$ in Hour 3, and so on. In how many hours will the reservoir be completely filled for the first time?

Step 1: Set Capacity and Hourly Rates via LCM

Let reservoir capacity $W = \text{LCM}(12, 20) = 60\text{ units}$. Then:

$$\eta_A = +\frac{60}{12} = +5\text{ units/hr}, \qquad \eta_B = -\frac{60}{20} = -3\text{ units/hr}$$

Net gain per complete $2\text{-hour}$ cycle ($A$ followed by $B$): $g = 5 - 3 = +2\text{ units}$.

Step 2: Apply the Safety-Ceiling Method (Avoid the 60-Hour Naive Trap!)

Reserve Pipe $A$'s final positive jump ($a = 5\text{ units}$). The safety ceiling is:

$$W_{\text{safe}} = W - a = 60 - 5 = 55\text{ units}$$

Compute the exact number of complete $2\text{-hour}$ cycles needed to reach or exceed $55\text{ units}$:

$$k = \left\lceil \frac{W - a}{a - b} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{55}{2} \right\rceil = \lceil 27.5 \rceil = 28\text{ full cycles}$$

Why $27$ Cycles Fails: After $27$ cycles ($54\text{ hours}$), volume $= 27 \times 2 = 54\text{ units}$. In Hour $55$, Pipe $A$ adds $+5$, bringing it to $59\text{ units}$—still $1\text{ unit}$ short of full! Thus Pipe $B$ must run in Hour $56$, dropping the level back to $59 - 3 = 56\text{ units}$ (which is exactly $28 \times 2 = 56\text{ units}$ at the end of Cycle $28$).

Step 3: Finish the Final Stroke

At $t = 56\text{ hours}$ ($28$ cycles), remaining empty volume $= 60 - 56 = 4\text{ units}$. In Hour $57$, Pipe $A$ fills these $4\text{ units}$ at $+5\text{ units/hr}$ in $\frac{4}{5}\text{ hour} = 48\text{ minutes}$. Therefore:

$$T_{\text{total}} = 56 + \frac{4}{5} = \mathbf{56\text{ hours } 48\text{ minutes}}\quad\left(56.8\text{ hours}\right)$$
LEVEL 3 · RANK-BOOSTER & OLYMPIAD SYNTHESIS (EXAMPLES 7–9)
Level 3 Speed-Change · Breakdown Worked Example 7: Engine Breakdown Delay Differential

A freight train departs Station $X$ for Station $Y$ at its normal cruising speed. After traveling $150\text{ km}$, its engine develops a fault, forcing it to reduce its speed to $\frac{3}{5}$ of its original speed, and it reaches Station $Y$ $8\text{ hours}$ late. Had the engine fault occurred $180\text{ km}$ further along the route, the train would have reached Station $Y$ only $5\text{ hours}$ late. Find the original speed of the train and the total distance between Stations $X$ and $Y$.

Method 1: Rigorous First-Principles Algebraic Derivation

Let the original speed be $v\text{ km/h}$ and total distance be $D\text{ km}$. The reduced speed is $\frac{3}{5}v\text{ km/h}$.

In Scenario 1 (fault at $150\text{ km}$), the first $150\text{ km}$ incurs zero delay; the entire $8\text{-hour}$ delay is accumulated over the remaining distance $(D - 150)\text{ km}$:

$$\frac{D - 150}{\frac{3}{5}v} - \frac{D - 150}{v} = 8 \implies \frac{D - 150}{v}\left(\frac{5}{3} - 1\right) = 8 \implies \frac{2(D - 150)}{3v} = 8 \qquad \text{(1)}$$

In Scenario 2 (fault at $150 + 180 = 330\text{ km}$), the $5\text{-hour}$ delay is accumulated over the remaining $(D - 330)\text{ km}$:

$$\frac{2(D - 330)}{3v} = 5 \qquad \text{(2)}$$

Subtract equation (2) from equation (1) to eliminate $D$:

$$\frac{2\big[(D - 150) - (D - 330)\big]}{3v} = 8 - 5 \implies \frac{2(180)}{3v} = 3 \implies \frac{120}{v} = 3 \implies v = \mathbf{40\text{ km/h}}$$

Substitute $v = 40$ back into equation (1):

$$\frac{2(D - 150)}{3 \times 40} = 8 \implies D - 150 = \frac{8 \times 120}{2} = 480 \implies D = 150 + 480 = \mathbf{630\text{ km}}$$
30-Second Exam Shortcut · Differential Segment Isolation

Compare the two journeys side-by-side: before $150\text{ km}$ both run at $v$; after $330\text{ km}$ both run at $\frac{3}{5}v$. The only difference is the middle $180\text{ km}$ stretch, which saves $8 - 5 = 3\text{ hours}$ when covered at normal speed.

1. On that $180\text{ km}$ stretch, Speed ratio $= 3 : 5 \implies$ Time ratio $= 5 : 3$. Difference $= 2\text{ parts} = 3\text{ h} \implies 1\text{ part} = 1.5\text{ h}$. Normal time for $180\text{ km} = 3\text{ parts} = 4.5\text{ h} \implies v = \frac{180}{4.5} = \mathbf{40\text{ km/h}}$.

2. Slow travel causes $3\text{ h}$ delay per $180\text{ km}$ ($= 1\text{ h}$ delay per $60\text{ km}$). Since Scenario 1 had $8\text{ h}$ total delay, its slow-travel stretch must be $8 \times 60 = 480\text{ km}$. Thus $D = 150 + 480 = \mathbf{630\text{ km}}$.

Level 3 Escalators · Speed Change Worked Example 8: Escalator Reversal with Doubled Walking Speed

Aarav walks up a moving upward escalator at a steady pace and reaches the top after taking $40$ steps. On his next trip, he doubles his walking speed (steps per second) and walks down the same upward-moving escalator (against its motion), taking $120$ steps to reach the bottom. Assuming the escalator maintains a constant speed throughout, how many visible steps does the escalator have when stationary?

Method 1: Rigorous First-Principles Step-Conservation Derivation

Let $N$ be the number of visible steps, $e$ be the upward escalator speed (steps/s), and $p$ be Aarav's initial walking speed (steps/s).

  • Trip 1 (Walking UP, with escalator at speed $p$): Time taken is $t_1 = \dfrac{40}{p}$. During $t_1$, the escalator carries him upward by $e t_1 = 40\left(\dfrac{e}{p}\right)$ steps. Setting $r = \dfrac{e}{p}$: $$N = 40 + 40r \qquad \text{(1)}$$
  • Trip 2 (Walking DOWN, against escalator at speed $2p$): Time taken is $t_2 = \dfrac{120}{2p} = \dfrac{60}{p}$. During $t_2$, the upward-moving escalator pushes him back by $e t_2 = 60\left(\dfrac{e}{p}\right) = 60r$ steps. Since he makes net downward progress of $N$ steps: $$N = 120 - 60r \qquad \text{(2)}$$

Eliminate $r$ by taking $3 \times \text{(1)} + 2 \times \text{(2)}$:

$$3N + 2N = (120 + 120r) + (240 - 120r) \implies 5N = 360 \implies N = \mathbf{72\text{ visible steps}}$$

Physical Consistency Check: From (1), $r = \frac{72 - 40}{40} = 0.8 \implies e = 0.8p$. Since $2p = 2p > 0.8p = e$, Aarav is indeed faster than the escalator on Trip 2 and successfully reaches the bottom! Warning: Blindly plugging into the equal-speed harmonic mean formula $\frac{2(40)(120)}{40+120}$ yields the wrong trap answer $60$.

30-Second Exam Shortcut · Time-Ratio Escalator Scaling

1. Write the ratio of trip durations directly from $(\text{steps} \div \text{relative walking rate})$: $$t_1 : t_2 = \frac{40}{1} : \frac{120}{2} = 40 : 60 = 2 : 3$$

2. Since the escalator runs at constant speed, its step contributions in the two trips are strictly in the time ratio $2x$ (helping) and $-3x$ (opposing):

$$N = 40 + 2x = 120 - 3x \implies 5x = 80 \implies x = 16 \implies N = 40 + 2(16) = \mathbf{72\text{ steps}}$$
Level 3 Work & Wages · Mid-Way Shifts Worked Example 9: Early Departure, Pre-Completion Exit & Exact Wage Split

Three contractors $A$, $B$, and $C$ can individually lay a fiber-optic network in $24$, $30$, and $40\text{ days}$ respectively. All three begin working together on a contract worth ₹$18,000$. However, $A$ leaves $4\text{ days}$ after the start, and $B$ leaves $3\text{ days}$ before the project is completed, while $C$ stays until the end. Find: (a) the total number of days required to finish the project, and (b) the exact payment earned by $B$.

Method 1: Rigorous First-Principles LCM Equation

Set Total Work $W = \text{LCM}(24, 30, 40) = 120\text{ units}$. Individual daily efficiencies are:

$$\eta_A = \frac{120}{24} = 5\text{ u/day}, \qquad \eta_B = \frac{120}{30} = 4\text{ u/day}, \qquad \eta_C = \frac{120}{40} = 3\text{ u/day}$$

Let $T$ be the total number of days taken to complete the project. Track each contractor's exact active duration and output:

  • $A$ works for $4\text{ days}$ $\implies W_A = 4 \times 5 = 20\text{ units}$.
  • $B$ works for $(T - 3)\text{ days}$ $\implies W_B = 4(T - 3)\text{ units}$.
  • $C$ works for $T\text{ days}$ $\implies W_C = 3T\text{ units}$.

Since total work completed equals $120\text{ units}$:

$$20 + 4(T - 3) + 3T = 120 \implies 7T + 8 = 120 \implies 7T = 112 \implies T = \mathbf{16\text{ days}}$$

Now verify work units for wage distribution: $W_A = 20$, $W_B = 4(16 - 3) = 52$, and $W_C = 3(16) = 48$ (check: $20 + 52 + 48 = 120$). Since wages are proportional strictly to work done (not solo rates!):

$$\text{Payment per unit of work} = \frac{₹18,000}{120\text{ units}} = ₹150/\text{unit}$$ $$\text{B's Payment} = 52 \times ₹150 = \mathbf{₹7,800}$$

Complete Split Verification: $A = 20 \times 150 = ₹3,000$; $B = ₹7,800$; $C = 48 \times 150 = ₹7,200$; Total $= ₹18,000$.

30-Second Exam Shortcut · "Kick Out Early Leavers, Drag In Late Leavers"

1. Kick out $A$ (leaves after 4 days): Remove $A$'s $4 \times 5 = 20\text{ units}$ from the $120\text{-unit}$ pool $\implies 100\text{ units}$ left for $B$ and $C$.

2. Drag in $B$ (leaves 3 days before end): Force $B$ to stay those last $3\text{ days}$ by adding $3 \times 4 = 12$ phantom units $\implies 100 + 12 = 112\text{ units}$.

3. Divide adjusted work by $(B + C)$'s combined rate: $T = \dfrac{112}{4 + 3} = \mathbf{16\text{ days}}$.

4. Multiply $B$'s actual units $(13 \times 4 = 52)$ by the unit wage $(₹18,000 / 120 = ₹150) \implies \mathbf{₹7,800}$.
