Q4 Level 1 Correct Answer: (B)

Given parametric system: $ax + by = a^2 + b^2$ and $bx - ay = 0$ with $a^2 + b^2 \neq 0$.

Apply $2 \times 2$ Cramer’s Rule directly:

$$\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ b & -a \end{vmatrix} = -a^2 - b^2 = -(a^2 + b^2) \neq 0$$ $$\Delta_x = \begin{vmatrix} a^2 + b^2 & b \\ 0 & -a \end{vmatrix} = -a(a^2 + b^2) - 0 = -a(a^2 + b^2)$$ $$\Delta_y = \begin{vmatrix} a & a^2 + b^2 \\ b & 0 \end{vmatrix} = 0 - b(a^2 + b^2) = -b(a^2 + b^2)$$

Therefore:

$$x = \frac{\Delta_x}{\Delta} = \frac{-a(a^2 + b^2)}{-(a^2 + b^2)} = a, \qquad y = \frac{\Delta_y}{\Delta} = \frac{-b(a^2 + b^2)}{-(a^2 + b^2)} = b$$

Thus $(x, y) = (a, b)$. Hence (B).

Q20 Level 3 Correct Answer: (C)

Given system:

$$(k^2 - 1)x + (k - 1)y = k - 1 \qquad \text{(1)}$$ $$(k + 1)x + y = k^2 - 5k + 7 \qquad \text{(2)}$$

Step 1 — Check Determinant $\Delta$:

$$\Delta = (k^2 - 1)(1) - (k + 1)(k - 1) = (k^2 - 1) - (k^2 - 1) = 0 \quad \text{for ALL } k \in \mathbb{R}$$

Because $\Delta \equiv 0$ identically, for every real $k$ the system has either no solution or infinitely many solutions. We split strictly on whether the common factor $(k - 1)$ in Eq (1) vanishes!

  • Eq (1) becomes $(1 - 1)x + (1 - 1)y = 1 - 1 \implies 0x + 0y = 0$, which is satisfied by every point $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$.
  • Eq (2) becomes $2x + y = 1 - 5 + 7 = 3$, which is a valid straight line containing infinitely many points.

Hence the combined solution set at $k = 1$ is the entire line $2x + y = 3$, giving infinitely many solutions! Thus $k = 1$ MUST be included.

Case 2 — Non-Degenerate Case $k \neq 1$:

Since $k - 1 \neq 0$, we may legally divide Eq (1) by $(k - 1)$:

$$(k + 1)x + y = 1$$

$$k^2 - 5k + 7 = 1 \implies k^2 - 5k + 6 = 0 \implies (k - 2)(k - 3) = 0 \implies k = 2 \text{ or } k = 3$$

    $$k(3) + 2(2) = 22 \implies 3k + 4 = 22 \implies 3k = 18 \implies k = 6$$

The three distinct real values are $k \in \{-1, 4, 6\}$, whose sum is $-1 + 4 + 6 = 9$. Hence (D) $9$.

Q24 Level 3 • Passage 2 Correct Answer: (C)

Given Diophantine line: $5x + 12y = 426$ with $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ ($x \ge 1,\ y \ge 1$). Note $\gcd(5, 12) = 1$.

Step 1 — Reduce Modulo $5$ to Find $P_{\text{first}}$:

$$12y \equiv 426 \pmod{5} \implies 2y \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$$

Multiplying by $3$ (since $3 \times 2 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}$): $y \equiv 3 \pmod{5}$. The smallest positive value is $y_0 = 3$, giving:

$$5x_0 = 426 - 12(3) = 426 - 36 = 390 \implies x_0 = 78$$

Thus the point with the largest $x$-coordinate is $P_{\text{first}} = (78, 3)$.

Step 2 — Parametrize & Bound $t$:

$$x = 78 - 12t, \qquad y = 3 + 5t \qquad (t \in \mathbb{Z})$$
  • $y \ge 1 \implies 3 + 5t \ge 1 \implies t \ge -\frac{2}{5} \implies t \ge 0$.
  • $x \ge 1 \implies 78 - 12t \ge 1 \implies 12t \le 77 \implies t \le \dfrac{77}{12} = 6.416\ldots \implies t \le 6$.

Thus $t \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$, giving $6 - 0 + 1 = 7$ positive integer lattice points. Hence (C) $7$.

Q25 Level 3 • Passage 2 Correct Answer: (B)

From Q24, the extreme lattice points correspond to $t = 0$ and $t = 6$:

  • $P_{\text{first}} = (78, 3)$ (at $t = 0$).
  • $P_{\text{last}} = (78 - 12(6),\; 3 + 5(6)) = (6, 33)$ (at $t = 6$).

Method 1 — Shoelace Formula with Origin $O(0, 0)$:

$$\text{Area}(\triangle O P_{\text{first}} P_{\text{last}}) = \frac{1}{2}\bigl|x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1\bigr| = \frac{1}{2}\bigl|(78)(33) - (6)(3)\bigr|$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\bigl|2574 - 18\bigr| = \frac{2556}{2} = 1278 \text{ sq. units}$$

Method 2 — Olympiad Geometry Shortcut (Base $\times$ Perpendicular Distance):

Each lattice step has vector $(-12, +5)$ of length $\sqrt{(-12)^2 + 5^2} = 13$, so $6$ steps give base $P_{\text{first}} P_{\text{last}} = 6 \times 13 = 78$. The perpendicular distance from $O(0, 0)$ to $5x + 12y - 426 = 0$ is $h = \dfrac{426}{\sqrt{5^2 + 12^2}} = \dfrac{426}{13}$. Thus $\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}(78)\left(\frac{426}{13}\right) = 3 \times 426 = 1278$!

Q26 Level 3 NAT Answer: 9

Given equation: $13x + 17y = 2026$ with $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}^+$. Since $13$ and $17$ are distinct primes, $\gcd(13, 17) = 1$.

Step 1 — Find Particular Solution Modulo $13$:

Divide $2026$ by $13$: $2026 = 13 \times 155 + 11$, so $2026 \equiv 11 \pmod{13}$, and $17 \equiv 4 \pmod{13}$:

$$4y \equiv 11 \pmod{13}$$

Add $13$ to the RHS so it becomes divisible by $4$: $4y \equiv 11 + 13 = 24 \pmod{13} \implies y \equiv 6 \pmod{13}$.

Take $y_0 = 6$: $13x_0 = 2026 - 17(6) = 2026 - 102 = 1924 \implies x_0 = \dfrac{1924}{13} = 148$.

Step 2 — Parametrize & Bound $t$:

$$x = 148 - 17t, \qquad y = 6 + 13t \qquad (t \in \mathbb{Z})$$
  • $y \ge 1 \implies 6 + 13t \ge 1 \implies t \ge -\frac{5}{13} \implies t \ge 0$.
  • $x \ge 1 \implies 148 - 17t \ge 1 \implies 17t \le 147 \implies t \le \dfrac{147}{17} = 8.647\ldots \implies t \le 8$.

Hence $t \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 8\}$, yielding $8 - 0 + 1 = 9$ ordered pairs.

30-Second Quotient-Remainder Verification: $13 \times 17 = 221$, and $2026 = 221 \times 9 + 37$ ($q = 9,\ r = 37$). Check $13x + 17y = 37$ in positive integers: $y = 1 \implies 13x = 20$ (no); $y = 2 \implies 13x = 3$ (no). Since $r = 37$ has no positive solution, the exact count is $q = 9$.

NAT Answer: $9$.

Q27 Level 3 NAT Answer: 68

Step 1 — Invert Each Reciprocal Equation:

Since $x, y, z \neq 0$, take reciprocals of all three equations and split each fraction:

$$\frac{2x + 3y}{xy} = 12 \implies \frac{3}{x} + \frac{2}{y} = 12$$ $$\frac{3y + 4z}{yz} = 27 \implies \frac{4}{y} + \frac{3}{z} = 27$$ $$\frac{4z + 2x}{zx} = 18 \implies \frac{4}{x} + \frac{2}{z} = 18$$

Step 2 — Linearize with $a = \frac{1}{x},\ b = \frac{1}{y},\ c = \frac{1}{z}$:

$$3a + 2b = 12 \qquad \text{(1)}$$ $$4b + 3c = 27 \qquad \text{(2)}$$ $$4a + 2c = 18 \iff 2a + c = 9 \implies c = 9 - 2a \qquad \text{(3)}$$

Substitute (3) into (2):

$$4b + 3(9 - 2a) = 27 \implies 4b + 27 - 6a = 27 \implies 4b = 6a \implies 2b = 3a$$

Substitute $2b = 3a$ into (1):

$$3a + 3a = 12 \implies 6a = 12 \implies a = 2$$

Immediately $2b = 3(2) = 6 \implies b = 3$, and $c = 9 - 2(2) = 5$.

Step 3 — Evaluate the Target Expression:

$$\frac{1}{xyz} + \frac{1}{x^2} + \frac{1}{y^2} + \frac{1}{z^2} = abc + a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = (2)(3)(5) + 2^2 + 3^2 + 5^2 = 30 + 4 + 9 + 25 = 68$$

Q28 Level 3 NAT Answer: 125

Step 1 — Intercepts of $L_1: 5x + 6y = 60$:

Setting $y = 0$ gives $A(12, 0)$; setting $x = 0$ gives $B(0, 10)$.

Step 2 — Equal-Area Trisection Property:

The three triangles $\triangle OAP$, $\triangle OPQ$, and $\triangle OQB$ all share the exact same perpendicular altitude from the origin $O$ to the line $AB$. Therefore, equal areas imply equal bases along $AB$: $AP = PQ = QB$. Thus $P$ and $Q$ are the points of trisection of segment $AB$!

  • Point $P$ (closer to $A$, divides $AB$ in ratio $1 : 2$): $$P = \left(\frac{2(12) + 1(0)}{3},\; \frac{2(0) + 1(10)}{3}\right) = \left(8,\; \frac{10}{3}\right)$$ Since $P$ lies on $y = mx$: $$m = \frac{y_P}{x_P} = \frac{10/3}{8} = \frac{10}{24} = \frac{5}{12}$$
  • Point $Q$ (closer to $B$, divides $AB$ in ratio $2 : 1$): $$Q = \left(\frac{1(12) + 2(0)}{3},\; \frac{1(0) + 2(10)}{3}\right) = \left(4,\; \frac{20}{3}\right)$$ Since $Q$ lies on $y = nx$: $$n = \frac{y_Q}{x_Q} = \frac{20/3}{4} = \frac{20}{12} = \frac{5}{3}$$

Check: $n = \frac{5}{3} > m = \frac{5}{12} > 0$, and both $P, Q$ satisfy $5x + 6y = 60$.

Step 3 — Compute $60(m + n)$:

$$60(m + n) = 60\left(\frac{5}{12} + \frac{5}{3}\right) = 60\left(\frac{5 + 20}{12}\right) = 5 \times 25 = 125$$

NAT Answer: $125$.

Trap & Error Forensics — Chapter 8 Post-Mortem

Error Forensics #1 — The $k^2 = c \implies k = \pm\sqrt{c}$ Extraneous-Root Trap

The Blunder: In problems like $kx + 3y = k - 3,\; 12x + ky = k$ (Worked Example 1) or homogeneous systems like Q10, setting $\Delta = 0$ produces a quadratic such as $k^2 = 36 \implies k = \pm 6$. Students reflexively mark both roots $+6$ and $-6$ as the answer for “infinitely many solutions” or “no solution.”

Why It Fails: The condition $\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = 0$ ONLY guarantees that the two lines have the same slope (parallel OR coincident). It does NOT distinguish between the two! Almost universally in exam design, one root ($k = +6$) aligns the constant terms to produce infinitely many solutions while the opposite root ($k = -6$) misaligns them to produce no solution. Conversely, in a homogeneous system ($c_1 = c_2 = 0$, Q10), BOTH roots produce infinitely many solutions and NEITHER can produce no solution.

Exam Antidote: Treat $\Delta = 0$ strictly as a candidate generator. Budget 10 seconds to plug every single root back into both original equations and visually confirm identical lines vs. contradiction.

Error Forensics #2 — Zero-Denominator & Premature Cancellation in Ratio Tests

The Blunder: Writing $\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}$ directly when coefficients contain parameters—as in Q9, where writing $\dfrac{k-2}{k^2-4} = \dfrac{4}{3k-2}$ and cancelling $(k-2)$ yields only $k = -10$ and completely loses $k = 2$; or in Q20, where cancelling $(k - 1)$ silently deletes the degenerate identity $0x + 0y = 0$ at $k = 1$.

Why It Fails: Algebraic cancellation $\frac{f(k)}{f(k)} = 1$ is strictly illegal whenever $f(k) = 0$. Furthermore, two horizontal lines ($0x + 4y = 3$ and $0x + 4y = 9$) or a degenerate identity ($0x + 0y = 0$) have undefined ratios $\frac{0}{0}$ yet are 100% valid geometric configurations.

Exam Antidote: Ban fractions during the first step of any parametric consistency problem. Always write $\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = 0$ as a factored polynomial, list all roots (including zeros of common factors!), and explicitly inspect any value where an entire row $(a_i, b_i)$ becomes $(0, 0)$.

Error Forensics #3 — Reducible-System Domain Killers ($\sqrt{x} > 0$ & Lost Origin)

The Blunder: (a) In Q11, substituting $u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$, getting $u = -\frac{1}{2}$, and mindlessly squaring to claim $x = 4$ is a solution. (b) In Q12, dividing $6x + 3y = 6xy$ by $xy$ without first testing $xy = 0$, thereby reporting only $1$ solution $(1, 2)$ instead of $2$ solutions $\{(0, 0), (1, 2)\}$.

Why It Fails: Every variable substitution $u = g(x)$ carries an invisible range constraint. For $u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$, the range over $\mathbb{R}$ is strictly $(0, \infty)$—never negative! And dividing an equation by a variable expression $xy$ silently assumes $xy \neq 0$, discarding boundary solutions lying on the coordinate axes.

Exam Antidote: Before linearizing, write a 2-word domain header on your scratchpad: “Require $u > 0, v > 0$” for radicals, or “Test $x=0, y=0$ first” whenever variables multiply on the RHS.

Error Forensics #4 — Diophantine Boundary Off-By-One & Integer-Parity Errors

The Blunder: Three fatal off-by-one traps dominate Olympiad Diophantine counting (Q18, Q24, Q26) and integer-parameter systems (Q21):

  • Positive vs. Non-Negative Confusion: Using $x \ge 1$ when zero is allowed (Q18: “zero tokens permitted” $\implies x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0$) drops the boundary solution $(115, 0)$ and gives $14$ instead of $15$. Conversely, using $x \ge 0$ when positive integers are required adds fake axis intercepts.
  • Fencepost Miscounting: Finding $0 \le t \le 8$ (Q26) and reporting $8 - 0 = 8$ instead of $t_{\max} - t_{\min} + 1 = 9$.
  • Half-Integer Blindness (Q21): Counting all $12$ integer divisors of $12$ for $x = \frac{12}{k-6}$ without checking whether $y = \frac{5 - 3x}{2}$ is ALSO an integer! Parity eliminates all $8$ even divisors immediately.

Exam Antidote: Underline the exact domain phrase (“positive integers” $\to \ge 1$ vs. “non-negative / whole numbers” $\to \ge 0$), explicitly list the first and last pairs $(x, y)$ at $t_{\min}$ and $t_{\max}$ to verify both are valid, and always apply the inclusive count formula $t_{\max} - t_{\min} + 1$.
