Solution 03 — Answer: (A) Level 1 Statement I / II MCQ

Statement I is Correct: Nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) is a potent oxidizing agent ($\text{N}$ is in $+5$ state). When reacted with $\text{Zn}$ at standard dilute concentration ($\approx 20\%$), it oxidizes the nascent hydrogen immediately into $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ while reducing itself to $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ or $\text{NO}$: $$4\text{Zn} + 10\text{HNO}_3(\text{dil.}) \to 4\text{Zn(NO}_3)_2 + \text{N}_2\text{O} + 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Statement II is Correct: At extreme dilution ($\approx 1\text{--}2\%$), $\text{HNO}_3$ loses enough oxidizing power that the two mandatory NCERT exception metals — $\text{Mg}$ and $\text{Mn}$ — liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas directly: $$\text{Mg} + 2\text{HNO}_3(\text{very dil.}) \to \text{Mg(NO}_3)_2 + \text{H}_2\uparrow, \qquad \text{Mn} + 2\text{HNO}_3(\text{very dil.}) \to \text{Mn(NO}_3)_2 + \text{H}_2\uparrow$$

Therefore, both statements are correct → (A).

Solution 06 — Answer: 023 Level 1 NAT (000–999)

For any ionic compound $\text{M}_a\text{X}_b$, the number of valence electrons transferred per formula unit equals the total positive charge on the cations (equivalently, the magnitude of total negative charge on the anions):

  • $1$ formula unit of $\text{NaCl}$: $\text{Na} \to \text{Na}^+ + 1e^-$. Electrons transferred $= 1 \times 1 = 1e^-$.
  • $2$ formula units of $\text{MgCl}_2$: Each $\text{Mg} \to \text{Mg}^{2+} + 2e^-$ transfers $2e^-$ (one to each $\text{Cl}$). For $2$ units: $2 \times 2 = 4e^-$.
  • $3$ formula units of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$: Each formula unit contains two $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ions ($2\text{Al} \to 2\text{Al}^{3+} + 6e^-$) donating $6e^-$ to three $\text{O}$ atoms ($3\text{O} + 6e^- \to 3\text{O}^{2-}$). For $3$ units: $3 \times 6 = 18e^-$.

Grand Total Electrons Transferred:

$$1 + 4 + 18 = \mathbf{023}$$

Solution 08 — Answer: (B) Level 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Apply the Reactivity Series segment $\text{Zn} \gt \text{Fe} \gt \text{Cu} \gt \text{Ag}$ to each beaker:

  • Beaker 1 ($\text{Fe} + \text{ZnSO}_4$): Since $\text{Fe} \lt \text{Zn}$, no reaction occurs.

  • Beaker 3 ($\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4$): Since $\text{Fe} \gt \text{Cu}$, displacement occurs: $\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \to \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$. The solution changes from blue ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$) to pale green ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$).

Solution 09 — Answer: (D) Level 2 Statement I / II MCQ

Statement I is Incorrect: Solid $\text{NaCl}$ does not exist as neutral molecules! It is already composed entirely of $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions in the solid state. It fails to conduct electricity strictly because strong electrostatic attractions lock those ions rigidly at fixed crystal lattice nodes, preventing ion migration.

Statement II is Correct: By Coulombic lattice energy scaling $U \propto \dfrac{|q_+ q_-|}{r_+ + r_-}$, $\text{MgO}$ has a fourfold larger charge product ($|(+2)(-2)| = 4$ vs. $|(+1)(-1)| = 1$ for $\text{NaCl}$) combined with smaller ionic radii ($r_{\text{Mg}^{2+}} + r_{\text{O}^{2-}} \lt r_{\text{Na}^+} + r_{\text{Cl}^-}$), giving $\text{MgO}$ a vastly higher melting point ($2852^\circ\text{C}$ vs. $801^\circ\text{C}$).

Hence Statement I is incorrect, and Statement II is correct → (D).

Solution 10 — Answer: (B) Level 2 Single-Correct MCQ

Step 1 (Selective Sulfide Separation): Both $\text{PbS}$ and $\text{ZnS}$ are sulfide ores concentrated by Froth Floatation using pine oil (frother) and a collector. To prevent $\text{ZnS}$ from rising into the froth alongside $\text{PbS}$, sodium cyanide ($\text{NaCN}$) is added as a depressant — it reacts selectively with $\text{ZnS}$ to form a hydrophilic, water-soluble complex $\text{Na}_2[\text{Zn(CN)}_4]$, sinking $\text{ZnS}$ while pure $\text{PbS}$ floats into the froth.

Step 2 (Conversion of Sulfide to Oxide): Because $\text{PbS}$ is a sulfide ore, converting it to $\text{PbO}$ requires roasting (strong heating in excess air), never calcination: $$2\text{PbS}(s) + 3\text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2\text{PbO}(s) + 2\text{SO}_2(g)$$

Hence (B) is exact.

Solution 17 — Answer: (B) Level 2 Passage I MCQ

Solution 18 — Answer: (C) Level 3 Statement I / II MCQ

Solution 25 — Answer: (C) Level 3 Passage II MCQ

Step 1 (Pure Copper Extracted from $318\text{ g }\text{Cu}_2\text{S}$):

$$M(\text{Cu}_2\text{S}) = 2(63.5) + 32 = 159\text{ g mol}^{-1} \implies n(\text{Cu}_2\text{S}) = \frac{318}{159} = 2.0\text{ mol}$$

By Principle of Atom Conservation (POAC) on copper through partial roasting + auto-reduction ($\text{Cu}_2\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{Cu} + \text{SO}_2$):

$$n(\text{Cu}) = 2 \times 2.0 = 4.0\text{ mol} \implies m(\text{pure Cu at cathode}) = 4.0 \times 63.5 = \mathbf{254\text{ g}}$$

Step 2 (Reactivity Filter for Anode Mud):

  • Iron ($2.0\text{ g}$): More reactive than copper ($\text{Fe} \gt \text{Cu}$). It oxidizes into $\text{Fe}^{2+}(aq)$ and stays dissolved in the electrolyte — it does NOT become anode mud!
  • Silver ($1.5\text{ g}$) & Gold ($0.5\text{ g}$): Less reactive than copper ($\text{Cu} \gt \text{Ag} \gt \text{Au}$). Neither oxidizes; both drop beneath the anode as Anode Mud: $$m(\text{Anode Mud}) = 1.5 + 0.5 = \mathbf{2.0\text{ g}}$$

Hence $254\text{ g}$ pure copper and $2.0\text{ g}$ anode mud → (C).

25.8 Trap & Error Forensics — Zero-Error Checklist

Error Forensics #1 — Swapping Calcination and Roasting

The Classic Blunder: Writing "Roasting" for carbonate ores ($\text{ZnCO}_3$) or claiming calcination uses excess oxygen to drive off $\text{CO}_2$.

The Fail-Safe Memory Lock: Match the initial letters directly — Calcination = Carbonates ($\text{ZnCO}_3$) heated in closed (limited/absent) air, releasing $\mathbf{C}\text{O}_2$. Roasting = Sulfides ($\text{ZnS}, \text{PbS}, \text{Cu}_2\text{S}, \text{HgS}$) heated in excess oxygen (think "open-air barbecue roast"), releasing $\text{SO}_2$. Remember: carbonates already contain enough oxygen inside $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ to leave behind $\text{MO}$; sulfides contain zero oxygen, so they must be supplied excess $\text{O}_2$!

Error Forensics #2 — The $\text{HNO}_3$ Blanket Rule Fallacy ($\text{Mg}$ & $\text{Mn}$ Exception)

The Classic Blunder: Blindly marking "NO metal ever liberates $\text{H}_2$ gas with $\text{HNO}_3$" on Statement I/II or Assertion-Reason questions.

The Fail-Safe Memory Lock: Split nitric acid into three distinct concentration zones:
1. Very dilute ($\approx 1\text{--}2\%$) $\text{HNO}_3$: Reacts with $\text{Mg}$ and $\text{Mn}$ to liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas!
2. Standard dilute / conc. $\text{HNO}_3$: Oxidizes nascent hydrogen to $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and emits nitrogen oxides ($\text{N}_2\text{O}/\text{NO}/\text{NO}_2$).
3. Concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ + $\text{Al}, \text{Cr}, \text{Fe}$: Causes instant passivation (protective surface oxide film stops reaction cold).

Error Forensics #3 — Reversing Anode/Cathode & Miscounting Anode Mud in Electrolytic Refining

The Classic Blunder: Making the pure thin metal strip the anode, or summing all metallic impurities ($\text{Zn} + \text{Fe} + \text{Ag} + \text{Au}$) into the anode mud mass.

The Fail-Safe Memory Lock: Remember "Anode Oxidizes & Erodes" — the thick slab of IMPURE metal is ALWAYS the Anode ($+$) so it dissolves away ($\text{M} \to \text{M}^{n+} + ne^-$), while the thin strip of PURE metal is ALWAYS the Cathode ($-$) so pure ions plate onto it. For impurities during copper refining: draw a reactivity cut right at $\text{Cu}$ — metals above $\text{Cu}$ ($\text{Zn}, \text{Fe}$) dissolve into the bath as ions (causing $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ to slowly fall), while ONLY noble metals below $\text{Cu}$ ($\text{Ag}, \text{Au}, \text{Pt}$) drop down as Anode Mud.

Error Forensics #4 — Mixing Up Brass, Bronze & Solder Compositions

The Classic Blunder: Swapping $\text{Zn}$ and $\text{Sn}$ between Brass and Bronze, putting copper inside Solder, or assuming Brass conducts electricity better than pure Copper.

The Fail-Safe Memory Lock: Use the "No-Z / Z-Flip" rule:
• Brass (has no 'z' in spelling) $\longrightarrow$ contains Zn ($\text{Cu} + \text{Zn}$).
• Bronze (has a 'z' in spelling) $\longrightarrow$ contains NO $\text{Zn}$; it is $\text{Cu} + \mathbf{\text{Sn}}$ (Tin).
• Solder $\longrightarrow$ contains NO Copper; it is low-MP $\text{Pb} + \text{Sn}$ (Lead + Tin).
And never forget: alloying pure $\text{Cu}$ into Brass or Bronze lowers electrical conductivity and melting point while raising hardness!

Error Forensics #5 — The "Scratched Tin Can" & "Steam + Iron Oxide" Traps

The Classic Blunder: Thinking any metal coating (like Tin on food cans) continues protecting iron after being scratched, or writing $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ as the product of iron reacting with steam.

The Fail-Safe Memory Lock:
• Sacrificial Rule: A coating protects scratched iron ONLY if the coating metal sits above $\text{Fe}$ in the reactivity series ($E^\circ \lt -0.44\text{ V}$, e.g., $\text{Zn}$ or $\text{Mg}$). Because $\text{Sn}$ ($-0.14\text{ V}$) sits below $\text{Fe}$, scratching a tin can turns iron into the anode and accelerates rusting!
• Iron-Steam Formula: Iron + Steam yields magnetic black $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$ (mixed oxide $\text{FeO}\cdot\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) in a reversible reaction ($3\text{Fe} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \rightleftharpoons \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + 4\text{H}_2$), whereas rusting in moist air yields flaky reddish-brown hydrated $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
