Q1 Level 1 Answer: (A)

Step 1 — Determine the mass of oxygen combining with $1\text{ g}$ of nitrogen in each oxide:

  • Oxide $X$: Oxygen $= 36.36\%$, so Nitrogen $= 100 - 36.36 = 63.64\%$.
    Mass of $\text{O}$ per $1\text{ g}$ of $\text{N} = \frac{36.36}{63.64} = 0.5713\text{ g}$ (exact fraction $\frac{4}{7}\text{ g}$, corresponding to $\text{N}_2\text{O}$).
  • Oxide $Y$: Oxygen $= 53.33\%$, so Nitrogen $= 100 - 53.33 = 46.67\%$.
    Mass of $\text{O}$ per $1\text{ g}$ of $\text{N} = \frac{53.33}{46.67} = 1.1427\text{ g}$ (exact fraction $\frac{8}{7}\text{ g}$, corresponding to $\text{NO}$).

Step 2 — Take the ratio of oxygen masses for a fixed mass ($1\text{ g}$) of nitrogen:

$$\frac{m_{\text{O in } X}}{m_{\text{O in } Y}} = \frac{0.5713}{1.1427} = \frac{4/7}{8/7} = \frac{1}{2} \implies \mathbf{1:2}$$

Because the same two elements ($\text{N}$ and $\text{O}$) form two distinct compounds where the masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of the other bear a simple whole-number ratio ($1:2$), this directly illustrates Dalton's Law of Multiple Proportions. Hence, (A) is correct.

Q8 Level 2 Answer: (A)

Balanced Equation: $2\text{Al}(s) + 3\text{Cl}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3(s)$.

Step 1 — Convert Reactants to Moles: ($M(\text{Al}) = 27\text{ g/mol}$, $M(\text{Cl}_2) = 2 \times 35.5 = 71\text{ g/mol}$)

Step 2 — Limiting Reagent Test ($R = n / \nu$):

$$R(\text{Al}) = \frac{0.50}{2} = 0.25, \qquad R(\text{Cl}_2) = \frac{0.60}{3} = 0.20$$

Since $0.20 \lt 0.25$, $\text{Cl}_2$ is the Limiting Reagent ($\xi = 0.20$) and $\text{Al}$ is in excess (notice that $\text{Cl}_2$ had MORE mass AND more raw moles than $\text{Al}$, yet still ran out first!).

Step 3 — Mass of Excess $\text{Al}$ Leftover:

$$n(\text{Al consumed}) = 2 \times \xi = 2 \times 0.20 = 0.40\text{ mol}$$ $$n(\text{Al remaining}) = 0.50 - 0.40 = 0.10\text{ mol} \implies w(\text{Al remaining}) = 0.10 \times 27 = \mathbf{2.7\text{ g}}$$

Hence, (A) is correct.

Q10 Level 2 Answer: (C)

Evaluation of Statement I (TRUE): Heating an aqueous solution from $25^\circ\text{C}$ to $60^\circ\text{C}$ causes thermal expansion, increasing solution volume $V$ while solute moles $n_B$ and solvent mass $w_A$ stay constant. Hence Molarity ($M = n_B / V$) decreases, whereas Molality ($m = n_B / w_A$) is strictly temperature-independent and remains unchanged.

Evaluation of Statement II (FALSE): Recall the exact $m \leftrightarrow M$ interconversion relation:

$$m = \frac{1000 M}{1000 d - M \cdot M_B} \implies \frac{m}{M} = \frac{1000}{1000 d - M \cdot M_B}$$

If $d \lt 1.0\text{ g/mL}$, then $1000 d \lt 1000$. Subtracting the positive quantity $M \cdot M_B$ makes the denominator strictly less than $1000$. Therefore, $\frac{m}{M} \gt 1 \implies \mathbf{m \gt M}$ (meaning $M \lt m$, NOT $M \gt m$!). Statement II has the inequality backwards.

Thus Statement I is true and Statement II is false → (C).

Q11 Level 2 Answer: (D)

In oxyacids of phosphorus, only hydrogen atoms bonded to oxygen ($\text{P-O-H}$) are acidic/replaceable; hydrogen atoms bonded directly to phosphorus ($\text{P-H}$) are non-ionizable:

  • Hypophosphorous acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_2$): Contains one $\text{P=O}$ bond, two $\text{P-H}$ bonds, and only one $\text{P-OH}$ group. It is monobasic ($n$-factor $= 1$): $$E(\text{H}_3\text{PO}_2) = \frac{M}{1} = \frac{66}{1} = \mathbf{66}$$
  • Phosphorous acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3$): Contains one $\text{P=O}$ bond, one $\text{P-H}$ bond, and two $\text{P-OH}$ groups. It is dibasic ($n$-factor $= 2$): $$E(\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3) = \frac{M}{2} = \frac{82}{2} = \mathbf{41}$$

Hence, the equivalent weights are $66$ and $41$ respectively → (D).

Step 1 — Total $\text{O}_2$ Consumed: From the balanced equations in Q23 with $a = 15\text{ mL}$ ($\text{CH}_4$) and $b = 25\text{ mL}$ ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$):

$$V(\text{O}_2\text{ consumed}) = 2a + 3b = 2(15) + 3(25) = 30 + 75 = \mathbf{105\text{ mL}}$$

(Cross-check: $V(\text{O}_2\text{ unused}) = 130 - 105 = 25\text{ mL}$, which matches the exact $25\text{ mL}$ gas remaining after $\text{KOH}$ absorption! Also $\Delta V_1 = 2a + 2b = 80\text{ mL} = (40 + 130) - 90$ ✓)

Q25 Level 3 Answer: (C)

Step 1 — Initial Millimoles of Every Ion Before Reaction:

  • From $200\text{ mL}$ of $0.4\text{ M}$ $\text{BaCl}_2$ ($n = 200 \times 0.4 = 80\text{ mmol}$): $$n(\text{Ba}^{2+}) = 80\text{ mmol}, \qquad n(\text{Cl}^-) = 2 \times 80 = \mathbf{160\text{ mmol}}$$
  • From $300\text{ mL}$ of $0.2\text{ M}$ $\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($n = 300 \times 0.2 = 60\text{ mmol}$): $$n(\text{Na}^+) = 2 \times 60 = 120\text{ mmol}, \qquad n(\text{SO}_4^{2-}) = 60\text{ mmol}$$

Step 2 — Net Ionic Precipitation Reaction: $\text{Ba}^{2+}(aq) + \text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq) \rightarrow \text{BaSO}_4(s)\downarrow$ ($1:1$ ratio).

  • $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ ($60\text{ mmol}$) is the Limiting Reagent and precipitates completely.
  • Unreacted $\text{Ba}^{2+}$ remaining in solution $= 80 - 60 = 20\text{ mmol}$.
  • $\text{Cl}^-$ is a spectator ion, so all $160\text{ mmol}$ remain in solution! (Watch the factor of 2 in $\text{BaCl}_2$—forgetting it yields the distractor $0.16\text{ M}$ in Option A!)

Step 3 — Final Molarities in Total Volume $V_{\text{total}} = 200 + 300 = 500\text{ mL}$:

$$[\text{Ba}^{2+}] = \frac{20\text{ mmol}}{500\text{ mL}} = \mathbf{0.04\text{ M}}, \qquad [\text{Cl}^-] = \frac{160\text{ mmol}}{500\text{ mL}} = \mathbf{0.32\text{ M}}$$

Hence, (C) is correct.

Chapter 21 Trap & Error Forensics

Trap Forensics #1: Mass-Based / Raw-Mole Guessing in Limiting Reagent Problems (See Q8)

The Fatal Blunder: Seeing $13.5\text{ g Al}$ vs. $42.6\text{ g Cl}_2$ (or even $0.50\text{ mol Al}$ vs. $0.60\text{ mol Cl}_2$) and immediately assuming $\text{Al}$ must be the limiting reagent because it has both a smaller mass and fewer raw moles.

Forensic Autopsy: Balanced stoichiometry ($2\text{Al} + 3\text{Cl}_2$) burns through $\text{Cl}_2$ $1.5\times$ faster than $\text{Al}$. Dividing moles by stoichiometric coefficients reveals $R(\text{Al}) = 0.25 \gt R(\text{Cl}_2) = 0.20$, making $\text{Cl}_2$ the true Limiting Reagent.

Antidote Rule: NEVER identify a limiting reagent by mass or raw moles. Always compute the normalized extent ratio $R_i = \frac{n_i}{\nu_i}$ for every reactant—the smallest $R_i$ wins without exception.

Trap Forensics #2: Forgetting Water-of-Crystallization Atoms in Hydrated Salts (See WE1 & Q4)

The Fatal Blunder: When asked for oxygen atoms in $\text{MgSO}_4 \cdot 7\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Q4) or $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (WE1), students correctly include the water mass when computing molar mass ($246\text{ g/mol}$), yet only count the $4$ oxygens in $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$—completely forgetting the $7$ oxygen atoms locked inside $7\text{H}_2\text{O}$!

Forensic Autopsy: In Q4, counting $4$ O atoms instead of $4 + 7 = 11$ drops $y$ from $3.3$ to $1.2$, changing the NAT answer from $114$ to an incorrect $93$—zero marks with no warning.

Antidote Rule: Before counting atoms in any hydrated crystal $\text{Salt} \cdot x\text{H}_2\text{O}$, mentally rewrite it as a flat molecular formula first: $\text{MgSO}_4 \cdot 7\text{H}_2\text{O} \equiv \text{Mg}_1\text{S}_1\text{H}_{14}\text{O}_{11}$ (total $= 27$ atoms).

Trap Forensics #3: Liquid Water vs. Gaseous Steam in Eudiometry & STP Volume (See Q2, Q14, Q19)

The Fatal Blunder: Either (a) keeping $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ as a gas when a eudiometer is cooled to room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$), OR (b) blindly assuming $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ condenses to zero volume when a problem specifies combustion at $120^\circ\text{C}$ (Q19), OR (c) applying $22.4\text{ L/mol}$ to liquid water at STP (Q2).

Forensic Autopsy: At $25^\circ\text{C}$ ($T \lt 100^\circ\text{C}$), $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is liquid ($\text{vol} \approx 0$), causing methane combustion $\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ to contract by $2V$. At $120^\circ\text{C}$ ($T \gt 100^\circ\text{C}$), $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ stays gaseous steam, so $\Delta n_g = (1+2)-(1+2) = 0$ and contraction is strictly ZERO!

Antidote Rule: Circle the temperature in every gas/eudiometry problem before writing a single equation. Below $100^\circ\text{C}$ (at $1\text{ atm}$): $\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \equiv 0\text{ mL}$. Above $100^\circ\text{C}$: $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$ counts fully as a gas.

Trap Forensics #4: Dividing by Solution Mass Instead of Solvent Mass in Molality (See Q10 & Q21)

The Fatal Blunder: In Q21 ($2.0\text{ M}$ glucose, $d = 1.16\text{ g/mL}$), students compute total mass of $1\text{ L}$ solution as $1160\text{ g} = 1.16\text{ kg}$ and immediately divide $m = \frac{2.0}{1.16} = 1.72\text{ m}$ (falling directly into Distractor A!).

Forensic Autopsy: Molality is defined strictly per kilogram of pure SOLVENT, NOT solution! You MUST subtract solute mass ($360\text{ g}$) from total solution mass ($1160\text{ g}$) to isolate solvent mass ($800\text{ g} = 0.80\text{ kg}$), giving the true value $m = \frac{2.0}{0.80} = 2.50\text{ m}$.

Antidote Rule: Write the mantra "Solution minus Solute equals Solvent" ($w_A = 1000 d - M \cdot M_B$) on every Molarity-to-Molality conversion. Also verify qualitatively: for dilute-to-moderate aqueous solutions where $d \le 1$, always expect $m \gt M$.

Trap Forensics #5: Counting Total Hydrogens as Acid Basicity ($n$-Factor) & Missing Stoichiometric Ion Multipliers (See Q11 & Q25)

The Fatal Blunder: Blindly assuming every hydrogen in a formula is acidic ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_2 \rightarrow n=3$, $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3 \rightarrow n=3$) or forgetting that $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{BaCl}_2$ releases $2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Cl}^-$ ions ($[\text{Cl}^-] = 2 \times [\text{BaCl}_2]$).

Forensic Autopsy: In Q11, dividing by $3$ yields Option (A) ($22$ and $27.33$) instead of the true values $66$ ($n=1$) and $41$ ($n=2$). In Q25, omitting the factor of $2$ gives $[\text{Cl}^-] = 0.16\text{ M}$ (Option A) instead of $0.32\text{ M}$ (Option C).

Antidote Rule: Memorize the phosphorus oxyacid ladder ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4: n=3$; $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3: n=2$; $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_2: n=1$), and in ionic mixing problems always break every strong electrolyte completely into individual ion millimoles BEFORE applying limiting reagent or dilution.
