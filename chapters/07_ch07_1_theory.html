7.1 Polynomial Structure, Zero Multiplicity & End-Behavior Geometry

First-Principles Core • Real Polynomials & Degree Rules

A polynomial in $x$ of degree $n$ (where $n \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$) over the real numbers is an algebraic expression of the form:

$$P(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0, \qquad a_n \neq 0$$
  • Leading Term & Monic Form: $a_n x^n$ is the leading term and $a_n$ is the leading coefficient. If $a_n = 1$, $P(x)$ is called a monic polynomial.
  • Non-Polynomial Traps: Exponents of $x$ must be non-negative integers. Expressions like $x^{-2} + 3$, $\sqrt{x} + 1$, or $\dfrac{1}{x - 1}$ are not polynomials.
  • Zero Polynomial vs. Non-Zero Constant: A non-zero constant $P(x) = c$ ($c \neq 0$) has degree $0$ and no zeroes. The zero polynomial $P(x) \equiv 0$ has an undefined degree and infinitely many zeroes.
  • Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (Real Consequence): A polynomial of degree $n \ge 1$ has exactly $n$ complex roots counting multiplicity, and therefore at most $n$ distinct real zeroes. Furthermore, for polynomials with real coefficients, non-real complex roots always occur in conjugate pairs ($p \pm iq$), which forces every odd-degree real polynomial to have at least one real root.
Geometric Meaning of Zeroes • Multiplicity & Sign Changes

A real number $r$ is a zero (root) of $P(x)$ if and only if $P(r) = 0$, which geometrically means the graph $y = P(x)$ meets the $x$-axis at $(r, 0)$. Suppose $(x - r)^m$ is the highest power of $(x - r)$ dividing $P(x)$, so that $P(x) = (x - r)^m Q(x)$ with $Q(r) \neq 0$. The positive integer $m$ is the multiplicity of the root $r$, and it completely dictates the local shape of the curve at $x = r$:

  • Odd Multiplicity ($m = 1, 3, 5, \dots$) — Curve CROSSES the $x$-axis: As $x$ passes from $r - \varepsilon$ to $r + \varepsilon$, the factor $(x - r)^m$ flips sign from negative to positive, while $Q(x)$ keeps the same sign near $r$. Hence $P(x)$ changes sign and crosses the $x$-axis.
    • If $m = 1$ (simple root): the graph crosses cleanly with a non-zero slope.
    • If $m = 3, 5, \dots$: the graph flattens out horizontally (inflection point) as it crosses.
  • Even Multiplicity ($m = 2, 4, 6, \dots$) — Curve TOUCHES (Bounces off) the $x$-axis: Because $(x - r)^m \ge 0$ on both sides of $r$, $P(x)$ retains the same sign immediately before and after $r$. The graph is tangent to the $x$-axis at $(r, 0)$ and bounces back without crossing.
  • Turning Points Bound: A smooth polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $(n - 1)$ local turning points (peaks and valleys). Reading $T$ turning points on a graph immediately gives a lower bound on degree: $n \ge T + 1$.
Figure 7.1: Top: Local geometry at zeroes of multiplicity $m = 1, 2, 3$. Bottom: On $P(x) = (x+2)(x-1)^2(x-3)$, the curve crosses at the odd-multiplicity roots $x = -2$ and $x = 3$, but touches and rebounds downward at the even-multiplicity root $x = 1$, keeping $P(x) \lt 0$ on both sides of $1$.
Asymptotic Dominance • Leading Coefficient End-Behavior

Factor out the leading term $a_n x^n$ from $P(x)$:

$$P(x) = a_n x^n \left(1 + \frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n x} + \frac{a_{n-2}}{a_n x^2} + \dots + \frac{a_0}{a_n x^n}\right)$$

As $|x| \to \infty$, every fractional term $\dfrac{a_k}{a_n x^{n-k}} \to 0$, so the parenthesis approaches $1$. Therefore, for very large $|x|$, the entire polynomial behaves identically to its single leading term $a_n x^n$. 7.2 Division Algorithm, Remainder Theorem & Factor Theorem

Polynomial Division Engine • Linear, Quadratic & Cubic Divisors

Division Algorithm for Polynomials: Given any dividend polynomial $P(x)$ and any non-zero divisor polynomial $D(x)$, there exist unique polynomials $Q(x)$ (quotient) and $R(x)$ (remainder) such that:

$$P(x) = D(x) \cdot Q(x) + R(x), \qquad \text{where either } R(x) \equiv 0 \text{ or } \deg R(x) \lt \deg D(x)$$

1. Remainder Theorem (Linear Divisor): Set $D(x) = x - \alpha$. Since $\deg D = 1$, $R(x)$ must be a constant $r$. Substituting $x = \alpha$ annihilates $(x - \alpha)Q(x)$ immediately:

$$P(\alpha) = (\alpha - \alpha)Q(\alpha) + r \implies r = P(\alpha)$$

For a general linear divisor $(ax - b)$ with $a \neq 0$, the remainder is $P\!\left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)$.

2. Factor Theorem: $(x - \alpha)$ is an exact factor of $P(x)$ if and only if the remainder $P(\alpha) = 0$. More generally, if $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_k$ are distinct roots of $P(x)$, then the product $(x - \alpha_1)(x - \alpha_2)\cdots(x - \alpha_k)$ divides $P(x)$.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Non-Linear Divisors (Quadratic & Cubic Remainders)

BEST rarely stops at linear divisors. Whenever $D(x)$ has degree $2$ or $3$, set up the general remainder polynomial $R(x)$ of degree strictly less than $\deg D(x)$ and annihilate $Q(x)$ by substituting the roots of $D(x)$:

  • Case A — Quadratic Divisor with Distinct Roots $D(x) = (x - \alpha)(x - \beta)$: Since $\deg D = 2$, write $R(x) = ax + b$: $$P(x) = (x - \alpha)(x - \beta)Q(x) + (ax + b)$$ Substitute $x = \alpha$ and $x = \beta$ to obtain the $2 \times 2$ linear system $a\alpha + b = P(\alpha)$ and $a\beta + b = P(\beta)$. Solving explicitly gives the instant two-point formula: $$R(x) = P(\alpha) + \frac{P(\beta) - P(\alpha)}{\beta - \alpha}(x - \alpha)$$
  • Case B — Cubic Divisor $D(x) = (x - \alpha)(x - \beta)(x - \gamma)$: Write $R(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ (degree $\le 2$) and solve the $3 \times 3$ system from $R(\alpha) = P(\alpha)$, $R(\beta) = P(\beta)$, $R(\gamma) = P(\gamma)$. Pro Move: Write $R(x)$ in staged Newton form $R(x) = c_0 + c_1(x - \alpha) + c_2(x - \alpha)(x - \beta)$ so each constant pops out in one line without solving simultaneous equations!
  • Case C — Repeated Root Divisor $D(x) = (x - \alpha)^2$ (Algebraic Reduction): Substitute $t = x - \alpha$ (so $x = t + \alpha$) into $P(x)$ and expand up to $t^1$; all terms with $t^2, t^3, \dots$ are divisible by $(x - \alpha)^2$, leaving $R(x) = P(\alpha) + c_1(x - \alpha)$ immediately. (Equivalently, using Class 11 calculus, $c_1 = P'(\alpha)$.)
  • Case D — Unfactorable Quadratic Divisor (e.g., $x^2 + x + 1$ or $x^2 + 1$): Use polynomial congruence! For $D(x) = x^2 + 1$, replace every $x^2$ by $-1$ (so $x^4 \equiv 1$, $x^3 \equiv -x$). For $D(x) = x^2 + x + 1$, since $(x - 1)(x^2 + x + 1) = x^3 - 1$, replace every $x^3$ by $1$ and then replace any leftover $x^2$ by $-x - 1$!

7.3 Vieta’s Formulas: Quadratic, Cubic & Biquadratic Polynomials

First-Principles Derivation • Connecting Coefficients to Elementary Symmetric Sums

If a degree-$n$ polynomial $P(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \dots + a_0$ ($a_n \neq 0$) has roots $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n$, the Factor Theorem guarantees the identity:

$$a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \dots + a_0 \equiv a_n (x - \alpha_1)(x - \alpha_2)\cdots(x - \alpha_n)$$

Dividing both sides by $a_n$ and expanding the right-hand product matches the coefficient of $x^{n-k}$ to $(-1)^k e_k$, where $e_k$ is the $k$-th elementary symmetric sum (sum of all products of $k$ distinct roots chosen from the $n$ roots):

$$e_k = \sum_{1 \le i_1 \lt \dots \lt i_k \le n} \alpha_{i_1}\alpha_{i_2}\cdots\alpha_{i_k} = (-1)^k \frac{a_{n-k}}{a_n}$$

Notice the strictly alternating sign pattern: $-,\, +,\, -,\, +,\, \dots$ starting from the ratio of the second coefficient to the leading coefficient!

Rewrite explicitly as $1\cdot x^3 + 0\cdot x^2 + 5x - 7 = 0$ before reading off Vieta’s sums: $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 0$, $\sum \alpha\beta = 5$, and $\alpha\beta\gamma = 7$. Missing-term equations (depressed cubics/quartics) are a favorite BEST speed trap.

7.4 Symmetric Functions of Roots & Newton’s Sums Shortcut

Symmetric Identities • Expressing Power Sums via $e_1, e_2, e_3$

An expression in the roots is symmetric if swapping any two roots leaves it unchanged. By the Fundamental Theorem of Symmetric Polynomials, every symmetric polynomial can be written purely in terms of Vieta’s sums without ever computing irrational or complex roots:

  • Quadratic Core Toolkit ($\alpha + \beta = s$, $\alpha\beta = p$): $$\alpha^2 + \beta^2 = s^2 - 2p, \qquad (\alpha - \beta)^2 = s^2 - 4p \implies |\alpha - \beta| = \sqrt{s^2 - 4p} = \frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{|a|}$$ $$\alpha^3 + \beta^3 = s^3 - 3ps, \qquad \alpha^4 + \beta^4 = (\alpha^2 + \beta^2)^2 - 2p^2 = (s^2 - 2p)^2 - 2p^2$$ $$\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta} = \frac{s}{p}, \qquad \frac{\alpha}{\beta} + \frac{\beta}{\alpha} = \frac{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}{\alpha\beta} = \frac{s^2 - 2p}{p}$$
  • Cubic Core Toolkit ($e_1 = \sum \alpha,\; e_2 = \sum \alpha\beta,\; e_3 = \alpha\beta\gamma$): $$\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 = e_1^2 - 2e_2, \qquad \frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta} + \frac{1}{\gamma} = \frac{e_2}{e_3}$$ $$\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3 = e_1(e_1^2 - 3e_2) + 3e_3 = e_1^3 - 3e_1 e_2 + 3e_3$$ $$\alpha^2\beta^2 + \beta^2\gamma^2 + \gamma^2\alpha^2 = e_2^2 - 2e_1 e_3$$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Newton’s Sums Recurrence for $S_n = \sum \alpha_i^n$

When an Olympiad problem asks for $\alpha^5 + \beta^5$, $\alpha^7 + \beta^7$, or $\alpha^4 + \beta^4 + \gamma^4$, expanding binomials by hand is slow and error-prone. Newton’s Sums convert high-power root sums into a 5-second linear recurrence directly from the polynomial itself!

First-Principles Proof (Quadratic $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$): Since $\alpha$ and $\beta$ satisfy $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$, multiply both sides by $x^{n-2}$ (for any integer $n \ge 2$):

$$a x^n + b x^{n-1} + c x^{n-2} = 0$$

Evaluate this exact equation at $x = \alpha$ and $x = \beta$, and add the two equations together:

$$a(\alpha^n + \beta^n) + b(\alpha^{n-1} + \beta^{n-1}) + c(\alpha^{n-2} + \beta^{n-2}) = 0$$

Defining $S_n = \alpha^n + \beta^n$ (with $S_0 = \alpha^0 + \beta^0 = \mathbf{2}$ and $S_1 = \alpha + \beta = -b/a$), we obtain the Quadratic Newton Recurrence:

$$\boxed{a S_n + b S_{n-1} + c S_{n-2} = 0 \qquad (\text{valid for all } n \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ if } c \neq 0!)}$$

Cubic Newton Recurrence ($ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d = 0$): By the identical multiplication by $x^{n-3}$ and summing over $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$, with $S_n = \alpha^n + \beta^n + \gamma^n$:

  • Seed values: $S_0 = \mathbf{3}$, $S_1 = -\dfrac{b}{a}$, $S_2 = S_1^2 - 2\dfrac{c}{a}$ (or equivalently $a S_1 + b = 0$ and $a S_2 + b S_1 + 2c = 0$).
  • Recurrence for all $n \ge 3$: $$\boxed{a S_n + b S_{n-1} + c S_{n-2} + d S_{n-3} = 0}$$

Doubling Shortcut (When $n$ is Even or a Multiple of $3$): For a quadratic with product $p = \alpha\beta$, you can jump exponentially instead of stepping one by one: $S_{2k} = S_k^2 - 2p^k$ and $S_{3k} = S_k^3 - 3p^k S_k$!

7.5 Transformation of Equations (10-Second Substitution Method)

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • The Reverse-Substitution Engine

Suppose $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n$ are the roots of a polynomial equation $P(x) = 0$, and you want the new polynomial equation $Q(y) = 0$ whose roots are $y_i = g(\alpha_i)$ for a known invertible transformation $g$.

First-Principles Derivation: If $y = g(x)$ is a root of the new equation, then the corresponding old root is $x = g^{-1}(y)$. Since $x$ satisfies $P(x) = 0$, substituting $x = g^{-1}(y)$ directly yields:

$$P\!\big(g^{-1}(y)\big) = 0$$

Clearing denominators (or isolating radicals and squaring for $y = x^2$) instantly produces the exact transformed polynomial—without computing a single Vieta sum!

Desired New Roots Set $y = g(x)$ Substitute into $P(x) = 0$ 10-Second Structural Shortcut on $a_n x^n + \dots + a_0 = 0$
Negated: $-\alpha_i$ $y = -x$ $P(-y) = 0$ Flip signs of odd-power terms only.
Scaled: $k\alpha_i$ ($k \neq 0$) $y = kx$ $P(y/k) = 0$ Multiply $a_{n-r}$ by $k^r$: $a_n y^n + k a_{n-1} y^{n-1} + k^2 a_{n-2} y^{n-2} + \dots + k^n a_0 = 0$.
Shifted: $\alpha_i + k$ $y = x + k$ $P(y - k) = 0$ Replace every $x$ with $(y - k)$ and expand. (For $\alpha_i - k$, substitute $y + k$.)
Reciprocal: $1/\alpha_i$ ($a_0 \neq 0$) $y = 1/x$ $y^n P(1/y) = 0$ Reverse the order of coefficients: $a_0 y^n + a_1 y^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$.
Squared: $\alpha_i^2$ $y = x^2$ $P(\sqrt{y}) = 0$ Separate even powers and odd powers of $x$ on opposite sides, square both sides, then replace $x^2 \to y$.
Fractional Linear: $\dfrac{p\alpha_i + q}{r\alpha_i + s}$ $y = \dfrac{px + q}{rx + s}$ $x = \dfrac{q - sy}{ry - p}$ Substitute $x = \dfrac{q - sy}{ry - p}$ into $P(x) = 0$ and multiply through by $(ry - p)^n$.

7.6 Olympiad Polynomial Construction & Rational Root Theorem

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Pattern Construction $P(x) = k\prod(x - a_i) + R(x)$

One of the highest-yield IOQM/PRMO and BEST Level 3 weapons occurs when you are told the degree $n$ of a polynomial $P(x)$ along with its values at $n$ points $x = a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n$ that follow a recognizable rule $R(x)$ (such as a constant $c$, a linear rule $mx + c$, or a quadratic $x^2 + 1$).

The Auxiliary Difference Trick: Instead of setting $P(x) = c_n x^n + \dots + c_0$ and drowning in an $n \times n$ linear system, define the auxiliary polynomial:

$$Q(x) = P(x) - R(x)$$

Whenever $\deg R(x) \lt \deg P(x) = n$, the polynomial $Q(x)$ has the exact same degree $n$ and leading coefficient $k$ as $P(x)$, AND satisfies $Q(a_1) = Q(a_2) = \dots = Q(a_n) = 0$. By the Factor Theorem, $Q(x)$ collapses instantly into a single factored product:

$$P(x) - R(x) = k(x - a_1)(x - a_2)\cdots(x - a_n) \implies \boxed{P(x) = k(x - a_1)(x - a_2)\cdots(x - a_n) + R(x)}$$

Crucial Extension (Non-Polynomial Patterns like $P(a_i) = \dfrac{1}{a_i}$ or $\dfrac{a_i}{a_i + 1}$): You cannot subtract $\dfrac{1}{x}$ because it is not a polynomial! Instead, clear the denominator first: form the higher-degree polynomial $H(x) = x P(x) - 1$ (degree $n + 1$) or $H(x) = (x + 1)P(x) - x$. Now $H(x)$ vanishes at all given points, and substituting the denominator root ($x = 0$ or $x = -1$) kills the unknown $P(x)$ term and immediately pins down the leading constant $k$!

Figure 7.3: Geometric meaning of the Olympiad construction trick. Subtracting the pattern curve $R(x)$ from $P(x)$ flattens the intersection points $(a, R(a)), (b, R(b)), (c, R(c))$ directly onto the $x$-axis as exact zeroes of $Q(x)$, triggering the Factor Theorem immediately.
Factoring High-Degree Polynomials • Rational Root Theorem

Rational Root Theorem: Let $P(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$ be a polynomial with integer coefficients ($a_n \neq 0, a_0 \neq 0$). If a reduced rational number $\dfrac{p}{q}$ (with $\gcd(p, q) = 1$ and $q \gt 0$) is a root of $P(x) = 0$, then:

$$p \mid a_0 \quad (\text{numerator divides constant term}) \qquad \text{AND} \qquad q \mid a_n \quad (\text{denominator divides leading coefficient})$$

First-Principles Proof: Substitute $x = p/q$ into $P(x) = 0$ and multiply through by $q^n$:

$$a_n p^n + a_{n-1} p^{n-1} q + \dots + a_1 p q^{n-1} + a_0 q^n = 0$$
  • Isolating the last term gives $p\big(a_n p^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 q^{n-1}\big) = -a_0 q^n$, so $p \mid a_0 q^n$. Because $\gcd(p, q) = 1$, Euclid’s Lemma forces $p \mid a_0$.
  • Isolating the first term gives $q\big(a_{n-1} p^{n-1} + \dots + a_0 q^{n-1}\big) = -a_n p^n$, so $q \mid a_n p^n$, forcing $q \mid a_n$. $\blacksquare$

Fast Monic Corollary & 2-Second Checks: If $P(x)$ is monic ($a_n = 1$), any rational root MUST be an integer dividing $a_0$. Before testing divisors, always run two instant mental checks:

  • Test $x = 1$: $x = 1$ is a root $\iff$ sum of all coefficients $= 0$ ($a_n + a_{n-1} + \dots + a_0 = 0$).
  • Test $x = -1$: $x = -1$ is a root $\iff$ sum of even-power coefficients $=$ sum of odd-power coefficients.

7.7 High-Density Formula Vault

Master Quick-Revision Vault • Chapter 7
  • Zero Multiplicity & End-Behavior: Odd multiplicity $m$ $\implies$ crosses $x$-axis (sign flips); even $m$ $\implies$ touches/bounces (sign preserved). Right end ($x \to +\infty$) matches sign of $a_n$; left end matches right end for even $n$ and opposes it for odd $n$.

  • 10-Second Transformations of $P(x) = 0$: $$-\alpha \to P(-y) = 0; \qquad k\alpha \to P(y/k) = 0; \qquad \alpha + k \to P(y - k) = 0; \qquad 1/\alpha \to y^n P(1/y) = 0 \text{ (reverse coeffs)}$$ $$\alpha^2 \to \text{split even/odd powers of } x, \text{ square both sides, replace } x^2 = y$$

Level 1 • Warmup Example 1 • Multiplicity, Graph Reconstruction & End Behavior

A real polynomial $P(x)$ of the minimum possible degree has a graph that crosses the $x$-axis cleanly (non-zero slope) at $x = -3$ and $x = 4$, touches the $x$-axis without crossing at $x = 2$, and passes through the $y$-intercept $(0, 24)$.

(a) Determine the explicit factored formula for $P(x)$ and its leading coefficient.
(b) Describe its end behavior as $x \to \pm\infty$ and compute $P(1)$.

Part (a): Match each geometric feature to its minimum allowable root multiplicity:

  • Clean crossing at $x = -3 \implies$ odd multiplicity $m = 1 \implies$ factor $(x + 3)^1$.
  • Touch without crossing at $x = 2 \implies$ even multiplicity $m = 2 \implies$ factor $(x - 2)^2$.
  • Clean crossing at $x = 4 \implies$ odd multiplicity $m = 1 \implies$ factor $(x - 4)^1$.

Thus the minimum possible degree is $1 + 2 + 1 = 4$, and $P(x)$ takes the form:

$$P(x) = A(x + 3)(x - 2)^2(x - 4)$$

Use the $y$-intercept condition $P(0) = 24$ to pin down the leading coefficient $A$:

$$P(0) = A(0 + 3)(0 - 2)^2(0 - 4) = A(3)(4)(-4) = -48A = 24 \implies A = -\frac{24}{48} = -\frac{1}{2}$$

Therefore, the exact polynomial is $\mathbf{P(x) = -\dfrac{1}{2}(x + 3)(x - 2)^2(x - 4)}$, with leading coefficient $\mathbf{-\dfrac{1}{2}}$.

Part (b): The leading term is $-\dfrac{1}{2}x^4$. Since the degree $n = 4$ is even and the leading coefficient is negative, both ends fall: as $x \to -\infty$ and as $x \to +\infty$, $P(x) \to -\infty$.

Evaluating at $x = 1$:

$$P(1) = -\frac{1}{2}(1 + 3)(1 - 2)^2(1 - 4) = -\frac{1}{2}(4)(1)(-3) = \mathbf{6}$$
Level 1 • Warmup Example 2 • Remainder Theorem with Quadratic Divisor

When a polynomial $P(x)$ is divided by $(x - 2)$, the remainder is $7$; when divided by $(x + 3)$, the remainder is $-8$. Find the exact remainder when $P(x)$ is divided by $x^2 + x - 6$.

Step 1 — Factor the Quadratic Divisor and Set Up the Division Algorithm:

$$x^2 + x - 6 = (x - 2)(x + 3)$$

Since the divisor has degree $2$, the remainder $R(x)$ must have degree at most $1$, so write $R(x) = ax + b$:

$$P(x) = (x - 2)(x + 3)Q(x) + (ax + b)$$

Step 2 — Apply the Remainder Theorem at Both Roots of the Divisor:

  • From division by $(x - 2)$: $P(2) = 7 \implies (0)Q(2) + 2a + b = 7 \implies 2a + b = 7$  …(1)
  • From division by $(x + 3)$: $P(-3) = -8 \implies (0)Q(-3) - 3a + b = -8 \implies -3a + b = -8$  …(2)

Step 3 — Solve for $a$ and $b$: Subtract (2) from (1):

$$(2a + b) - (-3a + b) = 7 - (-8) \implies 5a = 15 \implies a = 3$$

Substitute $a = 3$ into (1): $2(3) + b = 7 \implies b = 1$.

Check: $R(2) = 3(2) + 1 = 7$ and $R(-3) = 3(-3) + 1 = -8$. Hence the exact remainder is $\mathbf{3x + 1}$.

Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be the zeroes of $f(x) = 2x^2 - 5x + 1$. Without solving for $\alpha$ and $\beta$, compute the exact values of:

(a) $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$,   (b) $\alpha^3 + \beta^3$,   (c) $\dfrac{1}{\alpha^2} + \dfrac{1}{\beta^2}$.

From $2x^2 - 5x + 1 = 0$ (here $a = 2, b = -5, c = 1$; note $\Delta = 25 - 8 = 17 \gt 0$ and $c \neq 0$, so both roots are distinct, real, and non-zero), Vieta’s formulas give:

$$s = \alpha + \beta = -\frac{-5}{2} = \frac{5}{2}, \qquad p = \alpha\beta = \frac{1}{2}$$
  1. Compute $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$: $$\alpha^2 + \beta^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha\beta = \left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^2 - 2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{25}{4} - 1 = \mathbf{\frac{21}{4}}$$
  2. Compute $\alpha^3 + \beta^3$: $$\alpha^3 + \beta^3 = (\alpha + \beta)^3 - 3\alpha\beta(\alpha + \beta) = \left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^3 - 3\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{5}{2}\right) = \frac{125}{8} - \frac{15}{4} = \frac{125 - 30}{8} = \mathbf{\frac{95}{8}}$$
  3. Compute $\dfrac{1}{\alpha^2} + \dfrac{1}{\beta^2}$: Combine over the common denominator $(\alpha\beta)^2$: $$\frac{1}{\alpha^2} + \frac{1}{\beta^2} = \frac{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}{(\alpha\beta)^2} = \frac{21/4}{(1/2)^2} = \frac{21/4}{1/4} = \mathbf{21}$$

Level 2 • Exam-Real Example 4 • Cubic Vieta’s Formulas & Power Sums

Let $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ be the roots of the cubic equation $x^3 - 4x^2 + 5x - 3 = 0$. Compute the exact numerical values of:

(a) $\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2$,   (b) $\dfrac{1}{\alpha} + \dfrac{1}{\beta} + \dfrac{1}{\gamma}$,   (c) $\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3$.

Reading the coefficients $a = 1, b = -4, c = 5, d = -3$ with alternating signs $(-, +, -)$ gives Vieta’s elementary symmetric sums:

$$e_1 = \alpha + \beta + \gamma = 4, \qquad e_2 = \alpha\beta + \beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha = 5, \qquad e_3 = \alpha\beta\gamma = 3$$

(Since $e_3 = 3 \neq 0$, none of the roots is zero, so all reciprocals are well-defined.)

  1. Sum of squares: $$\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2 = e_1^2 - 2e_2 = 4^2 - 2(5) = 16 - 10 = \mathbf{6}$$
  2. Sum of reciprocals: $$\frac{1}{\alpha} + \frac{1}{\beta} + \frac{1}{\gamma} = \frac{\beta\gamma + \gamma\alpha + \alpha\beta}{\alpha\beta\gamma} = \frac{e_2}{e_3} = \mathbf{\frac{5}{3}}$$
  3. Sum of cubes (Direct Equation-Substitution / Newton’s Method): Because each root satisfies $x^3 = 4x^2 - 5x + 3$, substitute $x = \alpha, \beta, \gamma$ and add all three equations together: $$(\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3) = 4(\alpha^2 + \beta^2 + \gamma^2) - 5(\alpha + \beta + \gamma) + (3 + 3 + 3)$$ $$\alpha^3 + \beta^3 + \gamma^3 = 4(6) - 5(4) + 9 = 24 - 20 + 9 = \mathbf{13}$$ Cross-check via identity: $e_1(e_1^2 - 3e_2) + 3e_3 = 4(16 - 15) + 3(3) = 4 + 9 = 13$. Matches identically!
Level 2 • Exam-Real Example 5 • Transformation of Equations via Substitution

If $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are the roots of $x^3 - 2x^2 + 4x - 5 = 0$, find the monic cubic equations (in variable $y$) whose roots are:

(a) $\alpha + 2,\; \beta + 2,\; \gamma + 2$,   (b) $\dfrac{1}{\alpha},\; \dfrac{1}{\beta},\; \dfrac{1}{\gamma}$,   (c) $\alpha^2,\; \beta^2,\; \gamma^2$.

Part (a) — Shift Transformation ($y = x + 2 \implies x = y - 2$): Substitute $x = y - 2$ directly into $x^3 - 2x^2 + 4x - 5 = 0$:

$$(y - 2)^3 - 2(y - 2)^2 + 4(y - 2) - 5 = 0$$ $$(y^3 - 6y^2 + 12y - 8) - 2(y^2 - 4y + 4) + (4y - 8) - 5 = 0$$ $$y^3 + (-6 - 2)y^2 + (12 + 8 + 4)y + (-8 - 8 - 8 - 5) = 0 \implies \mathbf{y^3 - 8y^2 + 24y - 29 = 0}$$

Part (b) — Reciprocal Transformation ($y = 1/x \implies x = 1/y$): Since $x = 0$ is not a root ($-5 \neq 0$), substitute $x = 1/y$:

$$\frac{1}{y^3} - \frac{2}{y^2} + \frac{4}{y} - 5 = 0$$

Multiply through by $-y^3$ (reversing coefficients): $5y^3 - 4y^2 + 2y - 1 = 0$. Dividing by $5$ for monic form:

$$\mathbf{y^3 - \frac{4}{5}y^2 + \frac{2}{5}y - \frac{1}{5} = 0}$$

Part (c) — Squaring Transformation ($y = x^2$): Isolate odd powers of $x$ on the left and even powers on the right before squaring:

$$x^3 + 4x = 2x^2 + 5 \implies x(x^2 + 4) = 2x^2 + 5$$

Square both sides to eliminate the lone factor of $x$:

$$x^2(x^2 + 4)^2 = (2x^2 + 5)^2$$

Now substitute $x^2 = y$ cleanly:

$$y(y + 4)^2 = (2y + 5)^2 \implies y(y^2 + 8y + 16) = 4y^2 + 20y + 25$$ $$y^3 + 8y^2 + 16y - 4y^2 - 20y - 25 = 0 \implies \mathbf{y^3 + 4y^2 - 4y - 25 = 0}$$

Quick Vieta Check for (c): $\sum \alpha^2 = 2^2 - 2(4) = -4$ (coeff of $y^2$ is $+4$), and $\alpha^2\beta^2\gamma^2 = 5^2 = 25$ (constant is $-25$). Confirmed!

Level 2 • Exam-Real Example 6 • Rational Root Theorem & Quartic Factorization

Use the Rational Root Theorem to completely factor the quartic polynomial $P(x) = 2x^4 - 3x^3 - 12x^2 + 7x + 6$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and list all four real roots.

Step 1 — List Candidate Rational Roots $p/q$: Here $a_0 = 6$ (divisors $p \in \{\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6\}$) and $a_4 = 2$ (divisors $q \in \{1, 2\}$). Thus every rational root lies in $\left\{\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6, \pm \frac{1}{2}, \pm \frac{3}{2}\right\}$.

Step 2 — Run the Instant $x = 1$ Check: Sum of coefficients $= 2 - 3 - 12 + 7 + 6 = 0$. Therefore $x = 1$ is a root, and $(x - 1)$ is an exact factor! Dividing $P(x)$ by $(x - 1)$:

$$2x^4 - 3x^3 - 12x^2 + 7x + 6 = (x - 1)(2x^3 - x^2 - 13x - 6)$$

Step 3 — Factor the Depressed Cubic $Q(x) = 2x^3 - x^2 - 13x - 6$: Check small integers:

  • $Q(1) = 2 - 1 - 13 - 6 = -18 \neq 0$;   $Q(-1) = -2 - 1 + 13 - 6 = 4 \neq 0$.
  • $Q(-2) = 2(-8) - (-2)^2 - 13(-2) - 6 = -16 - 4 + 26 - 6 = 0$!

Hence $(x + 2)$ divides $Q(x)$. Dividing $Q(x)$ by $(x + 2)$ leaves a simple quadratic:

$$2x^3 - x^2 - 13x - 6 = (x + 2)(2x^2 - 5x - 3)$$

Step 4 — Factor the Quadratic: Splitting the middle term ($-5 = -6 + 1$):

$$2x^2 - 5x - 3 = 2x^2 - 6x + x - 3 = 2x(x - 3) + 1(x - 3) = (2x + 1)(x - 3)$$

Therefore, the complete factorization is $\mathbf{P(x) = (x - 1)(x + 2)(x - 3)(2x + 1)}$, and the four real roots are $\mathbf{x \in \left\{-2,\; -\dfrac{1}{2},\; 1,\; 3\right\}}$.

LEVEL 3 • RANK-BOOSTER & OLYMPIAD BRIDGE (FULL DERIVATION + 30-SECOND SHORTCUT)
Level 3 • Rank-Booster Example 7 • Newton’s Sums Recurrence for High Powers

Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be the roots of $x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0$, and define $S_n = \alpha^n + \beta^n$ for integers $n \ge 0$. Find the exact integer value of $S_6 = \alpha^6 + \beta^6$.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Newton’s Sums Recurrence):

  1. Since $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are roots of $x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0$, we have $x^2 = 3x - 1$. Multiplying by $x^{n-2}$ (for $n \ge 2$) yields $x^n = 3x^{n-1} - x^{n-2}$.
  2. Summing this identity over $x = \alpha$ and $x = \beta$ produces the linear recurrence: $$S_n = 3S_{n-1} - S_{n-2}$$
  3. Establish the two seed values directly from Vieta’s formulas: $$S_0 = \alpha^0 + \beta^0 = 1 + 1 = 2, \qquad S_1 = \alpha + \beta = 3$$
  4. Climb the recurrence ladder step-by-step up to $S_6$: $$S_2 = 3S_1 - S_0 = 3(3) - 2 = 7$$ $$S_3 = 3S_2 - S_1 = 3(7) - 3 = 18$$ $$S_4 = 3S_3 - S_2 = 3(18) - 7 = 54 - 7 = 47$$ $$S_5 = 3S_4 - S_3 = 3(47) - 18 = 141 - 18 = 123$$ $$S_6 = 3S_5 - S_4 = 3(123) - 47 = 369 - 47 = \mathbf{322}$$
30-Second Exam Shortcut (Doubling Jump): Since $p = \alpha\beta = 1$, don’t climb one step at a time! Jump straight $S_1 \to S_3 \to S_6$: first $S_3 = S_1^3 - 3pS_1 = 3^3 - 3(1)(3) = 18$. Then square it: $S_6 = \alpha^6 + \beta^6 = (\alpha^3 + \beta^3)^2 - 2(\alpha\beta)^3 = S_3^2 - 2(1)^3 = 18^2 - 2 = 324 - 2 = \mathbf{322}$ in 10 seconds flat!
Level 3 • Rank-Booster Example 8 • Olympiad Polynomial Construction (Linear Pattern)

Let $P(x)$ be a monic polynomial of degree $4$ satisfying $P(1) = 3$, $P(2) = 5$, $P(3) = 7$, and $P(4) = 9$. Find the exact integer value of $P(5) + P(0)$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

  1. Identify the Pattern $R(x)$: Inspect the input-output pairs $(1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 7), (4, 9)$. Every output satisfies the exact linear formula $R(x) = 2x + 1$.
  2. Construct the Auxiliary Polynomial $Q(x)$: Define $Q(x) = P(x) - (2x + 1)$.
    • Because $P(x)$ is monic of degree $4$ and $2x + 1$ has degree $1$, $Q(x)$ is also a monic polynomial of degree $4$.
    • Evaluating at $x = 1, 2, 3, 4$ gives $Q(1) = Q(2) = Q(3) = Q(4) = 0$.
  3. Apply the Factor Theorem: Having four distinct roots and leading coefficient $1$, $Q(x)$ is uniquely determined as: $$Q(x) = (x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)(x - 4)$$ Therefore, for every real number $x$: $$P(x) = (x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)(x - 4) + (2x + 1)$$
  4. Evaluate $P(5)$ and $P(0)$: $$P(5) = (5 - 1)(5 - 2)(5 - 3)(5 - 4) + (2(5) + 1) = (4)(3)(2)(1) + 11 = 24 + 11 = 35$$ $$P(0) = (0 - 1)(0 - 2)(0 - 3)(0 - 4) + (2(0) + 1) = (-1)(-2)(-3)(-4) + 1 = 24 + 1 = 25$$ Adding both values yields: $$P(5) + P(0) = 35 + 25 = \mathbf{60}$$
30-Second Exam Shortcut (Endpoint Factorial Symmetry): Whenever a monic degree-$n$ polynomial matches a pattern $R(x)$ on consecutive integers $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, the product term at $x = n + 1$ is always $+n!$, and at $x = 0$ is always $(-1)^n n!$. Here $n = 4$ (even), so both product terms equal $+4! = 24$, immediately giving $P(5) + P(0) = 2(24) + R(5) + R(0) = 48 + 11 + 1 = \mathbf{60}$!
Level 3 • Rank-Booster Example 9 • IOQM Classic: Reciprocal Pattern $P(k) = 1/k$

Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial of degree $3$ such that $P(1) = 1$, $P(2) = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $P(3) = \dfrac{1}{3}$, and $P(4) = \dfrac{1}{4}$. Determine the exact values of $P(5)$ and $P(6)$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

  1. Clear the Non-Polynomial Denominator: Notice that $P(k) = \dfrac{1}{k}$ cannot be written as $P(x) - \dfrac{1}{x}$ because $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is not a polynomial. Instead, multiply by $x$ and define the auxiliary polynomial: $$H(x) = x P(x) - 1$$
  2. Determine the Degree and Roots of $H(x)$: Since $\deg P(x) = 3$, multiplying by $x$ raises the degree to $\deg H(x) = 4$. Testing the four given points: $$H(1) = 1\cdot 1 - 1 = 0, \quad H(2) = 2\cdot\frac{1}{2} - 1 = 0, \quad H(3) = 3\cdot\frac{1}{3} - 1 = 0, \quad H(4) = 4\cdot\frac{1}{4} - 1 = 0$$ Thus $H(x)$ is a degree-$4$ polynomial with four distinct roots $1, 2, 3, 4$. By the Factor Theorem, there exists a non-zero constant $k$ such that: $$x P(x) - 1 = k(x - 1)(x - 2)(x - 3)(x - 4)$$
  3. Pin Down $k$ by Annihilating $x P(x)$ at $x = 0$: Substitute $x = 0$ into the identity: $$0 \cdot P(0) - 1 = k(-1)(-2)(-3)(-4) \implies -1 = 24k \implies k = -\frac{1}{24}$$
  4. Evaluate at $x = 5$ and $x = 6$:
    • At $x = 5$: $5 P(5) - 1 = -\dfrac{1}{24}(4)(3)(2)(1) = -\dfrac{24}{24} = -1 \implies 5 P(5) = 0 \implies \mathbf{P(5) = 0}$.
    • At $x = 6$: $6 P(6) - 1 = -\dfrac{1}{24}(5)(4)(3)(2) = -\dfrac{120}{24} = -5 \implies 6 P(6) = -4 \implies \mathbf{P(6) = -\dfrac{2}{3}}$.
30-Second Exam Shortcut (Universal $P(k) = 1/k$ Theorem): Whenever a polynomial $P(x)$ of degree $n$ satisfies $P(k) = \dfrac{1}{k}$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots, n+1$, the exact next value $P(n + 2)$ obeys a famous parity law: $$P(n + 2) = \begin{cases} \mathbf{0}, & \text{if } n \text{ is odd} \\ \mathbf{\dfrac{2}{n + 2}}, & \text{if } n \text{ is even} \end{cases}$$ Here $n = 3$ is odd, so $P(5) = \mathbf{0}$ instantaneously! (And for $x = n + 3 = 6$, $(n+2)$ factorial ratio gives $6 P(6) - 1 = -(n+2) = -5 \implies P(6) = -2/3$.)
