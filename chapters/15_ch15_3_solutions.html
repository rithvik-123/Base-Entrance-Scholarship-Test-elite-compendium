Step 1 — Write Both Shadow Lengths in Terms of Pole Height $h$: Let $h$ be the height of the vertical pole. At altitude $30^\circ$, the shadow length is $x_1 = h\cot 30^\circ = h\sqrt{3}$. At altitude $60^\circ$, the shorter shadow length is $x_2 = h\cot 60^\circ = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{3}}$.

Q24 Level 3 Answer: (B)

Step 1 — Express $\tan\theta$ via Compound-Angle Subtraction: Let the observer stand at distance $x \gt 0$ from the wall. The bottom edge of the billboard is $4\text{ m}$ above eye level (elevation $\alpha$, $\tan\alpha = \frac{4}{x}$) and the top edge is $4 + 12 = 16\text{ m}$ above eye level (elevation $\beta$, $\tan\beta = \frac{16}{x}$). Trap Forensics #1 • Measuring Angle of Depression from the Vertical Wall Instead of the Horizontal

The Fatal Error: When a problem states "from the top of a building, the angle of depression of the foot of a tower is $30^\circ$" (as in Q3), students place $30^\circ$ inside the triangle against the vertical building wall $AB$ instead of against the upper horizontal ray $AE$.

Why It Fails: Swapping the horizontal for the vertical replaces $\theta$ with its complement $90^\circ - \theta$ (turning $\tan 30^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$ into $\tan 60^\circ = \sqrt{3}$). In Q3, this miscomputes $AE = \frac{15}{\sqrt{3}}$ and destroys $CD$ from $60\text{ m}$ to $30\text{ m}$. Conversely, in Q4, the prompt explicitly says "makes $30^\circ$ with the vertical standing trunk"—students who reflexively put $30^\circ$ on the ground fall into the mirror-image trap!

The Antidote: Draw a dashed horizontal ray through every observer's eye before labeling a single angle. Depression and elevation ALWAYS hug the dashed horizontal—unless the wording explicitly specifies "with the vertical."

Trap Forensics #2 • The Cloud–Lake Mirror-Symmetry Offset Error ($DC' = H$ vs. $DC' = H + h$)

The Fatal Error: In Q9 and Q10, students write $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{H - h}{x}$ correctly for the cloud, then mistakenly write $\tan\beta = \dfrac{H}{x}$ (or $\dfrac{H - h}{x}$) for its reflection, forgetting that the observer stands $h$ meters above the water.

Why It Fails: The lake surface—NOT the observer's eye—is the plane mirror! Because the cloud sits at height $H$ above the water, its virtual reflection sits at depth $H$ below the water. Dropping from the observer's eye level to the reflection crosses first the cliff height $h$ to reach the lake, and then the full depth $H$ below the lake, totaling $H + h$. Writing $H$ instead of $H + h$ in Q9 yields $H = 3(H - 35) \implies H = 52.5\text{ m}$ instead of the true integer $70\text{ m}$.

The Antidote: Always draw two horizontal reference lines in reflection diagrams: a solid blue line for the water surface (mirror) and a dashed black line $h$ units higher for eye level. Mark $H$ symmetrically above and below the blue water line before writing a single equation.

Trap Forensics #3 • Confusing "Distance Moved / Remaining Time" with "Distance to Foot / Total Time"

The Fatal Error: Three closely related misreads cost easy marks in Q11, Q12, and Q15: (1) stopping at the remaining time $14\text{ s}$ in Q11 when the question asks for the total time ($28 + 14 = 42\text{ s}$); (2) plugging the gap $AB = 20\text{ m}$ directly into $h = \sqrt{ab}$ in Q15 instead of $OB = 16 + 20 = 36\text{ m}$; and (3) subtracting $x_2 - x_1$ instead of adding $x_1 + x_2$ when an airplane crosses overhead in Q13.

Why It Fails: Formulas like $h = \sqrt{ab}$ and $d = h(\cot\alpha \mp \cot\beta)$ encode strict geometric endpoints. Mixing up the segment $AB$ with the full base $OB$ produces clean-looking distractors planted specifically by BEST paper-setters (e.g., $8\sqrt{5}$ in Q15).

The Antidote: Underline the exact target phrase before boxing your answer: "additional/remaining time" vs. "total time"; "distance from foot" vs. "distance between points"; "same side" vs. "flies directly overhead".

Trap Forensics #4 • Prematurely Rounding $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.73$ or $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ in NAT Integer Problems

The Fatal Error: Seeing $30\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$ in Q2, $1500\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$ in Q13, or $18\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$ in Q16 and Q23, students immediately replace $\sqrt{3}$ with $1.73$ at Step 1, creating ugly decimals ($31.14 \times 2 \times 0.866 = 53.93\dots$) and losing both speed and exact integer precision.

Why It Fails: Every NAT integer question in BEST Paper 1 is engineered so that irrational radicals ($\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{2}$, $2 \pm \sqrt{3}$) cancel algebraically at the final multiplication or division (e.g., $\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{3} = 3$ in Q2, Q5, Q13, Q16, Q23). Early decimal rounding introduces truncation drift that can easily shift a NAT answer off by $\pm 1$—which scores zero.

The Antidote: Carry exact symbolic surds ($\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3}, 2\pm\sqrt{3}$) all the way to the very last line. Never convert a radical to a decimal unless the question explicitly provides a constant like "(Take $\sqrt{3} = 1.732$)".

Trap Forensics #5 • Flattening 3D Orthogonal Bearings (North & East) into a 2D Collinear Line

The Fatal Error: In Q21 and Q27, students see two ground observers $A$ (Due North) and $B$ (Due East) separated by $AB = d$ and apply the 2D opposite-side formula $d = h(\cot\alpha + \cot\beta)$ instead of the 3D ground-plane Pythagoras relation $d^2 = h^2(\cot^2\alpha + \cot^2\beta)$.

Why It Fails: Due North and Due East meet at $90^\circ$, NOT $180^\circ$! Using $OA + OB = 100$ in Q27 gives $\frac{7}{3}h = 100 \implies h = \frac{300}{7}\text{ m}$ instead of the clean exact integer $h = 60\text{ m}$.

The Antidote: Whenever compass bearings appear (North, South, East, West) or a road "does not pass through the foot $O$" (Q22), draw a separate top-down Ground-Plane Map of $\triangle AOB$ showing $OA = h\cot\alpha$ and $OB = h\cot\beta$, then solve the ground triangle via Pythagoras, Law of Cosines, or Apollonius's Theorem.
