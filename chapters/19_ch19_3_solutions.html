Q10 Correct Answer: (C)

Analysis of Statement I: At a smooth local maximum of $U(x)$, the first derivative vanishes ($\frac{dU}{dx} = 0 \implies F_x = -\frac{dU}{dx} = 0$, confirming equilibrium) and the second derivative is negative ($\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} < 0$). Analysis of Statement II: Potential energy $U(x)$ is defined only for conservative forces, where $\oint \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = 0$. Even in one-dimensional back-and-forth motion along a straight line, kinetic friction reverses direction whenever velocity reverses, so work done over a closed round trip ($x_1 \to x_2 \to x_1$) is $-2f_k|x_2 - x_1| \ne 0$. Therefore, no single-valued potential energy function $U(x)$ exists for kinetic friction. Hence Statement II is false.

Hence, option (C) is correct.

  • COME: $v_s^2 = v_0^2 - 2gh_s$.
  • Radial equation at $T = 0$: $-mg\cos\theta_s = \dfrac{mv_s^2}{R} \implies v_s^2 = -gR\cos\theta_s = g(h_s - R)$ (since $\cos\theta_s = 1 - \frac{h_s}{R}$).

Equating both expressions for $v_s^2$:

$$v_0^2 - 2gh_s = gh_s - gR \implies 3gh_s = v_0^2 + gR \implies h_s = \frac{v_0^2 + gR}{3g}$$

Substituting $v_0 = 9\text{ m/s}$, $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$, and $R = 2.4\text{ m}$:

$$h_s = \frac{81 + (10)(2.4)}{3(10)} = \frac{81 + 24}{30} = \frac{105}{30} = 3.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{350\text{ cm}}$$

NAT Integer Answer: 350.

Q27 Correct Answer: 560

Step 1 — Initial kinetic energy and friction magnitude:

$$K_0 = \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}(2)(6)^2 = 36\text{ J}, \qquad f_k = \mu_k mg = (0.25)(2)(10) = 5\text{ N}$$

Step 2 — Find maximum compression $x_{\max}$ during the compression stroke: By WET from spring contact ($K_0 = 36\text{ J}$) to instantaneous stop at $x_{\max}$ ($K = 0$):

$$-\frac{1}{2}k x_{\max}^2 - f_k x_{\max} = 0 - K_0 \implies 50x_{\max}^2 + 5x_{\max} - 36 = 0$$

Solving via the quadratic formula (discriminant $\Delta = 5^2 - 4(50)(-36) = 25 + 7200 = 7225 = 85^2$):

$$x_{\max} = \frac{-5 + \sqrt{7225}}{2(50)} = \frac{-5 + 85}{100} = \frac{80}{100} = 0.80\text{ m}$$

Step 3 — Verify that the block rebounds: At $x_{\max} = 0.80\text{ m}$, the spring restoring force is $F_s = kx_{\max} = 100(0.80) = 80\text{ N}$, which vastly exceeds maximum static friction $f_{s,\max} = 5\text{ N}$. Thus, the block rebounds past the spring's uncompressed position.

Step 4 — Find stopping distance $d$ past natural length: At $x_{\max}$, elastic energy stored is $U_s = \frac{1}{2}(100)(0.80)^2 = 32\text{ J}$. As the block rebounds from $x_{\max}$ back to natural length (distance $0.80\text{ m}$) and then slides distance $d$ past it until $v_f = 0$, the total rebound path length is $0.80 + d$. By WET on the rebound phase:

$$W_s + W_{f_k} = \Delta K = 0 \implies +32 - 5(0.80 + d) = 0$$ $$0.80 + d = \frac{32}{5} = 6.40\text{ m} \implies d = 6.40 - 0.80 = 5.60\text{ m} = \mathbf{560\text{ cm}}$$

Global Energy Audit: Total distance slid over the entire round trip is $x_{\max} + x_{\max} + d = 0.8 + 0.8 + 5.6 = 7.2\text{ m}$. Total heat dissipated by friction $= (5\text{ N})(7.2\text{ m}) = 36\text{ J} = K_0$. Exact match! NAT Integer Answer: 560.

Trap & Error Forensics: The 5 Deadliest Chapter 19 Pitfalls

Forensic Card #1 • The "Friction Always Does Negative Work" Myth (Ref: Q2)

The Fatal Illusion: Students memorize "friction opposes motion, so $W_f < 0$ always."

The Forensic Autopsy: Friction opposes relative slipping between two contacting surfaces—not necessarily the absolute motion of a body relative to the ground! When a box rides without slipping on an accelerating truck bed (Q2), static friction is the only horizontal force pushing the box forward, so $W_{f_s} > 0$ in the ground frame. Even kinetic friction does positive work on a luggage bag dropped gently onto a fast-moving airport conveyor belt until its speed catches up to the belt.

Ironclad Exam Rule: For a single body, static or kinetic friction work can be positive, negative, or zero depending on the observer's frame. Only the paired two-body total work of kinetic friction, $W_{f_k,\text{pair}} = -f_k s_{\text{rel}}$, is strictly negative and frame-invariant.

Forensic Card #2 • The Incremental Spring-Work Blunder (Ref: Q9)

The Fatal Illusion: When asked for the extra work to stretch an already-extended spring from $x_1 = 10\text{ cm}$ by a further $\Delta x = 20\text{ cm}$, students plug $\Delta x = 0.20\text{ m}$ into $\frac{1}{2}k(\Delta x)^2$ and get $40\text{ J}$ instead of the true $80\text{ J}$.

The Forensic Autopsy: Hooke's Law $F_s = -kx$ is linear in the total deformation $x$ measured from the natural length—NOT linear in $\Delta x$ from an arbitrary pre-stretched state. Geometrically, the area under the $F = kx$ line between $x_1$ and $x_2$ is a trapezoid of height $(x_2 - x_1)$ and parallel sides $kx_1$ and $kx_2$, not a triangle starting from zero force:

$$W_{\text{ext}} = \frac{1}{2}k(x_2^2 - x_1^2) = \frac{1}{2}k(x_2 - x_1)^2 + kx_1(x_2 - x_1) \ne \frac{1}{2}k(x_2 - x_1)^2$$

Ironclad Exam Rule: Before touching any spring formula, write down both $x_1$ and $x_2$ strictly in metres measured from the unstrained natural length, then compute $\frac{1}{2}k(x_2^2 - x_1^2)$.

Forensic Card #3 • String ($\sqrt{5gR}$) vs. Rigid Rod ($\sqrt{4gR}$) in Vertical Circles (Ref: Q13, Q22, Q28)

The Fatal Illusion: Students blindly apply $v_{\min} = \sqrt{5gR}$ at the bottom (and $v_{\text{top}} = \sqrt{gR}$) to every vertical circular motion question, failing whenever the problem replaces a string with a massless rigid rod, a bead on a circular wire, or a ball inside a closed smooth tube.

The Forensic Autopsy: A string or one-sided inner track can only exert inward tension/normal force ($T \ge 0, N \ge 0$). If $v_C < \sqrt{gR}$, gravity exceeds the required centripetal force $mv_C^2/R$ and pulls the bob inward off the circle. By contrast, a rigid rod or closed tube is a two-sided constraint: it can push radially outward (compression) to support the bob's weight at the top even when $v_C \to 0^+$! Consequently, the rod requires $1\,gR$ less initial $v_0^2$ ($4gR$ vs. $5gR$) and never exhibits slackening.

Ironclad Exam Rule: Underline the constraint noun in sentence 1 of every vertical-circle question: string / inner loop $\implies \sqrt{5gR}$ (with slackening zone $\sqrt{2gR} < v_0 < \sqrt{5gR}$); rigid rod / wire bead / double-walled tube $\implies \sqrt{4gR}$ (zero slackening zone).

Forensic Card #4 • Confusing $\text{kWh}$ (Energy) with $\text{kW}$ (Power) (Ref: Q5)

The Fatal Illusion: Seeing the word "watt" inside "kilowatt-hour" ($\text{kWh}$), students treat $\text{kWh}$ as a unit of power—or divide by $3600$ instead of multiplying, or forget to divide useful output power by efficiency $\eta$ before computing electricity consumption.

The Forensic Autopsy: Power is energy per unit time ($\text{J/s} = \text{W}$). Multiplying power ($\text{kW}$) by time ($\text{h}$) cancels time completely and leaves pure energy:

$$1\text{ kWh} = \left(1000\,\frac{\text{J}}{\text{s}}\right) \times (3600\text{ s}) = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}$$

Similarly, an electric meter bills you for input electrical energy ($P_{\text{in}} \cdot t = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{\eta}\cdot t$), never useful mechanical output.

Ironclad Exam Rule: $\text{W}, \text{kW}, \text{HP}$ measure Power; $\text{J}, \text{erg}, \text{Wh}, \text{kWh}$ measure Energy. Always verify $P_{\text{in}} > P_{\text{out}}$ when $\eta < 100\%$.

Forensic Card #5 • Linearization & Constant-Acceleration Abuse (Ref: Q3, Q12, Q19, Q29)

The Fatal Illusion: Two closely related calculus traps: (a) using $\%\Delta K = 2(\%\Delta p)$ for large changes like $-30\%$ (getting $-60\%$ instead of $-51\%$ in Q3), and (b) using $s = \frac{1}{2}v_f t$ for constant-power motion from rest (getting $135\text{ m}$ instead of $180\text{ m}$ in Q19).

The Forensic Autopsy: Both errors replace a nonlinear curve with a straight line. In Q3, $K \propto p^2$ is parabolic, so large changes strictly obey $\%\Delta K = 2x + \frac{x^2}{100}$. In Q19, constant power gives $v(t) \propto t^{1/2}$ (concave-down curve), whose area under the $v\text{-}t$ graph is $\frac{2}{3}v_f t$—not the triangle area $\frac{1}{2}v_f t$! Finally, in Q29, students frequently report only $a_c = 50\text{ m/s}^2$ at mid-height $B$ and forget the orthogonal tangential gravity component $a_t = g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.

Ironclad Exam Rule: Use differential approximations only when $|x| \le 2\%$; for constant power from rest, lock in $x = \frac{2}{3}v_f t \propto t^{3/2}$; and for non-uniform circular motion, always compute $a_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_t^2}$.
