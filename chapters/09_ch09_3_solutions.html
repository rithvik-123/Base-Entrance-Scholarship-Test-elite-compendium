Q1 Level 1 Correct Answer: (B)

Given equation: $(k - 2)x^2 - 2(k - 2)x + 3 = 0$.

Step 1 — Enforce the Quadratic Non-Degeneracy Condition ($a \neq 0$):

Because the problem specifies a quadratic equation, the leading coefficient cannot vanish: $k - 2 \neq 0 \implies k \neq 2$. (Indeed, at $k = 2$, the equation collapses to $0x^2 - 0x + 3 = 0 \implies 3 = 0$, which has zero solutions.)

Step 2 — Impose $D = 0$ for Real and Equal Roots:

$$D = \bigl[-2(k - 2)\bigr]^2 - 4(k - 2)(3) = 4(k - 2)^2 - 12(k - 2) = 4(k - 2)(k - 2 - 3) = 4(k - 2)(k - 5) = 0$$

This gives $k = 2$ or $k = 5$. Rejecting $k = 2$ (from Step 1) leaves $k = 5$ only. (Verification: at $k = 5$, $3x^2 - 6x + 3 = 3(x - 1)^2 = 0$, giving repeated root $x = 1$.) Hence (B) $k = 5$ only.

Q2 Level 1 Correct Answer: (C)

Given equation: $(a^2 - 3a + 2)x^2 + (a^2 - 5a + 6)x + (a^2 - 4) = 0$.

Apply the Identity Theorem: A non-zero polynomial of degree $\le 2$ can have at most two distinct roots. Since this equation holds for more than two distinct real values of $x$, it must be the zero identity $0x^2 + 0x + 0 = 0$, forcing all three coefficients to vanish simultaneously:

  • $a^2 - 3a + 2 = (a - 1)(a - 2) = 0 \implies a \in \{1, 2\}$
  • $a^2 - 5a + 6 = (a - 2)(a - 3) = 0 \implies a \in \{2, 3\}$
  • $a^2 - 4 = (a - 2)(a + 2) = 0 \implies a \in \{-2, 2\}$

The unique common intersection of all three sets is $a = 2$. Hence (C) $2$.

Q11 Level 2 Correct Answer: (A)

Proof of (A) via Modulo-8 Arithmetic:

Since $a, b, c$ are odd integers, write $b = 2k + 1$, so $b^2 = 4k(k + 1) + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$ (as $k(k+1)$ is always even). Meanwhile, $ac$ is odd, so $4ac \equiv 4 \pmod{8}$. Consequently:

$$D = b^2 - 4ac \equiv 1 - 4 \equiv 5 \pmod{8}$$

However, every odd integer squared leaves remainder $1 \pmod{8}$ (never $5 \pmod{8}$), so $D$ can NEVER be a perfect square of an integer. Since $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$, a rational root requires $D$ to be a perfect integer square. Thus $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ can never have any rational root.

Counterexamples Eliminating (B), (C), (D):

  • (B) is false: $x^2 + 3x + 1 = 0$ has $D = 5 > 0$ (two real roots).
  • (C) is false: $x^2 + x + 1 = 0$ has $D = -3 < 0$ (non-real roots, not irrational).
  • (D) is false: $D = b^2 - 4ac \equiv 1 \pmod{4}$, never divisible by $4$.

Hence (A).

Q23 Level 3 Correct Answer: (D)

CRITICAL FIRST STEP — Always Factor Before Cross-Multiplying!

A student who blindly cross-multiplies $y(x^2 - 6x + 5) = x^2 - 4x + 3$ and takes $D_x \ge 0$ gets a perfect-square discriminant $(6y - 4)^2 - 4(y - 1)(5y - 3) = 4(2y - 1)^2 \ge 0$ and falsely concludes the range is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, missing the removable hole at $x = 1$!

$$y = \frac{(x - 1)(x - 3)}{(x - 1)(x - 5)} = \frac{x - 3}{x - 5} \qquad (x \neq 1,\ 5)$$

Solve for $x$ in terms of $y$:

$$y(x - 5) = x - 3 \implies x(y - 1) = 5y - 3$$
  • Check $y = 1$: Yields $0 \cdot x = 2$ (contradiction), so $y \neq 1$.
  • Check the Removable Hole $x = 1$: Since $x = \dfrac{5y - 3}{y - 1}$ is strictly forbidden from equaling $1$, solve $\dfrac{5y - 3}{y - 1} = 1 \implies 5y - 3 = y - 1 \implies 4y = 2 \implies y = \dfrac{1}{2}$. Because $x \neq 1$, the value $y = \dfrac{1}{2}$ can NEVER be attained!
  • Check $x = 5$: $\dfrac{5y - 3}{y - 1} = 5 \implies 5y - 3 = 5y - 5$ (impossible), so no other $y$ is lost.

Therefore, the exact range is $\mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\dfrac{1}{2},\ 1\right\}$. Hence (D).

  3. Vertex position ($x_v > 4$): $a > 4$.

Intersecting $a \le 6$, $a \in (-\infty, 2) \cup (5, \infty)$, and $a > 4$ gives $a \in (5, 6]$. (At $a = 6$, $f(x) = (x - 6)^2 = 0$ has equal roots $6, 6 > 4$, so $6$ is included.) Hence (A) $(5, 6]$.

Q26 Level 3 NAT Answer: 7

Q27 Level 3 NAT Answer: 113

Let $\alpha > 0$ be the common positive root of all three quadratic equations:

$$\alpha^2 + a\alpha + 12 = 0 \qquad \text{(1)}$$ $$\alpha^2 + b\alpha + 15 = 0 \qquad \text{(2)}$$ $$\alpha^2 + (a + b)\alpha + 36 = 0 \qquad \text{(3)}$$

Step 1 — Eliminate Both Parameters $(a + b)\alpha$ in One Line:

Add Eq (1) and Eq (2):

$$2\alpha^2 + (a + b)\alpha + 27 = 0 \qquad \text{(4)}$$

Subtract Eq (3) from Eq (4):

$$(2\alpha^2 - \alpha^2) + (27 - 36) = 0 \implies \alpha^2 - 9 = 0 \implies \alpha = 3 \quad (\text{since } \alpha > 0)$$

Step 2 — Recover $a$ and $b$:

  • From Eq (1): $3^2 + 3a + 12 = 0 \implies 3a = -21 \implies a = -7$.
  • From Eq (2): $3^2 + 3b + 15 = 0 \implies 3b = -24 \implies b = -8$.

Verification in Eq (3): $3^2 + (-7 - 8)(3) + 36 = 9 - 45 + 36 = 0$. Exact match!

Step 3 — Compute $a^2 + b^2$:

$$a^2 + b^2 = (-7)^2 + (-8)^2 = 49 + 64 = 113$$

NAT Answer: $113$.

Q28 Level 3 NAT Answer: 105

Step 1 — Verify the Denominator Is Strictly Positive Everywhere:

For $x^2 - x + 1$, $a = 1 > 0$ and $D = (-1)^2 - 4(1)(1) = -3 < 0$, so $x^2 - x + 1> 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Because the denominator is strictly positive everywhere, we are 100% legally permitted to multiply through both inequalities by $(x^2 - x + 1)$ without reversing either sign!

Step 2 — Solve Left Inequality for All $x \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$-8(x^2 - x + 1) < x^2 + kx - 4 \implies 9x^2 + (k - 8)x + 4> 0 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}$$

Since the leading coefficient is $9 > 0$, this holds for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$ iff $D_1 < 0$:

$$(k - 8)^2 - 4(9)(4) < 0 \implies (k - 8)^2 < 144 \implies -12 < k - 8 < 12 \implies -4 < k < 20$$

Step 3 — Solve Right Inequality for All $x \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$x^2 + kx - 4 < 5(x^2 - x + 1) \implies 4x^2 - (k + 5)x + 9> 0 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}$$

Since the leading coefficient is $4 > 0$, this holds for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$ iff $D_2 < 0$:

$$(k + 5)^2 - 4(4)(9) < 0 \implies (k + 5)^2 < 144 \implies -12 < k + 5 < 12 \implies -17 < k < 7$$

Step 4 — Intersect & Sum the Squares of Valid Integers:

Intersecting $(-4, 20)$ and $(-17, 7)$ yields $-4 < k < 7$. The valid integers are $k \in \{-3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$:

$$\sum k^2 = (-3)^2 + (-2)^2 + (-1)^2 + 0^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2 + 6^2$$ $$= (9 + 4 + 1) + 0 + (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36) = 14 + 91 = 105$$

NAT Answer: $105$.

Trap & Error Forensics — Chapter 9 Post-Mortem

Error Forensics #1 — The Two-Faced $a = 0$ Parametric Quadratic Trap

The Blunder: Students treat $a = 0$ inconsistently—either forgetting to exclude $a = 0$ when a problem demands a quadratic equation (selecting $k = 2, 5$ in Q1 instead of $k = 5$ only), OR forgetting to include $a = 0$ when a problem asks for an inequality or real-root condition valid for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$ (selecting $(3, 8)$ in Q9 instead of $[3, 8)$!).

Why It Fails: Setting $D = 0$ or $D < 0$ is only meaningful when $a \neq 0$. At $a=0$, the parabola disappears and collapses into a straight line $bx + c$ or a horizontal constant $c$—whose behavior cannot be predicted by $D$.

Exam Antidote: Whenever the coefficient of $x^2$ contains a parameter $k$, write “Boundary Check: $a(k) = 0$” as Line 1 of your scratchpad. Plug that value directly into the original equation/inequality and judge its truth by hand before touching $D$.

Error Forensics #2 — Blindly Assuming Conjugate Pairs Without Checking Coefficient Fields

The Blunder: Applying “roots occur in conjugate pairs” mechanically without verifying the number field of the coefficients: claiming that if $2 + \sqrt{3}$ is a root of $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with real coefficients, then $2 - \sqrt{3}$ must also be a root; OR forgetting to rationalize $\frac{1}{3 - \sqrt{5}}$ first in Q3.

Why It Fails: Conjugate pairing is strictly field-dependent:

  • Complex conjugates ($p \pm iq$): Guaranteed ONLY when $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ (this is why Q10 works!).
  • Irrational surd conjugates ($p \pm \sqrt{q}$): Guaranteed ONLY when $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Q}$! With irrational real coefficients like $x^2 - (3 + \sqrt{3})x + (2 + \sqrt{3}) = 0$, the roots are $2 + \sqrt{3}$ and $1$—NOT conjugates.

Exam Antidote: Before invoking conjugates, underline the exact field hypothesis in the problem stem ($a, b, c \in \mathbb{Q}$ for surds; $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ for complex roots). Never assume $\mathbb{Q}$ when the problem only grants $\mathbb{R}$.

Error Forensics #3 — Squaring Radical Equations & Ignoring Substitution Range Filters

The Blunder: In Q7 ($\sqrt{5x + 19} = x + 1$), squaring both sides to get $x^2 - 3x - 18 = 0$ and summing the roots via Vieta ($-b/a = 3$) without noticing that $x = -3$ makes the RHS $-2 < 0$! The same silent killer occurs in exponential quadratics ($2^x=-3$, impossible), reciprocal quartics ($|x + 1/x| < 2$, non-real), and rational ranges where cancelling $(x - 1)$ hides a hole (Q23).

Why It Fails: Squaring is NOT a reversible equivalence: $A = B \implies A^2 = B^2$, but $A^2 = B^2$ merges the true equation $A = B$ with the shadow equation $A = -B$. Thus $x = -3$ is actually the root of $\sqrt{5x + 19} = -(x + 1)$, NOT the original problem!

Exam Antidote: Never apply Vieta’s sum of roots to a squared radical equation! Write the non-negativity filter $Q(x) \ge 0$ BEFORE squaring, and explicitly back-substitute every candidate root into the original un-squared equation.

Error Forensics #4 — Cross-Multiplying Inequalities by Unknown Signs & Wavy Curve Blind Spots

The Blunder: Solving $\dfrac{x^2 - 3x - 40}{x^2 - 5x - 6} \le 0$ by cross-multiplying $(x^2 - 5x - 6)$ to the right side to get $x^2 - 3x - 40 \le 0$; OR forgetting three Wavy Curve edge cases: (i) keeping even-multiplicity poles like $(x + 2)^2$ in Q13 excluded as $(-2, -2)$ splits, (ii) omitting isolated even-multiplicity numerator zeros like $\{3\}$ in Q13 and $\{2\}$ in Q26 under $\le/\ge$, and (iii) failing to flip the sign when normalizing $(3 - 4m) = -(4m - 3)$ in Q22.

Why It Fails: Multiplying an inequality by an expression $E(x)$ preserves the inequality ONLY when $E(x) > 0$ everywhere (which is WHY cross-multiplying by $x^2 - x + 1 > 0$ WAS legal in Q28, because $a = 1 > 0,\ D = -3 < 0$!). Whenever $E(x)$ changes sign, cross-multiplication destroys half the solution set.

Exam Antidote: Only cross-multiply by an expression after proving $a > 0, D < 0$ (strictly positive everywhere). Otherwise, move everything to one side, normalize every factor’s $x$-coefficient to $+1$, mark poles with hollow $\circ$, bounce on even multiplicities, and manually test every solid numerator zero $\bullet$ at the end.

Error Forensics #5 — Missing Boundary Zeros in “Exactly One Root in $(k_1, k_2)$” & Both-Roots Common Trap

The Blunder: (a) In Q22, using ONLY $f(0) \cdot f(2) < 0$ to answer $(-\infty, \frac{1}{2}) \cup (\frac{3}{4}, \infty)$ and losing the exact closed endpoints $m=\frac{1}{2}$ and $m=\frac{3}{4}$. (b) In Q20, solving $(k - 2)(\alpha - 1)=0$ and selecting both $k=2$ and $k=-3$ for “exactly one common root.”

Why It Fails: The strict product condition $f(k_1) f(k_2) < 0$ is sufficient for exactly one root in $(k_1, k_2)$, NOT necessary—because one root can sit harmlessly ON the excluded boundary ($0$ or $2$) while the second root sits strictly inside $(0, 2)$! Likewise, eliminating $x^2$ in common-root problems produces candidates where the two equations become completely identical (sharing BOTH roots).

Exam Antidote: Whenever a problem says “exactly one”—whether one root in $(k_1, k_2)$ or one common root—budget 15 seconds for explicit boundary verification: test $f(k_1) = 0$ and $f(k_2) = 0$ individually, and verify that common-root parameters do not make the two quadratics proportional.
