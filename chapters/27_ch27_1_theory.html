Life Processes

Nutrition, respiration, transportation and excretion, taught from first principles at NCERT-Exemplar / NSEJS depth. Every mechanism is traced to a gradient (concentration, osmotic, pressure or electrochemical) before the memory anchor is given. This is the standard that BEST Paper 2 actually tests.

⏱ 8 Hours NSEJS Depth All-MCQ Format (No NAT) 4 Diagrams · 9 Worked Examples
Master Idea — Every Life Process Exploits a Gradient

Diffusion drives gas exchange. Osmosis drives stomatal opening, root uptake and ADH action. Pressure gradients drive breathing, blood flow, glomerular ultrafiltration and Münch flow. Active transport spends ATP to build a gradient: K+ in guard cells, sucrose loading in phloem, glucose reabsorption in the PCT, the NaCl pump in the ascending limb.

Memory anchor: when an MCQ looks confusing, ask "What is the gradient, and who built it?"

1.2 Photosynthesis — Mechanism

Core Concept — Two Linked Stages

$$6\text{CO}_2 + 12\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{light, chlorophyll}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Cancelling the 6 $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ on both sides gives the textbook form $6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2$. The 12 $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is the honest form. The $6\text{O}_2$ (12 oxygen atoms) comes from 12 water molecules, and the Calvin cycle regenerates 6 water molecules.

Feature Light Reaction Dark Reaction (Calvin Cycle)
Site Thylakoid membranes of grana Stroma of the chloroplast
Needs light directly? Yes No. It is light-independent but needs the ATP and NADPH made in light.
Key events Photolysis: $2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^- + \text{O}_2$. Electron flow builds a proton gradient, and ATP synthase makes ATP. $\text{NADP}^+$ is reduced to NADPH. RuBisCO fixes $\text{CO}_2$ onto RuBP (5C) to give 2 × 3-PGA. ATP and NADPH reduce this to G3P. RuBP is regenerated, and G3P is built up into glucose.
Inputs → Outputs $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, light, ADP, $\text{NADP}^+$ → $\text{O}_2$, ATP, NADPH $\text{CO}_2$, ATP, NADPH → G3P → glucose, ADP, $\text{NADP}^+$
Source of $\text{O}_2$ Always water, never $\text{CO}_2$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Proof, Stoichiometry and CAM
  • Ruben–Kamen heavy-oxygen proof: with $\text{H}_2^{18}\text{O}$ the released oxygen is $^{18}\text{O}_2$. With $\text{C}^{18}\text{O}_2$ the label appears in sugar and water, and the released gas is unlabelled.
  • Calvin stoichiometry: per $\text{CO}_2$ fixed, 3 ATP + 2 NADPH are used. Three turns give 1 net G3P. Six turns make 1 glucose and cost 18 ATP + 12 NADPH.
  • "Dark" is a misnomer: in the dark the supply of ATP and NADPH is exhausted within seconds and the cycle stops.
  • Cyclic photophosphorylation (PSI only) makes ATP only: no NADPH and no $\text{O}_2$.
  • Desert CAM plants (Opuntia, Aloe, Kalanchoe, pineapple):
    • Night: stomata open (cool, humid). PEP carboxylase fixes $\text{CO}_2$ into malic acid stored in the vacuole, so leaf sap becomes acidic.
    • Day: stomata shut to save water. Malic acid releases $\text{CO}_2$ internally and the Calvin cycle runs in the stroma, powered by light-reaction ATP and NADPH.
Stomatal Opening / Closing — Guard-Cell Turgor

Guard cells are bean-shaped. The wall facing the pore is thick and inelastic, and the outer wall is thin and stretchy.

Opening: light (blue light receptors) activates a proton pump → active $\text{K}^+$ influx (with $\text{Cl}^-$ and malate) → osmotic potential falls → water enters by osmosis → guard cells swell → the thin outer wall bulges and the pore opens.

Closing: water stress (abscisic acid) or darkness → $\text{K}^+$ efflux → water leaves → guard cells go flaccid → pore closes.

Memory anchor: K+ in, water in, turgid, open.

Path: mouth → pharynx → oesophagus (peristalsis) → stomach → small intestine (duodenum → jejunum → ileum) → large intestine → rectum → anus.

⚠ Exam Trap — Bile Is Not an Enzyme Source

Bile contains no digestive enzymes. It is physical (emulsification) and chemical (neutralisation of acid) support only. Neutralised chyme gives the alkaline medium that pancreatic enzymes need.

Activation cascade: enterokinase (duodenal wall) converts trypsinogen → trypsin, and trypsin then activates the other pancreatic zymogens. Pancreatic juice is the most complete digestive juice because it digests all three food classes.

Absorption and Adaptations

The small intestine is lined by villi, each with microvilli, giving a huge surface area. Each villus has a capillary network and a central lacteal.

  • Glucose, other monosaccharides and amino acids → capillaries → hepatic portal vein → liver.
  • Fatty acids and glycerol are re-assembled into triglycerides and packed into chylomicrons → lacteals (lymph) → blood.
  • The large intestine absorbs water and salts and forms faeces.
  • Herbivores have a longer small intestine (cellulose digested slowly, with symbiotic bacteria in the caecum). Carnivores have a shorter one (meat is easily digested).

2. Respiration

2.1 Aerobic vs Anaerobic Breakdown of Glucose

Core Concept — One Common Start, Three Fates of Pyruvate

Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm: 6C glucose → 2 × 3C pyruvate, with net 2 ATP and 2 NADH. It needs no $\text{O}_2$.

1. Yeast (ethanol fermentation): $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \to 2\,\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\,\text{CO}_2 + 2\text{ ATP}$

2. Muscle (low $\text{O}_2$, "oxygen debt"): $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \to 2\,\text{C}_3\text{H}_6\text{O}_3 \text{ (lactic acid)} + 2\text{ ATP}$. No $\text{CO}_2$ is released, and lactic acid build-up causes cramps.

3. Mitochondria (aerobic): Krebs cycle in the matrix, electron transport chain on the inner membrane. $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2 \to 6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} + 36\text{–}38\text{ ATP}$

Feature Aerobic Anaerobic
$\text{O}_2$ Required Not required
Site Cytoplasm (glycolysis) + mitochondria Cytoplasm only
End products $\text{CO}_2$ + $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (complete oxidation) Ethanol + $\text{CO}_2$ (yeast) or lactic acid (muscle)
ATP per glucose 36–38 (BEST/NCERT convention) 2

Aerobic respiration yields about 18–19 times more ATP. Modern textbooks estimate ≈30–32 ATP, but the BEST answer key uses 36–38, so use it in the exam. ATP stores ≈30.5 kJ/mol in its terminal phosphate bond.

2.2 Human Respiratory System and Breathing Mechanics

Path: nostrils (hair and mucus filter) → nasal cavity → pharynx → larynx → trachea (C-shaped cartilage rings keep it open) → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli.

Why Alveoli Are Excellent Exchangers

The alveolar wall is one cell thick and moist. It is wrapped in a dense capillary network, and the combined surface area is enormous (≈70–100 m²). That gives a short diffusion path, a large area and a steep gradient (alveolar $p\text{O}_2 \approx 100$ vs venous blood ≈ 40 mmHg).

Residual volume (≈1.2 L) stays in the lungs even after maximal exhalation. It keeps alveoli from collapsing and lets exchange continue between breaths.

Boyle's Law Breathing: $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$ at constant $T$

Inhalation: the diaphragm contracts and flattens, and external intercostal muscles lift the ribs up and out. Thoracic volume rises, pressure drops below atmospheric, and air flows in.

Exhalation (at rest): the muscles relax, volume falls, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out.

Fish: dissolved $\text{O}_2$ in water is ≈30 times lower than in an equal volume of air, and water is dense and viscous. Fish must pump water over gills fast. In the gill lamellae blood flows opposite to water (counter-current), so $\text{O}_2$ is extracted along the whole length.

2.3 NSEJS Bridge — Oxygen–Haemoglobin Dissociation and Gas Transport

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin has 4 subunits (2α + 2β). Each carries a haem group with $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, so one Hb binds up to 4 $\text{O}_2$. Binding is cooperative: each $\text{O}_2$ bound makes the next easier. This gives the sigmoidal curve, which a non-cooperative single-site protein (e.g., myoglobin) would not show.

  • Plateau at high $p\text{O}_2$: in the lungs (≈100 mmHg) Hb is ≈97% saturated. A moderate fall in alveolar $p\text{O}_2$ barely changes loading.
  • Steep part at low $p\text{O}_2$: in tissues a small fall in $p\text{O}_2$ releases a lot of $\text{O}_2$.
  • Bohr effect (right shift): $\uparrow\text{CO}_2$, $\uparrow\text{H}^+$ ($\downarrow$ pH), $\uparrow$ temperature or $\uparrow$ 2,3-BPG lowers Hb's affinity for $\text{O}_2$, so more is unloaded exactly where tissues are most active.

Fig. 27.1 O₂–Hb dissociation curve. The sigmoid shape arises from cooperative binding of 4 O₂ per Hb. The Bohr shift moves P₅₀ right, so at tissue pO₂ ≈ 20 mmHg saturation falls from ≈35% to ≈20% (extra O₂ unloaded), while loading in the lungs (plateau) is almost unchanged.

⚠ Exam Trap — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

CO binds Hb's haem iron with an affinity about 200 times that of $\text{O}_2$, forming stable carboxyhaemoglobin (HbCO). Arterial dissolved $p\text{O}_2$ stays normal, but the oxygen-carrying capacity collapses. CO also left-shifts the curve of the remaining haems, so $\text{O}_2$ is released less readily.

The result is tissue hypoxia despite normal breathing. Treatment is pure (high-flow) $\text{O}_2$ to compete CO off.

$\text{CO}_2$ Transport (Tissue → Lungs)
  • ≈70% as bicarbonate: inside RBCs, $\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \overset{\text{carbonic anhydrase}}{\rightleftharpoons} \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{HCO}_3^-$. $\text{HCO}_3^-$ moves into plasma in exchange for $\text{Cl}^-$ (chloride shift). Hb buffers the $\text{H}^+$, which helps $\text{O}_2$ release.
  • ≈20–25% as carbaminohaemoglobin, bound to Hb's amino groups.
  • ≈7% dissolved in plasma.

In the lungs the reactions reverse and $\text{CO}_2$ diffuses into the alveoli.

3. Transportation

3.1 Human Blood

Component Key facts
Plasma (≈55%) ≈90% water. Carries nutrients, hormones, urea, $\text{CO}_2$ as $\text{HCO}_3^-$, and proteins (albumin, globulins, fibrinogen).
RBCs (≈5 million/µL) Biconcave and enucleated in mammals (large area, more room for Hb). Made in red bone marrow, life span ≈120 days, destroyed in spleen and liver.
WBCs (4,000–11,000/µL) Immunity: phagocytes (neutrophils, monocytes) and lymphocytes (antibodies).
Platelets (≈1.5–4 lakh/µL) Cell fragments that start clotting.
Clotting and the Plasma vs Serum Trap

Injury → platelets and damaged tissue release clotting factors (thromboplastin) → prothrombin → thrombin (needs $\text{Ca}^{2+}$; prothrombin synthesis needs vitamin K) → soluble fibrinogen → insoluble fibrin mesh that traps blood cells as a clot.

Serum = plasma minus clotting factors (fibrinogen). Serum is what remains after clotting, and it cannot clot again.

⚠ Exam Trap — Pulmonary Exception

Artery and vein are defined by direction, not by oxygen content. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood (heart → lungs) and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood (lungs → heart). The umbilical vein is the fetal analogue, carrying oxygenated blood to the fetus.

3.3 Heart, Double Circulation and Blood Pressure

Fig. 27.2 Double circulation. Path: vena cava → RA → tricuspid → RV → pulmonary semilunar valve → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → LA → bicuspid → LV → aortic semilunar valve → aorta → body. SA = sinoatrial node (pacemaker, RA wall); AV = atrioventricular node (base of RA).

Fish 2 (1 atrium + 1 ventricle) Single (blood passes the heart once per circuit) No, but low pressure beyond the gills

Heart Structure and Blood Pressure
  • Valves: tricuspid (RA|RV), bicuspid/mitral (LA|LV), semilunar valves at the pulmonary artery and aortic roots. Tendinous cords keep AV valve flaps from everting.
  • Wall thickness: the left ventricle is thickest because it pumps against systemic resistance (≈120 mmHg) while the right ventricle pumps to nearby lungs (≈25 mmHg).
  • Blood pressure: systolic $120$ / diastolic $80\text{ mmHg}$ in a healthy adult, measured with a sphygmomanometer. Pulse pressure = $120 - 80 = 40\text{ mmHg}$.
Lymph

Lymph is tissue (interstitial) fluid that has drained into lymph vessels. It is colourless, without RBCs and with far less protein than plasma, and it contains lymphocytes. One-way valves move it onward to the subclavian veins. Lacteals in villi absorb digested fats into lymph, and lymph nodes filter pathogens.

3.4 NSEJS Bridge — Cardiac Cycle Mechanics

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — The 0.8 s Cycle

NCERT works with a cycle of 0.8 s (exactly $60 / 0.8 = 75$ bpm). For the textbook figure of 72 bpm, $60 / 72 = 0.83\text{ s}$, so use 0.8 s in the exam unless numbers are given.

Phase Duration Valve status Event
Atrial systole 0.1 s AV open, SL closed SA node fires, atria contract, ≈30% of ventricular filling completed
Ventricular systole 0.3 s AV close (LUB), then SL open Isovolumetric rise, then ejection. Atria are in diastole meanwhile.
Joint diastole 0.4 s SL close (DUB), then AV open Passive filling (≈70%)
  • Conduction: SA node → AV node (delay lets ventricles fill) → Bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje fibres → ventricular contraction from apex upward. The heart is myogenic (its beat originates in the heart itself). Sympathetic nerves speed it up and the vagus slows it.
  • LUB = closure of tricuspid and bicuspid valves at the start of ventricular systole. DUB = closure of aortic and pulmonary semilunar valves at the start of ventricular diastole.
  • ECG: P wave = atrial depolarisation, QRS = ventricular depolarisation, T = ventricular repolarisation.
  • Cardiac output: $CO = SV \times HR \approx 70\text{ mL} \times 72 \approx 5\text{ L/min}$.

4. Excretion

4.1 Human Excretory System and Nephron

Pathway: kidney → ureter → urinary bladder → urethra. Each kidney contains about a million nephrons. A nephron has: Bowman's capsule + glomerulus (renal corpuscle) → PCT → Loop of Henle → DCT → collecting duct.

The Three Steps of Urine Formation
  1. Glomerular ultrafiltration: the afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent, so glomerular hydrostatic pressure is high (≈60 mmHg). This is opposed by capsule pressure (≈18) and blood oncotic pressure (≈32), giving a net filtration pressure of ≈10 mmHg. Water, glucose, amino acids, urea and ions filter. Blood cells and plasma proteins do not. GFR ≈125 mL/min (≈180 L/day).
  2. Selective reabsorption: the brush-border PCT reabsorbs ≈65% of water and $\text{Na}^+$ and ≈100% of glucose and amino acids by active transport. Glucose appears in urine only if blood glucose exceeds the transport maximum (≈180 mg/dL).
  3. Tubular secretion: $\text{H}^+$, $\text{K}^+$, $\text{NH}_3$ (and drugs) are added to the tubular fluid, regulating blood pH and ion balance.

About 99% of filtrate is reabsorbed, so ≈1.5 L of urine is made per day. It is ≈95% water, with urea as the main solute, and urochrome gives the yellow colour.

4.2 NSEJS Bridge — Counter-Current Mechanism and Osmoregulation

Fig. 27.4 Counter-current multiplier. Numbers beside the limbs are tubular-fluid osmolarity (mOsm/L) at each depth. Water leaves the descending limb and NaCl is pumped out of the ascending limb. Each level maintains a "single effect" of ≈200 mOsm/L, and counter-current flow multiplies this into a 300 → 1200 gradient. The vasa recta preserve it.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — How the Gradient Is Built and Used
  • Descending limb: permeable to water, impermeable to electrolytes. Water leaves into the hypertonic medulla, so the filtrate concentrates to ≈1200 mOsm/L at the tip.
  • Ascending limb: impermeable to water and actively pumps NaCl out, so the filtrate becomes dilute (≈100 mOsm/L at the top). The pumped NaCl is what makes the medulla hypertonic. Pump blocked → no gradient.
  • Vasa recta: blood in the hairpin capillaries gains solute going down and loses it coming up, so it removes water without washing out the gradient.
  • ADH (vasopressin) from the posterior pituitary rises when blood osmolarity rises (dehydration). It inserts aquaporins in the DCT and collecting duct, so water follows the medullary gradient out, giving a small volume of concentrated urine (up to ≈1200 mOsm/L). With no ADH (diabetes insipidus) the urine is large and dilute.
  • ADH opens the water gates; the gradient provides the pull.

4.3 Artificial Kidney (Haemodialysis)

⚠ Exam Trap — Dialysis Is Not Reabsorption

Blood is passed through tubes of cellophane (semipermeable) bathed in dialysing fluid that has the same osmotic pressure and the same glucose and salt levels as plasma but zero nitrogenous waste. Urea and other wastes diffuse down their gradient into the fluid, and the cleaned blood returns to the patient. An anticoagulant (heparin) prevents clotting in the circuit.

There is no selective reabsorption and no tubular secretion in dialysis. Useful solutes are retained only because the dialysing fluid already matches plasma (no gradient to lose them). The real kidney also has ADH control and continuous operation, and a dialyser works for a few hours at a time.

4.4 Excretion in Plants

Plants have no excretory organs. They get rid of:

  • Gaseous waste: $\text{O}_2$ (from photosynthesis) and $\text{CO}_2$ (from respiration) leave by stomata and lenticels, and excess water by transpiration.
  • Shed parts: old leaves and bark carry stored wastes away.
  • Stored wastes: resins and gums in old xylem (heartwood), and calcium oxalate crystals (raphides) in vacuoles.
  • Secreted wastes: some substances are excreted into the surrounding soil.
Physiological Constants and Formula Vault
  • Enzyme pH: salivary amylase ≈6.8; pepsin ≈1.8; pancreatic/intestinal enzymes ≈8 (alkaline).
  • Photosynthesis: $6\text{CO}_2 + 12\text{H}_2\text{O} \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Calvin cycle per glucose: 18 ATP + 12 NADPH.
  • Respiration: aerobic 36–38 ATP; anaerobic 2 ATP. Respiratory quotient $RQ = \dfrac{\text{CO}_2 \text{ released}}{\text{O}_2 \text{ consumed}}$: carbohydrate = 1.0; fat ≈ 0.7 (tripalmitin: $51/72.5$); protein ≈ 0.8–0.9; anaerobic = no $\text{O}_2$ consumed (RQ undefined).
  • Gas partial pressures (mmHg): alveolar $p\text{O}_2 \approx 100$, $p\text{CO}_2 \approx 40$; venous blood $p\text{O}_2 \approx 40$, $p\text{CO}_2 \approx 45$. P50 of Hb ≈ 27 mmHg (right-shifted ≈ 40).
  • Cardiac: cycle 0.8 s (0.1 s atrial systole + 0.3 s ventricular systole + 0.4 s joint diastole); $CO = SV \times HR \approx 70 \times 72 \approx 5\text{ L/min}$; BP 120/80 mmHg; pulse pressure 40 mmHg.
  • Renal: GFR ≈125 mL/min ≈180 L/day; urine ≈1.5 L/day; plasma ≈300 mOsm/L; medullary gradient 300 → 1200 mOsm/L; single effect ≈200 mOsm/L; net filtration pressure $\approx 60 - (18 + 32) = 10\text{ mmHg}$.
  • Nitrogenous wastes: ammonotelic (aquatic bony fish), ureotelic (mammals, adult amphibians), uricotelic (birds, reptiles, insects).

Match List I with List II.

List I: (P) Salivary amylase   (Q) Pepsin   (R) Bile   (S) Succus entericus

(B) P–1, Q–2, R–4, S–3
(C) P–2, Q–1, R–3, S–4

Level 1Experimental MCQExample 2 — Variegated Leaf Test

A potted plant with variegated leaves (green and white patches) is kept in the dark for 48 h, then in bright sunlight for 4 h. A leaf is plucked, boiled in water, then in alcohol (water bath), rinsed and flooded with iodine. What is observed?

(A) The entire leaf turns blue-black
(B) Only the regions that were green turn blue-black
(C) Only the white regions turn blue-black
(D) No region turns blue-black

Step 1. Keeping the plant in the dark first removes existing starch. Any starch found later was made during the sunlight exposure.

Step 2. Photosynthesis, and hence starch formation, needs chlorophyll. White patches lack chlorophyll, so they make no starch and stay brownish. Green patches in the light make starch, which gives the blue-black iodine colour.

Step 3. Boiling in alcohol only removes pigments so the colour can be seen. It does not change which regions contained starch.

Answer: (B). The experiment shows that chlorophyll (and light) is necessary for photosynthesis.

Level 1Single-Correct MCQExample 3 — Fermentation in Yeast

Yeast cells are placed in a sealed sugar solution with no oxygen. Which row correctly gives the site of the first stage of glucose breakdown, the products of fermentation and the net ATP?

(A) Cytoplasm; ethanol + $\text{CO}_2$; 2 ATP
(B) Mitochondria; ethanol + $\text{CO}_2$; 2 ATP
(C) Cytoplasm; lactic acid + $\text{CO}_2$; 2 ATP
(D) Cytoplasm; ethanol + $\text{CO}_2$; 36–38 ATP

Step 1. Glycolysis (glucose → 2 pyruvate) always occurs in the cytoplasm. Eliminate (B).

Step 2. Yeast converts pyruvate to ethanol and $\text{CO}_2$. Lactic acid (no $\text{CO}_2$) is the muscle pathway. Eliminate (C).

Step 3. Without $\text{O}_2$ the Krebs cycle and ETC do not run, so only glycolysis' 2 ATP is made. Eliminate (D).

Answer: (A)

(B) $CO_X = 5.25$ L/min; $CO_Y = 5.0$ L/min; Y's joint diastole = 0.4 s
(C) $CO_X = 5.25$ L/min; $CO_Y = 5.0$ L/min; Y's joint diastole = 1.2 s
(D) $CO_X = 4.2$ L/min; $CO_Y = 5.0$ L/min; Y's joint diastole = 0.8 s

Step 1 — Cardiac output. $CO_X = 70 \times 75 = 5250\text{ mL/min} = 5.25\text{ L/min}$. $CO_Y = 100 \times 50 = 5000\text{ mL/min} = 5.0\text{ L/min}$. Option (D)'s 4.2 L/min comes from wrongly using 60 bpm for X.

Step 2 — Cycle times. $T_X = 60/75 = 0.8\text{ s}$, $T_Y = 60/50 = 1.2\text{ s}$.

Step 3 — Joint diastole. $T - (0.1 + 0.3)$. For Y: $1.2 - 0.4 = 0.8\text{ s}$. Option (B) uses X's 0.4 s, and option (C) forgets to subtract the systoles.

Answer: (A). The athlete's stronger heart gives a larger stroke volume, so the same output needs fewer beats. The cycle lengthens only through the diastolic phase, which gives more filling time.

Level 2Data-Interpretation MCQExample 5 — Bohr Effect in Numbers

100 mL of fully saturated blood carries 20 mL of $\text{O}_2$. Using Fig. 27.1: arterial blood leaving the lungs is ≈97% saturated. At rest, venous blood is ≈75% saturated. In heavy exercise, muscle tissue has $p\text{O}_2 \approx 20$ mmHg. On the normal curve the saturation there is ≈35%, but with the Bohr shift in active muscle it is ≈20%. How much $\text{O}_2$ is unloaded per 100 mL of blood at rest, in exercise with the Bohr shift, and in exercise without it?

(A) 4.4 mL; 15.4 mL; 12.4 mL
(B) 4.4 mL; 12.4 mL; 15.4 mL
(C) 4.4 mL; 19.4 mL; 12.4 mL
(D) 15.4 mL; 4.4 mL; 12.4 mL

Step 1 — Rest. Fraction unloaded = $97\% - 75\% = 22\%$. $0.22 \times 20 = 4.4\text{ mL}$.

Step 2 — Exercise with Bohr shift. $97\% - 20\% = 77\%$. $0.77 \times 20 = 15.4\text{ mL}$.

Step 3 — Exercise without shift. $97\% - 35\% = 62\%$. $0.62 \times 20 = 12.4\text{ mL}$.

Answer: (A). The right shift delivers a further $15.4 - 12.4 = 3.0\text{ mL}$ (≈24% more), exactly in the tissues that need it. Option (B) swaps the two exercise values, which would mean the shift reduced delivery.

Level 2Experimental MCQExample 6 — Girdling Experiment

A ring of bark about 2 cm wide (outer tissues down to the phloem, leaving xylem intact) is removed from a branch of a leafy tree. After several days, which observation is correct?

(A) A bulge forms just above the ring because sucrose moving down from the leaves accumulates there; the leaves above stay turgid for some time
(B) A bulge forms just below the ring because water accumulates after the xylem is cut
(C) The leaves above the ring wilt first because the phloem carries water upward
(D) A bulge forms above the ring because mineral salts moving up in the phloem accumulate there

Step 1. Removing the ring removes the phloem only. The xylem (water and minerals going up) is intact, so leaves above keep getting water and remain turgid, which eliminates (B) and (C).

Step 2. The phloem carries sucrose from the source (leaves) to the sink (roots) downward. At the ring the sugar-rich sap is blocked and piles up above it, causing growth (a bulge). Phloem does not carry minerals upward, which eliminates (D).

Step 3. Below the ring the roots slowly starve (no sucrose), and eventually the whole tree dies.

Answer: (A). The experiment shows that the phloem is the sugar-transport tissue, and that it is distinct from the xylem.

Level 3Data-Interpretation MCQExample 7 — Reading the Cardiac Cycle from Pressures

Pressures (mmHg) are recorded at four instants during one cardiac cycle.

Instant Left atrium Left ventricle Aorta LV pressure trend
P 9 5 80 rising slowly
Q 7 40 80 rising fast
R 6 115 112 about to fall
S 8 55 100 falling fast

Which statement is correct?

(A) At Q both valve sets are closed and the ventricle is rising in pressure; the "lub" has just been produced
(B) At S both valve sets are closed and the ventricle is rising in pressure; the "lub" has just been produced
(C) At R the mitral valve and the aortic valve are both open
(D) At P the aortic valve is open because aortic pressure exceeds ventricular pressure

Rule: a valve opens only when pressure upstream exceeds pressure downstream.

P: LA 9 > LV 5, so the mitral valve is open. Aorta 80 > LV 5, so the aortic valve is closed. This is ventricular filling. Option (D) is false (higher aortic pressure keeps the valve shut).

Q: LV 40 > LA 7, so the mitral valve is closed. LV 40 < aorta 80, so the aortic valve is closed. Both closed with LV pressure rising is isovolumetric contraction, which begins with the closure of the AV valves, the "lub".

R: LV 115 > aorta 112, so the aortic valve is open. LV > LA, so the mitral valve is closed. This is ejection. Option (C) is false.

S: LV 55 < aorta 100, so the aortic valve is closed. LV 55 > LA 8, so the mitral valve is closed. Both are closed with LV pressure falling (isovolumetric relaxation), just after the "dub". Option (B) is false on both the trend and the sound.

Answer: (A)

30-second shortcut: compare two pairs only (LA vs LV for the mitral valve, LV vs aorta for the aortic valve). Then use the LV trend arrow to split the two "all closed" instants: rising = start of systole ("lub"), falling = start of diastole ("dub").
Level 3Analytical MCQExample 8 — Blocking the Ascending-Limb Pump

A loop diuretic blocks the active transport of NaCl out of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle. A dehydrated person with high ADH takes the drug. Which prediction is best supported?

(A) The medullary gradient dissipates, so the urine cannot be concentrated even with high ADH; urine volume rises and its osmolarity falls toward plasma level (≈300 mOsm/L)
(B) The descending limb loses water faster, so the urine becomes more concentrated
(C) The medullary gradient strengthens because NaCl stays in the filtrate, so urine becomes hypertonic
(D) Glomerular filtration stops because the loop of Henle is blocked

Step 1 — Where the gradient comes from. The ascending-limb NaCl pump (ATP-driven) is the "single effect" that makes the interstitium hypertonic. Blocking it means no new solute is deposited in the medulla, and the gradient collapses from 1200 toward 300 mOsm/L.

Step 2 — Effect on the descending limb. Water leaves the descending limb only because the interstitium is hypertonic. With the gradient gone, little water is drawn out, so the filtrate is not concentrated (this refutes (B)).

Step 3 — Effect on the collecting duct. ADH makes the duct permeable to water, but water moves only if the interstitium pulls it. Without the pull, water is not reclaimed, so urine volume rises and its osmolarity falls toward isotonic.

Step 4 — Rejecting the rest. (C) is backwards: retained NaCl in the lumen means it is not in the medulla. (D) confuses the site: filtration occurs in the glomerulus, upstream of the loop.

Answer: (A)

30-second shortcut: the gradient is built by the pump, and ADH only opens the water gates. "Pump blocked ⇒ no gradient ⇒ no concentrated urine, however high the ADH."
Level 3Data-Interpretation MCQExample 9 — A Succulent's Day and Night

A thick-leaved desert succulent is monitored over 24 h.

Measurement 2 AM 2 PM
Stomata Open Closed
Net uptake of $\text{CO}_2$ from air Yes None
Leaf-sap pH 4.2 (acidic) 5.8 (less acidic)
Release of $\text{O}_2$ None High

Which interpretation is best supported?

(A) CAM plant: $\text{CO}_2$ is fixed at night into malic acid; by day, with stomata shut, malic acid releases $\text{CO}_2$ internally for the Calvin cycle while the light reactions supply ATP, NADPH and $\text{O}_2$
(B) An ordinary C3 plant: it opens stomata at night because the light reactions need darkness
(C) The night acidity is lactic acid from anaerobic respiration caused by lack of $\text{O}_2$ in closed leaves
(D) The Calvin cycle runs only at night, and the $\text{O}_2$ released at 2 PM comes from breaking down malic acid

Step 1 — Reading the pattern. $\text{CO}_2$ enters only at night and the leaf sap is most acidic then, so $\text{CO}_2$ is being stored as an organic acid (malic acid). Stomata shut by day while acid falls, so the stored $\text{CO}_2$ is released inside the leaf. This is the CAM signature.

Step 2 — Light reactions need light. $\text{O}_2$ is released at 2 PM because water is photolysed in light. Photolysis cannot occur at night, which refutes (B) and (D).

Step 3 — The "dark reaction". The Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, so it runs in the day. Refuting (D) again, $\text{O}_2$ always comes from water, never from malic acid.

Step 4 — Acid identity. Open stomata at night supply plenty of $\text{O}_2$, and the nocturnal acid is malic acid made by PEP carboxylase. Lactic acid is a muscle product, so (C) is wrong.

Answer: (A)

30-second shortcut: "Acid at dawn + stomata shut at noon ⇒ CAM." Then apply two fixed rules: $\text{O}_2$ is always from water via light, and the Calvin cycle is a light-dependent-supplies process that runs in the daytime.
