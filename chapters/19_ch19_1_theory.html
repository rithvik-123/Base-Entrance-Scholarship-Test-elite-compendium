19.1 Work Done by Constant & Variable Forces

Core Principle • Mechanical Definition of Work

In physics, work is a scalar mechanism of energy transfer produced when a force acts through a displacement of its point of application. For a constant force $\vec{F}$ acting on a particle that undergoes a displacement $\vec{s}$:

$$W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s} = F s \cos\theta$$

where $\theta$ ($0^\circ \le \theta \le 180^\circ$) is the angle between the force vector $\vec{F}$ and the displacement vector $\vec{s}$. Equivalently, $W = (F\cos\theta)\,s = F\,(s\cos\theta)$—the product of the displacement and the force component parallel to it.

  • SI Unit: Joule ($\text{J}$), where $1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} = 1\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$.
  • CGS Unit: Erg, where $1\text{ erg} = 1\text{ dyne}\cdot\text{cm} = 1\text{ g}\cdot\text{cm}^2/\text{s}^2$.
  • Exact Conversion: $1\text{ J} = (10^5\text{ dyne})(10^2\text{ cm}) = 10^7\text{ erg}$.
  • Dimensional Formula: $[W] = [M^1 L^2 T^{-2}]$.

Sign Convention & Physical Meaning

  • Positive Work ($0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ \implies \cos\theta> 0$): The force has a component along the direction of motion and supplies kinetic energy to the body (e.g., gravity acting on a freely falling stone).
  • Zero Work ($\theta = 90^\circ$, or $F = 0$, or $s = 0$): No energy is transferred by that force (e.g., centripetal force in uniform circular motion, or gravity acting on a suitcase carried horizontally on a flat platform).
  • Negative Work ($90^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ \implies \cos\theta < 0$): The force opposes the displacement and extracts kinetic energy from the body (e.g., kinetic friction slowing a sliding crate, or gravity acting on a ball thrown vertically upward).
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Frame-Dependence of Work

A textbook trap in NSEJS and BEST Paper 2 is assuming work is an absolute physical invariant. It is not. Consider an inertial frame $S'$ moving at constant velocity $\vec{v}_0$ relative to ground frame $S$. Between time $0$ and $t$, the force $\vec{F}$ is identical in both frames ($\vec{F}' = \vec{F}$), but the displacement transforms as:

$$\vec{s}' = \vec{s} - \vec{v}_0 t \implies W' = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{s}' = W - (\vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}_0)t$$

Crucial Consequence: Work done by a force depends on the observer's reference frame! Example: A passenger holds a briefcase stationary inside an accelerating train. In the train frame, displacement is zero, so the passenger's hand does zero work. In the ground frame, the briefcase moves forward while the hand pulls it forward, so the hand does positive work.

Work Done by a Variable Force & Signed Area Under the $F\text{-}x$ Graph

When force varies with position, divide the path into infinitesimal displacements $d\vec{r}$ over which $\vec{F}$ is effectively constant. The infinitesimal work is $dW = \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz$. For one-dimensional motion along the $x$-axis from $x_1$ to $x_2$:

$$W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx = \text{Signed Area enclosed between the } F(x)\text{ curve and the } x\text{-axis}$$

Direction Sign Rule: When the particle moves in the $+x$ direction ($dx > 0$), area above the $x$-axis is positive work and area below the $x$-axis is negative work. If the particle reverses and moves in the $-x$ direction ($dx < 0$), both signs invert because $\int_{x_2}^{x_1} F(x)\,dx=-\int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\,dx$.

Figure 19.1: Signed-area interpretation of variable-force work $W = \int F(x)\,dx$. Areas above the $x$-axis add kinetic energy; areas below extract it.

Force-Specific Work Traps: Gravity, Normal Reaction & Friction

Olympiad examiners routinely test whether students rely on false heuristics like "normal force always does zero work" or "friction always does negative work." Both statements are false.

Force Type General Work Expression Common Myth (TRAP) Exact Physical Reality
Gravity ($m\vec{g}$) $W_g = -mg(y_f - y_i) = -mg\Delta h$ "Depends on incline angle $\theta$ or track shape." Strictly path-independent. Whether a block drops vertically, slides down a curved bowl, or descends a rough staircase through height $h$, $W_g = +mgh$.
Normal Reaction ($\vec{N}$) $W_N = \int \vec{N}\cdot d\vec{r}$ "Always zero because $\vec{N}$ is perpendicular to the surface." Zero only on a stationary surface. On a moving elevator floor ascending distance $h$, $W_N = +Nh > 0$. On a movable wedge, $W_N < 0$ on the sliding block and $W_N> 0$ on the wedge (their sum for the rigid pair is $0$).
Static Friction ($\vec{f}_s$) $W_{f_s} = \vec{f}_s \cdot \vec{s}$ "Friction always opposes motion and does negative work." Can be positive, negative, or zero! For a block resting on an accelerating truck bed, $\vec{f}_s$ accelerates the block forward in the ground frame, doing positive work ($W_{f_s} > 0$). Across a non-slipping contact pair, total $W_{f_s,\text{pair}} = 0$.
Kinetic Friction ($\vec{f}_k$) $W_{f_k} = \vec{f}_k \cdot \vec{s}$ "Always negative on every individual block." For a single block sliding on a moving belt faster than the block, $\vec{f}_k$ accelerates the block forward ($W_{f_k} > 0$ on that block). However, for the combined two-body pair, $W_{f_k,\text{total}} = -f_k s_{\text{rel}} < 0$ is always negative (frame-invariant thermal dissipation).

19.2 Kinetic Energy, Momentum Coupling & Stopping Scaling

Core Principle • Kinetic Energy & Linear Momentum Coupling

The translational kinetic energy of a particle of mass $m$ moving at speed $v$ is a non-negative, frame-dependent scalar:

$$K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

Multiplying numerator and denominator by $m$ and substituting linear momentum magnitude $p = mv$ yields the fundamental coupling identities:

$$K = \frac{p^2}{2m} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad p = \sqrt{2mK}$$
  • Equal Momentum ($p_1 = p_2$): $K \propto \dfrac{1}{m}$. The lighter body carries greater kinetic energy (e.g., when a gun fires a bullet, $p_{\text{bullet}} = p_{\text{gun}}$, so $K_{\text{bullet}} \gg K_{\text{gun}}$).
  • Equal Kinetic Energy ($K_1 = K_2$): $p \propto \sqrt{m}$. The heavier body carries greater linear momentum.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • The Exact Percentage-Change Trap

Students frequently apply the small-error calculus approximation $\frac{\Delta K}{K} \approx 2\frac{\Delta p}{p}$ to large percentage changes. This is a lethal error in BEST Paper 2!

Exact Derivation (Momentum changes by $x\%$, where $x$ carries its sign):

$$p_f = p_i\left(1 + \frac{x}{100}\right) \implies \frac{K_f}{K_i} = \left(\frac{p_f}{p_i}\right)^2 = \left(1 + \frac{x}{100}\right)^2 = 1 + \frac{2x}{100} + \frac{x^2}{10000}$$ $$\%\Delta K = \left(\frac{K_f - K_i}{K_i}\right)\times 100\% = \left(2x + \frac{x^2}{100}\right)\%$$
  • If $p$ increases by $+20\%$: $\%\Delta K = 2(20) + \frac{400}{100} = \mathbf{+44\%}$ (NOT $40\%$!).
  • If $p$ decreases by $20\%$ ($x = -20$): $\%\Delta K = 2(-20) + \frac{400}{100} = \mathbf{-36\%}$ (a $36\%$ decrease, NOT $40\%$!).
  • If $p$ increases by $+1\%$: $\%\Delta K = 2(1) + 0.01 = 2.01\% \approx 2\%$ (differential approximation works only for $|x| \lesssim 2\%$).

Reverse Exact Formula (Kinetic Energy changes by $y\%$):

$$\%\Delta p = \left(\sqrt{1 + \frac{y}{100}} - 1\right)\times 100\%$$

For instance, if $K$ increases by $+300\%$, then $K_f = 4K_i \implies p_f = 2p_i \implies p$ increases by $\mathbf{+100\%}$.

Bullet-Penetrating-Planks & Stopping-Distance Scaling

Consider a vehicle braking or a bullet piercing uniform wood under a constant average resistive force $F_r$. All initial kinetic energy is dissipated by negative work $-F_r s$:

$$-F_r s = 0 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 \implies s = \frac{mv_0^2}{2F_r} \propto v_0^2$$

Doubling a car's speed quadruples its braking distance ($4s$); tripling it increases stopping distance ninefold ($9s$).

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Exact Plank-Counting Formula

Suppose a bullet loses $\dfrac{1}{n}$-th of its speed while passing through a single wooden plank of thickness $d$ (offering constant resistive force $F_r$). How many identical planks $N$ are required to bring the bullet to rest?

First-Principles Derivation: After 1 plank, speed is $v_1 = v_0\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)$. The kinetic energy lost inside 1 plank is:

$$\Delta K_1 = \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2\left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^2 = K_0\left[1 - \left(1 - \frac{2}{n} + \frac{1}{n^2}\right)\right] = K_0\left(\frac{2n - 1}{n^2}\right)$$

Since each plank of thickness $d$ extracts the same energy $\Delta K_1 = F_r d$ (regardless of speed, because $F_r$ is constant!), the exact number of planks needed to absorb the entire $K_0$ is:

$$N = \frac{K_0}{\Delta K_1} = \frac{n^2}{2n - 1}$$

Integer Ceiling Rule: Because $N$ is almost never an integer (e.g., if $n = 10$, $N = \frac{100}{19} \approx 5.26$), the bullet penetrates $5$ full planks and stops inside the $6\text{th}$ plank. Thus, the minimum number of whole planks required to stop it is $\lceil N \rceil = \mathbf{6}$ (not $5$, and certainly not $10$!). Contrast: If a bullet loses $\frac{1}{n}$-th of its kinetic energy per plank, then $N = n$ exactly.

19.3 Potential Energy, Conservative Forces & Equilibrium Stability

Core Principle • Conservative Forces & Potential Energy

A force $\vec{F}_c$ is conservative if the work it performs moving a particle between any two points $A$ and $B$ depends solely on the initial and final positions and is completely independent of the path taken. Equivalently, work done around any closed loop is identically zero:

$$\oint \vec{F}_c \cdot d\vec{r} = 0$$

Potential Energy ($U$) is stored energy of configuration, defined exclusively for conservative forces via the negative of the internal conservative work:

$$\Delta U = U_f - U_i = -W_{\text{cons}} = -\int_{\vec{r}_i}^{\vec{r}_f} \vec{F}_c \cdot d\vec{r}$$

Notice that only changes in potential energy ($\Delta U$) have physical meaning; the zero-reference datum ($U = 0$) can be chosen anywhere convenient.

1. Gravitational Potential Energy ($U_g$)

Near Earth's surface (uniform gravitational field $g$), choosing a horizontal reference plane $y = 0$ where $U_g(0) = 0$, lifting a point mass $m$ to height $h$ gives $W_g = -mgh$, so:

$$U_g(h) = mgh$$

For an extended rigid or flexible body (rod, chain, water column), gravitational potential energy is calculated by concentrating its entire mass $M$ at its Center of Mass (CM): $U_g = M g y_{\text{CM}}$.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Pulling a Hanging Chain onto a Table

A uniform chain of mass $M$ and length $L$ rests on a smooth horizontal table with $\dfrac{1}{n}$-th of its length hanging vertically over the edge. Find the minimum work done by an external agent to pull the hanging portion completely onto the table slowly ($\Delta K = 0$).

Method 1 (Center-of-Mass Energy Accounting): Set the tabletop as the $U = 0$ datum. The hanging segment has mass $m_h = \frac{M}{n}$ and length $l_h = \frac{L}{n}$. Because the chain is uniform, the CM of the hanging portion lies at its midpoint—a depth $y_{\text{CM}} = \frac{l_h}{2} = \frac{L}{2n}$ below the tabletop.

$$U_i = -m_h g y_{\text{CM}} = -\left(\frac{M}{n}\right)g\left(\frac{L}{2n}\right) = -\frac{MgL}{2n^2}, \qquad U_f = 0$$ $$W_{\text{ext}} = \Delta U = U_f - U_i = \frac{MgL}{2n^2}$$

Method 2 (Exact Calculus Verification): When a length $y$ remains hanging, the downward weight pulling the chain is $F(y) = \left(\frac{M}{L}y\right)g$. Pulling an element $dy$ onto the table requires $dW = F(y)\,dy$. Integrating from $y = 0$ to $y = L/n$:

$$W_{\text{ext}} = \int_0^{L/n} \frac{Mg}{L}y\,dy = \frac{Mg}{L}\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^{L/n} = \frac{MgL}{2n^2}$$

Generalization: To pull the chain slowly from hanging length $L/n_1$ to a shorter hanging length $L/n_2$ ($n_2 > n_1$):

$$W_{\text{ext}} = \frac{MgL}{2}\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$$

2. Elastic Potential Energy of an Ideal Spring ($U_s$)

An ideal massless spring of stiffness (force constant) $k$ obeys Hooke's Law: $F_s = -kx$, where $x$ is the displacement (extension or compression) measured strictly from the spring's natural (unstrained) length. The work done by the spring force when the free end moves from $x_1$ to $x_2$ is:

$$W_s = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} (-kx)\,dx = -\left[\frac{1}{2}kx^2\right]_{x_1}^{x_2} = \frac{1}{2}k(x_1^2 - x_2^2)$$

Taking $U_s(0) = 0$ at natural length, the elastic potential energy stored at deformation $x$ is $U_s(x) = \dfrac{1}{2}kx^2$ (always $\ge 0$, identical for both stretching $+x$ and compression $-x$). Consequently, the work required by an external agent to deform the spring slowly from $x_1$ to $x_2$ is:

$$W_{\text{ext}} = \Delta U_s = \frac{1}{2}k(x_2^2 - x_1^2) = \frac{1}{2}k(x_2 - x_1)(x_2 + x_1)$$
Critical Exam Trap • Incremental Spring Stretching

If a spring is already stretched by $x_1$ and you stretch it by an additional $\Delta x$ (so $x_2 = x_1 + \Delta x$), the extra work is NOT $\frac{1}{2}k(\Delta x)^2$! Because the spring already pulls back with force $kx_1$ at the start of the second stretch, the true additional work is:

$$W_{\text{ext}} = \frac{1}{2}k\left[(x_1 + \Delta x)^2 - x_1^2\right] = \frac{1}{2}k(\Delta x)^2 + k x_1 \Delta x > \frac{1}{2}k(\Delta x)^2$$

For example, stretching from $0 \to d$ takes $W_1 = \frac{1}{2}kd^2$, whereas stretching further from $d \to 2d$ takes $W_2 = \frac{1}{2}k(4d^2 - d^2) = \frac{3}{2}kd^2 = \mathbf{3W_1}$!

3. Conservative Force from Potential Energy & Equilibrium Stability

In one dimension, inverting $\Delta U = -\int F_x\,dx$ yields the local differential relation between conservative force and potential energy:

$$F_x = -\frac{dU}{dx}$$

Geometrically, the conservative force equals the negative slope of the $U(x)$ graph—it always pushes a particle "downhill" toward lower potential energy. Equilibrium occurs where net force vanishes: $F_x = 0 \iff \dfrac{dU}{dx} = 0$.

Equilibrium Type Derivative Conditions $U(x)$ Graph Shape Response to Small Displacement
Stable Equilibrium $\dfrac{dU}{dx} = 0$ and $\dfrac{d^2U}{dx^2} > 0$ Local minimum (valley / bowl) Restoring force pulls particle back toward equilibrium (oscillations occur).
Unstable Equilibrium $\dfrac{dU}{dx} = 0$ and $\dfrac{d^2U}{dx^2} < 0$ Local maximum (hilltop / inverted bowl) Force pushes particle away from equilibrium; it never returns.
Neutral Equilibrium $\dfrac{dU}{dx} = 0$ and $\dfrac{d^2U}{dx^2} = 0$ (locally) Flat horizontal plateau ($U = \text{const}$) Force remains zero at nearby points; particle stays wherever placed.

19.4 Work–Energy Theorem & Conservation of Mechanical Energy

Universal Weapon • The Work–Energy Theorem (WET)

First-Principles Derivation from Newton's Second Law: Along a trajectory with net tangential force $F_{\text{net}}$:

$$F_{\text{net}} = m\frac{dv}{dt} = m\frac{dv}{dx}\frac{dx}{dt} = mv\frac{dv}{dx} \implies F_{\text{net}}\,dx = mv\,dv$$

Integrating from initial state $(x_i, v_i)$ to final state $(x_f, v_f)$:

$$W_{\text{all forces}} = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_{\text{net}}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_i^2 = \Delta K$$

Decomposing all forces acting on the particle into conservative, non-conservative, external, and (if using a non-inertial frame) pseudo forces:

$$W_{\text{cons}} + W_{\text{non-cons}} + W_{\text{ext}} + W_{\text{pseudo}} = \Delta K$$

Why WET Is Superior to Kinematics: The Work–Energy Theorem holds for curved paths, variable forces, friction, air drag, springs, and non-inertial frames (as long as $W_{\text{pseudo}}$ is included). You never need intermediate accelerations or time intervals.

Conservation of Mechanical Energy (COME)

Substituting $W_{\text{cons}} = -\Delta U = -(U_f - U_i)$ into the Work–Energy Theorem (in an inertial frame) gives the general mechanical-energy accounting equation:

$$W_{\text{non-cons}} + W_{\text{ext}} = \Delta K + \Delta U = \Delta E_{\text{mech}}$$

Whenever $W_{\text{non-cons}} + W_{\text{ext}} = 0$ (for example, no friction and no external push, or normal reaction doing zero work on a fixed smooth track), total mechanical energy $E = K + U$ is strictly conserved:

$$K_i + U_i = K_f + U_f$$

Rough-Track & Spring-Collision Accounting

When a block of mass $m$ enters a horizontal rough patch ($\mu_k$) at speed $v_0$ and compresses a horizontal spring of stiffness $k$ up to maximum compression $x_{\max}$ (where instantaneous velocity $v_f = 0$), both spring force and kinetic friction oppose motion throughout compression:

$$W_s + W_{f_k} = \Delta K \implies -\frac{1}{2}kx_{\max}^2 - \mu_k mg x_{\max} = 0 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2$$ $$\frac{1}{2}kx_{\max}^2 + \mu_k mg x_{\max} - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = 0$$

Rebound Condition: At $x_{\max}$, the block stops momentarily. It rebounds back only if the spring's restoring force exceeds maximum static friction: $kx_{\max} > \mu_s mg$. Otherwise, it remains permanently stuck in the compressed position!

19.5 Power, Efficiency, Fluid Pumps & Constant-Power Motion

Core Principle • Average & Instantaneous Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred:

$$P_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta W}{\Delta t}, \qquad P_{\text{inst}} = \frac{dW}{dt} = \frac{\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}}{dt} = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v} = Fv\cos\theta$$
  • SI Unit: Watt ($\text{W}$), where $1\text{ W} = 1\text{ J/s}$. Dimensional formula: $[P] = [M^1 L^2 T^{-3}]$.
  • Horsepower (Mechanical): $1\text{ HP} = 746\text{ W}$.
  • Commercial Unit of ENERGY (NOT Power!): Kilowatt-hour ($\text{kWh}$, or 1 electrical "unit"): $$1\text{ kWh} = (1000\text{ J/s})(3600\text{ s}) = 3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}$$
  • Efficiency ($\eta$): Ratio of useful output power to total input power drawn: $$\eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} = \frac{E_{\text{useful}}}{E_{\text{input}}} \quad (\text{or } \times 100\%)$$

Motor / Water Pump Lifting and Ejecting Fluid

Suppose a pump draws water from depth $h$ at mass flow rate $\dfrac{dm}{dt}$ (in $\text{kg/s}$) and discharges it from a pipe at exit velocity $v$. Each mass element $dm$ gains gravitational PE $(dm)gh$ and kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}(dm)v^2$. Dividing by $dt$ yields the useful output power:

$$P_{\text{out}} = \frac{dm}{dt}\left(gh + \frac{1}{2}v^2\right)$$

For a pipe of cross-sectional area $A$ discharging fluid of density $\rho$ at speed $v$, the mass flow rate is $\dfrac{dm}{dt} = \rho A v$. For a horizontal nozzle ($h = 0$):

$$P_{\text{jet}} = \frac{1}{2}(\rho A v)v^2 = \frac{1}{2}\rho A v^3 \implies P_{\text{jet}} \propto v^3$$

Exam Trap: To double the discharge speed (and thus double the volume flow rate $Av$) through the same horizontal pipe, the pump must deliver $2^3 = \mathbf{8\text{ times}}$ the power—not $2$ or $4$ times!

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Constant-Power Motion Scaling ($v \propto t^{1/2},\ x \propto t^{3/2}$)

A vehicle or particle of mass $m$ starts from rest ($v = 0, x = 0$ at $t = 0$) along a straight line under a machine delivering constant power $P$. Unlike constant-force motion ($a = \text{const}$), here acceleration decreases as speed grows!

Step 1 — Velocity vs. Time:

$$P = Fv = m\frac{dv}{dt}\cdot v \implies v\,dv = \frac{P}{m}\,dt$$

Integrating from $(0,0)$ to $(t,v)$:

$$\int_0^v v\,dv = \int_0^t \frac{P}{m}\,dt \implies \frac{v^2}{2} = \frac{P}{m}t \implies v(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m}}\,t^{1/2} \quad \left(v \propto t^{1/2}\right)$$

Step 2 — Displacement vs. Time:

$$x(t) = \int_0^t v(t)\,dt = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m}}\int_0^t t^{1/2}\,dt = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m}}\cdot\frac{2}{3}t^{3/2} = \sqrt{\frac{8P}{9m}}\,t^{3/2} \quad \left(x \propto t^{3/2}\right)$$

Step 3 — Force & Velocity-Position Scaling:

$$F(t) = \frac{P}{v(t)} = \sqrt{\frac{mP}{2}}\,t^{-1/2} \propto t^{-1/2}, \qquad v(x) = \left(\frac{3Px}{m}\right)^{1/3} \propto x^{1/3}$$

Check $v(x)$ independently: $P = m v \frac{dv}{dx} \cdot v = mv^2\frac{dv}{dx} \implies \int_0^v v^2\,dv = \int_0^x \frac{P}{m}\,dx \implies \frac{v^3}{3} = \frac{Px}{m} \implies v = \left(\frac{3Px}{m}\right)^{1/3}$. Exact match!

19.6 Olympiad Bridge: Vertical Circular Motion (String vs. Rigid Rod)

In horizontal uniform circular motion, speed is constant. In vertical circular motion under gravity, speed decreases as the bob rises and increases as it falls—making it a classic coupled test of Newton's Second Law (radial dynamics) and Conservation of Mechanical Energy.

First-Principles Derivation • Particle on a Light Inextensible String

A bob of mass $m$ tied to a light string of length $R$ is launched horizontally from its lowest point $A$ with speed $v_A = v_0$. Let $P$ be an arbitrary position where the string makes angle $\theta$ with the downward vertical.

1. Energy Conservation ($A \to P$): Taking $U = 0$ at $A$, the height of $P$ is $h = R(1 - \cos\theta)$:

$$\frac{1}{2}mv_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgR(1 - \cos\theta) \implies v^2(\theta) = v_0^2 - 2gR(1 - \cos\theta)$$

2. Radial Equation of Motion (toward center $O$): Resolving $mg$ into radial outward ($mg\cos\theta$) and tangential ($mg\sin\theta$) components:

$$T - mg\cos\theta = \frac{mv^2}{R}$$

Substituting $v^2(\theta)$ into the radial equation yields the master tension formula at any angle $\theta$:

$$T(\theta) = \frac{m}{R}\left[v_0^2 - 2gR(1 - \cos\theta)\right] + mg\cos\theta = \frac{mv_0^2}{R} - 2mg + 3mg\cos\theta$$
Figure 19.4: Vertical circular motion dynamics showing critical points $A, B, C$, general angle $\theta$ force resolution, and critical thresholds for strings vs. rigid rods.

Critical Loop Conditions & The Universal $6mg$ Tension Invariant

  1. Minimum Speed to Loop the Loop (String): A string can only pull ($T \ge 0$). Since $\cos\theta$ is smallest at the top point $C$ ($\theta = 180^\circ, \cos\theta = -1$), tension is minimum at $C$. Setting $T_C \ge 0$: $$T_C = \frac{mv_C^2}{R} - mg \ge 0 \implies v_{C,\min} = \sqrt{gR}$$ By COME from $A$ to $C$ ($\Delta h = 2R$): $$v_A^2 = v_C^2 + 4gR \ge 5gR \implies v_{A,\min} = \sqrt{5gR}$$ And at mid-height $B$ ($\Delta h = R$): $$v_B^2 = v_A^2 - 2gR \ge 3gR \implies v_{B,\min} = \sqrt{3gR}$$ At this critical launch ($v_A = \sqrt{5gR}$), the exact tensions are $T_C = 0$, $T_B = 3mg$, and $T_A = 6mg$.
  2. Universal Tension Difference ($T_{\text{bottom}} - T_{\text{top}} = 6mg$): For any launch speed $v_A \ge \sqrt{5gR}$ (even $v_A = 10\sqrt{gR}$!): $$T_A = \frac{mv_A^2}{R} + mg, \qquad T_C = \frac{mv_C^2}{R} - mg = \frac{m(v_A^2 - 4gR)}{R} - mg = \frac{mv_A^2}{R} - 5mg$$ Subtracting eliminates $v_A$ completely: $$T_A - T_C = 6mg \qquad \text{(and } T_A - T_B = 3mg,\ T_B - T_C = 3mg\text{)}$$
  3. Three Complete Motion Regimes (String):
    • Oscillation ($0 < v_0 \le \sqrt{2gR}$): Velocity vanishes ($v = 0$) at or below mid-height $B$ while $T = mg\cos\theta \ge 0$. Maximum height reached is $h_{\max} = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2g} \le R$.
    • String Slackening & Free-Flight Parabola ($\sqrt{2gR} < v_0 < \sqrt{5gR}$): The bob crosses $B$ ($v_B > 0$), enters the upper hemisphere ($\cos\theta < 0$), and the string goes slack ($T=0$) while $v> 0$. Setting $T(\theta_s) = 0$: $$\frac{mv_0^2}{R} - 2mg + 3mg\cos\theta_s = 0 \implies \cos\theta_s = \frac{2gR - v_0^2}{3gR}$$ Hence the exact slackening height measured vertically above lowest point $A$ is: $$h_s = R(1 - \cos\theta_s) = R\left(1 - \frac{2gR - v_0^2}{3gR}\right) = \frac{v_0^2 + gR}{3g}$$ Beyond this instant, the bob leaves the circular arc and follows a free parabolic projectile trajectory.
    • Complete Vertical Circle ($v_0 \ge \sqrt{5gR}$): String remains taut throughout the entire loop.

Critical Contrast: Light String vs. Massless Rigid Rod (or Smooth Closed Tube)

Unlike a string, a massless rigid rod (or a bead threaded on a smooth circular wire, or a ball inside a smooth double-walled circular tube) can sustain compression—it can push outward on the bob at the top ($T$ or $N$ can be negative). Therefore, the bob does not need $v_C = \sqrt{gR}$; it only needs to reach the top with $v_C \to 0^+$!

Level 1 Variable Force & F–x Graph Worked Example 1: Signed Area & Final Speed
A particle of mass $m = 2\text{ kg}$ starts from rest at $x = 0$ and moves along the $+x$-axis under a variable force $F(x)$ whose profile is identical to Figure 19.1:
  • From $x = 0$ to $x = 4\text{ m}$, $F$ increases linearly from $0$ to $+20\text{ N}$.
  • From $x = 4\text{ m}$ to $x = 8\text{ m}$, $F$ stays constant at $+20\text{ N}$.
  • From $x = 8\text{ m}$ to $x = 10\text{ m}$, $F$ decreases linearly from $+20\text{ N}$ to $0$.
  • From $x = 10\text{ m}$ to $x = 14\text{ m}$, $F$ decreases linearly from $0$ to $-20\text{ N}$.
Calculate (a) the net work done on the particle from $x = 0$ to $x = 14\text{ m}$, and (b) its speed at $x = 14\text{ m}$.

Step 1 — Decompose the Signed Area Under $F(x)$: Because the particle moves in the $+x$ direction throughout, area above the $x$-axis contributes positive work and area below contributes negative work.

  • Interval $[0, 4\text{ m}]$ (triangle): $W_1 = +\frac{1}{2}(4\text{ m})(20\text{ N}) = +40\text{ J}$.
  • Interval $[4\text{ m}, 8\text{ m}]$ (rectangle): $W_2 = +(4\text{ m})(20\text{ N}) = +80\text{ J}$.
  • Interval $[8\text{ m}, 10\text{ m}]$ (triangle): $W_3 = +\frac{1}{2}(2\text{ m})(20\text{ N}) = +20\text{ J}$.
  • Verification via Trapezoid Formula on $[0, 10\text{ m}]$: $A_{\text{pos}} = \frac{1}{2}(\text{base}_1 + \text{base}_2)(\text{height}) = \frac{1}{2}(10 + 4)(20) = +140\text{ J}$.
  • Interval $[10\text{ m}, 14\text{ m}]$ (triangle below axis): $W_4 = -\frac{1}{2}(4\text{ m})(20\text{ N}) = -40\text{ J}$.

(a) Net Work Done:

$$W_{\text{net}} = W_1 + W_2 + W_3 + W_4 = 40 + 80 + 20 - 40 = \mathbf{+100\text{ J}}$$

(b) Speed at $x = 14\text{ m}$ via Work–Energy Theorem: Since $v_i = 0$ at $x = 0$:

$$W_{\text{net}} = \frac{1}{2}mv_f^2 - 0 \implies 100 = \frac{1}{2}(2)v_f^2 \implies v_f^2 = 100 \implies v_f = \mathbf{10\text{ m/s}}$$
Level 1 KE–Momentum Coupling Worked Example 2: Exact Percentage-Change Calculations
(a) If the linear momentum of a moving body is increased by $20\%$, calculate the exact percentage increase in its kinetic energy.
(b) Conversely, if the kinetic energy of another body decreases by $19\%$, find the exact percentage decrease in its linear momentum.

(a) Momentum increases by $+20\%$: Let initial momentum be $p_i$ and initial kinetic energy be $K_i = \dfrac{p_i^2}{2m}$. Then:

$$p_f = p_i + 0.20\,p_i = 1.20\,p_i$$ $$K_f = \frac{p_f^2}{2m} = \frac{(1.20\,p_i)^2}{2m} = 1.44\left(\frac{p_i^2}{2m}\right) = 1.44\,K_i$$ $$\%\text{ Increase in } K = \frac{K_f - K_i}{K_i}\times 100\% = (1.44 - 1)\times 100\% = \mathbf{44\%}$$

Trap Check: Using the infinitesimal rule $2 \times 20\% = 40\%$ misses the quadratic $+4\%$ term.

(b) Kinetic energy decreases by $19\%$: Using $p = \sqrt{2mK}$:

$$K_f = (1 - 0.19)K_i = 0.81\,K_i \implies p_f = \sqrt{2m(0.81\,K_i)} = \sqrt{0.81}\,\sqrt{2mK_i} = 0.90\,p_i$$ $$\%\text{ Decrease in } p = \frac{p_i - p_f}{p_i}\times 100\% = (1 - 0.90)\times 100\% = \mathbf{10\%}$$
Level 1 Power, Efficiency & Units Worked Example 3: Water Pump Power & Commercial Energy
An electric water pump of efficiency $\eta = 80\%$ lifts $300\text{ kg}$ of water per minute from a well $11\text{ m}$ deep and ejects it horizontally through a nozzle at $10\text{ m/s}$ (take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$). Calculate: (a) the useful output power, (b) the electrical input power in watts and horsepower, and (c) the electrical energy consumed in $\text{kWh}$ and joules during $4\text{ hours}$ of continuous operation.

Step 1 — Convert Mass Flow Rate to SI Units:

$$\frac{dm}{dt} = \frac{300\text{ kg}}{60\text{ s}} = 5\text{ kg/s}$$

(a) Useful Output Power: Each kilogram of water gains gravitational potential energy $gh$ and kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}v^2$:

$$P_{\text{out}} = \frac{dm}{dt}\left(gh + \frac{1}{2}v^2\right) = 5\left(10 \times 11 + \frac{1}{2}\times 10^2\right) = 5(110 + 50) = 5 \times 160 = \mathbf{800\text{ W}}$$

(b) Electrical Input Power: Using $\eta = \dfrac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}} = 0.80$:

$$P_{\text{in}} = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{\eta} = \frac{800\text{ W}}{0.80} = \mathbf{1000\text{ W}} = 1.0\text{ kW}$$

In mechanical horsepower ($1\text{ HP} = 746\text{ W}$):

$$P_{\text{in}} = \frac{1000}{746}\text{ HP} \approx \mathbf{1.34\text{ HP}}$$

(c) Electrical Energy Consumed in $4\text{ hours}$: Always use input electrical power to compute electricity consumption:

$$E = P_{\text{in}} \times t = 1.0\text{ kW} \times 4\text{ h} = \mathbf{4\text{ kWh}}$$ $$E = 4 \times (3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}) = \mathbf{1.44 \times 10^7\text{ J}}$$

Level 2 Elastic & Chain Potential Energy Worked Example 4: Incremental Spring Stretch & Hanging Chain Work
Solve two classic potential-energy accounting scenarios ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$):
(a) A light ideal spring of stiffness $k = 400\text{ N/m}$ is already stretched by $5\text{ cm}$ from its natural length. Find the additional work required by an external agent to stretch it slowly by a further $10\text{ cm}$.
(b) A uniform chain of mass $M = 6\text{ kg}$ and length $L = 4\text{ m}$ lies on a smooth horizontal table with $25\%$ of its length hanging vertically over the edge. Find the minimum work required to pull the hanging portion completely onto the table.

(a) Incremental Spring Stretch: Convert all extensions to metres from the natural length:

$$x_1 = 5\text{ cm} = 0.05\text{ m}, \qquad x_2 = 5\text{ cm} + 10\text{ cm} = 15\text{ cm} = 0.15\text{ m}$$

Since the stretching is slow ($\Delta K = 0$), external work equals the exact increase in stored elastic potential energy:

$$W_{\text{ext}} = \frac{1}{2}k(x_2^2 - x_1^2) = \frac{1}{2}(400)\left[(0.15)^2 - (0.05)^2\right] = 200(0.0225 - 0.0025) = 200(0.0200) = \mathbf{4.0\text{ J}}$$

Trap Check: Plugging $\Delta x = 0.10\text{ m}$ into $\frac{1}{2}k(\Delta x)^2$ wrongly gives $2.0\text{ J}$—half the true value.

(b) Pulling the Hanging Chain: Hanging fraction is $25\% = \dfrac{1}{4}$, so $n = 4$.

  • Mass of hanging segment: $m_h = \dfrac{M}{4} = \dfrac{6}{4} = 1.5\text{ kg}$.
  • Length of hanging segment: $l_h = \dfrac{L}{4} = \dfrac{4}{4} = 1.0\text{ m}$.
  • Depth of CM of hanging segment below tabletop ($U = 0$ datum): $y_{\text{CM}} = \dfrac{l_h}{2} = 0.5\text{ m}$.

Minimum work ($\Delta K = 0$) lifts this mass $m_h$ through vertical height $y_{\text{CM}}$:

$$W_{\text{ext}} = U_f - U_i = 0 - (-m_h g y_{\text{CM}}) = (1.5)(10)(0.5) = \mathbf{7.5\text{ J}}$$

Cross-check via closed-form formula: $W_{\text{ext}} = \dfrac{MgL}{2n^2} = \dfrac{6 \times 10 \times 4}{2 \times 4^2} = \dfrac{240}{32} = 7.5\text{ J}$. Exact match.

Level 2 Work–Energy Theorem & Friction Worked Example 5: Rough Incline & Spring Compression Accounting
A block of mass $m = 3\text{ kg}$ is released from rest on a rough incline of angle $\theta = 30^\circ$ ($\mu_k = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}$). It slides a distance $d = 4\text{ m}$ down the incline, curves smoothly onto a horizontal floor, and compresses an ideal horizontal spring of force constant $k = 80\text{ N/m}$ (take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$).
(a) If the horizontal floor is frictionless, find the maximum compression $x_{\max}$ of the spring.
(b) If instead the horizontal floor has kinetic friction $\mu_h = 0.6$ starting right at the uncompressed end of the spring, find the new maximum compression $x'_{\max}$.

Step 1 — Energy Accounting Along the Rough Incline:

  • Vertical height descended: $h = d\sin 30^\circ = 4 \times 0.5 = 2.0\text{ m}$.
  • Work done by gravity: $W_g = +mgh = (3)(10)(2.0) = +60\text{ J}$.
  • Normal reaction on incline: $N = mg\cos 30^\circ = (3)(10)\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = 15\sqrt{3}\text{ N}$.
  • Kinetic friction on incline: $f_k = \mu_k N = \left(\dfrac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}\right)(15\sqrt{3}) = 5\text{ N}$.
  • Work done by incline friction: $W_{f,\text{inc}} = -f_k d = -(5)(4) = -20\text{ J}$.

Thus, the kinetic energy with which the block reaches the uncompressed spring is $K_0 = W_g + W_{f,\text{inc}} = 60 - 20 = 40\text{ J}$.

(a) Frictionless Horizontal Floor: Apply WET from release ($v_i = 0$) to maximum compression ($v_f = 0$):

$$W_g + W_{f,\text{inc}} + W_s = \Delta K = 0 \implies 60 - 20 - \frac{1}{2}k x_{\max}^2 = 0$$ $$\frac{1}{2}(80)x_{\max}^2 = 40 \implies 40x_{\max}^2 = 40 \implies x_{\max} = \mathbf{1.0\text{ m}}$$

(b) Rough Horizontal Floor ($\mu_h = 0.6$): Friction force on the horizontal floor is $f_h = \mu_h mg = (0.6)(3)(10) = 18\text{ N}$, doing negative work $W_{f,h} = -18 x'_{\max}$ during compression. Applying WET from release to $v_f = 0$:

$$40 - \frac{1}{2}(80)(x'_{\max})^2 - 18 x'_{\max} = 0$$ $$40(x'_{\max})^2 + 18 x'_{\max} - 40 = 0 \implies 20(x'_{\max})^2 + 9 x'_{\max} - 20 = 0$$

Factorizing the quadratic ($20 \times (-20) = -400 = (+25)(-16)$, and $25 - 16 = +9$):

$$(5x'_{\max} - 4)(4x'_{\max} + 5) = 0$$

Since compression $x'_{\max} > 0$, we reject $-\frac{5}{4}$ and obtain:

$$x'_{\max} = \frac{4}{5}\text{ m} = \mathbf{0.8\text{ m}}\ (80\text{ cm})$$
Level 2 Constant-Power Calculus Worked Example 6: Car Accelerating at Constant Power
A sports car of mass $m = 1000\text{ kg}$ starts from rest ($v = 0, x = 0$ at $t = 0$) on a flat straight track while its engine delivers a constant useful power $P = 18\text{ kW}$ (neglecting drag and rolling resistance). Derive from first principles: (a) its speed at $t = 4\text{ s}$, (b) the distance covered in the first $4\text{ s}$, and (c) the forward traction force at $t = 4\text{ s}$.

Given: $m = 1000\text{ kg}$, $P = 18000\text{ W}$, so $\dfrac{P}{m} = 18\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^3$ and $\dfrac{2P}{m} = 36\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^3$.

(a) Speed at $t = 4\text{ s}$: From $P = Fv = mv\dfrac{dv}{dt}$:

$$\int_0^v v\,dv = \int_0^t \frac{P}{m}\,dt \implies \frac{v^2}{2} = \frac{P}{m}t \implies v(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m}}\,t^{1/2} = \sqrt{36}\,t^{1/2} = 6\sqrt{t}\text{ m/s}$$

At $t = 4\text{ s}$:

$$v(4) = 6\sqrt{4} = \mathbf{12\text{ m/s}}$$

(b) Distance Covered in First $4\text{ s}$: Since $v = \dfrac{dx}{dt} = 6t^{1/2}$:

$$x(t) = \int_0^t 6t^{1/2}\,dt = 6\left[\frac{t^{3/2}}{3/2}\right]_0^t = 4t^{3/2}\text{ m}$$

At $t = 4\text{ s}$ (noting $4^{3/2} = (\sqrt{4})^3 = 8$):

$$x(4) = 4(8) = \mathbf{32\text{ m}}$$

Warning: Do not use $x = \frac{1}{2}v_f t = 24\text{ m}$—that formula is valid only for constant acceleration, whereas here $a(t) = 3t^{-1/2}$ is non-uniform!

(c) Driving Force at $t = 4\text{ s}$: From instantaneous power $P = Fv$:

$$F(4) = \frac{P}{v(4)} = \frac{18000\text{ W}}{12\text{ m/s}} = \mathbf{1500\text{ N}}$$

Energy Consistency Check: Total work delivered $W = Pt = 18000 \times 4 = 72000\text{ J}$, which matches $K = \frac{1}{2}(1000)(12)^2 = 72000\text{ J}$ identically.

Level 3: Rank-Booster & Olympiad Mastery (Examples 7 – 9)
Level 3 Vertical Circular Motion & Projectile Worked Example 7: String Slackening Height & Post-Slack Apex
A small bob of mass $m = 0.5\text{ kg}$ is suspended from a fixed pivot by a light inextensible string of length $R = 1.8\text{ m}$. At its lowest point $A$, it is projected horizontally with speed $v_0 = \sqrt{3.5\,gR}$ (take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$).
(a) Determine its motion regime.
(b) Calculate the exact angle $\theta_s$ from the downward vertical, the vertical height $h_s$ above $A$, and the speed $v_s$ at the instant the string goes slack.
(c) Find the maximum vertical height $H_{\max}$ above $A$ reached by the bob during its subsequent free flight.

(a) Regime Diagnosis: Comparing $v_0 = \sqrt{3.5\,gR}$ against the critical string thresholds $\sqrt{2gR}$ and $\sqrt{5gR}$:

$$\sqrt{2gR} < \sqrt{3.5\,gR} < \sqrt{5gR}$$

Therefore, the bob crosses mid-height $B$, enters the upper hemisphere, and the string goes slack before reaching the top $C$, launching the bob into a parabolic free-flight trajectory.

(b) Slackening Position & Speed (Full Derivation): At angle $\theta$ from the downward vertical, COME gives $v^2 = v_0^2 - 2gR(1 - \cos\theta)$ and the radial equation is $T - mg\cos\theta = \dfrac{mv^2}{R}$. Setting $T = 0$ at $\theta = \theta_s$:

$$-mg\cos\theta_s = \frac{m}{R}\left[3.5\,gR - 2gR(1 - \cos\theta_s)\right]$$

Dividing both sides by $mg$:

$$-\cos\theta_s = 3.5 - 2 + 2\cos\theta_s \implies -3\cos\theta_s = 1.5 \implies \cos\theta_s = -\frac{1}{2}$$

Hence $\theta_s = \mathbf{120^\circ}$ from the downward vertical (i.e., $30^\circ$ above the horizontal diameter). The slackening height above $A$ is:

$$h_s = R(1 - \cos 120^\circ) = R\left(1 - \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right) = 1.5\,R = 1.5(1.8) = \mathbf{2.7\text{ m}}$$

At $T = 0$, the radial equation immediately yields $v_s^2 = -gR\cos\theta_s$:

$$v_s^2 = -gR\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = 0.5\,gR = 0.5(10)(1.8) = 9\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2 \implies v_s = \mathbf{3.0\text{ m/s}}$$

(c) Maximum Height Above $A$ in Subsequent Parabolic Flight: At $\theta_s = 120^\circ$, the radius vector points $30^\circ$ above the horizontal. The velocity vector $\vec{v}_s$ is tangent to the circle (perpendicular to the radius), so it makes angle $\alpha = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$ above the horizontal. Its vertical launch component is:

$$v_{sy} = v_s\sin 60^\circ = 3\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\text{ m/s}$$

The additional vertical rise in free flight until $v_y = 0$ is:

$$\Delta h_{\text{proj}} = \frac{v_{sy}^2}{2g} = \frac{27/4}{2(10)} = \frac{27}{80}\text{ m} = 0.3375\text{ m} \quad \left(\text{symbolically } \frac{3}{16}R\right)$$

Total maximum height above lowest point $A$:

$$H_{\max} = h_s + \Delta h_{\text{proj}} = 2.7 + 0.3375 = \mathbf{3.0375\text{ m}} \quad \left(\text{or } \frac{27}{16}R < 2R\right)$$
30-Second Exam Trick (Dimensionless Parameter $k = v_0^2 / gR$):
Whenever $v_0 = \sqrt{k\,gR}$ with $2 < k < 5$, immediately evaluate the three one-line invariants: $$\cos\theta_s=\frac{2 - k}{3}, \qquad h_s=\left(\frac{k + 1}{3}\right)R, \qquad v_s=\sqrt{\left(\frac{k - 2}{3}\right)gR}$$ Here $k=3.5 \implies \cos\theta_s=-0.5$ ($\theta_s=120^\circ$), $h_s=1.5R=2.7\text{ m}$, $v_s=\sqrt{0.5 \times 18}=3\text{ m/s}$. Total execution time: under 15 seconds!
Level 3 Vertical Spring Drop & Equilibrium Trap Worked Example 8: Maximum Speed vs. Maximum Compression
A block of mass $m = 2\text{ kg}$ is dropped from rest from a height $h = 0.4\text{ m}$ directly above the upper end of a vertical massless spring of stiffness $k = 200\text{ N/m}$ fixed to a rigid floor ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$). Calculate: (a) the spring compression $x_0$ at which the block attains its maximum speed, (b) the maximum speed $v_{\max}$ of the block, and (c) the maximum compression $x_{\max}$ of the spring.

Critical Concept Check: The block does not achieve maximum speed the moment it touches the spring! Right after contact, gravity $mg$ still exceeds the upward spring force $kx$, so the block continues accelerating downward until the net force becomes zero.

(a) Compression for Maximum Speed ($a = 0$):

$$mg - kx_0 = 0 \implies x_0 = \frac{mg}{k} = \frac{(2)(10)}{200} = \mathbf{0.1\text{ m}}\ (10\text{ cm})$$

(b) Maximum Speed $v_{\max}$ via COME: Choose $U_g = 0$ at the equilibrium compression level $x_0$. Between release (height $h + x_0$ above datum, $v = 0$, $x = 0$) and position $x_0$:

$$mg(h + x_0) = \frac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2 + \frac{1}{2}kx_0^2$$ $$(2)(10)(0.4 + 0.1) = \frac{1}{2}(2)v_{\max}^2 + \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.1)^2$$ $$10 = v_{\max}^2 + 1 \implies v_{\max}^2 = 9 \implies v_{\max} = \mathbf{3.0\text{ m/s}}$$

Notice: At initial spring touch ($x = 0$), the block's speed is only $\sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83\text{ m/s} < 3.0\text{ m/s}$.

(c) Maximum Compression $x_{\max}$ via COME: At lowest turnaround point ($v_f = 0$), total vertical drop is $h + x_{\max}$:

$$mg(h + x_{\max}) = \frac{1}{2}kx_{\max}^2$$ $$20(0.4 + x_{\max}) = 100\,x_{\max}^2 \implies 100\,x_{\max}^2 - 20\,x_{\max} - 8 = 0 \implies 25\,x_{\max}^2 - 5\,x_{\max} - 2 = 0$$

Factorizing $(25 \times (-2) = -50 = (-10)(+5))$:

$$(5x_{\max} - 2)(5x_{\max} + 1) = 0$$

Since compression $x_{\max} > 0$, we discard $-\frac{1}{5}$ and obtain:

$$x_{\max} = \frac{2}{5}\text{ m} = \mathbf{0.4\text{ m}}\ (40\text{ cm})$$
30-Second Exam Trick (SHM-Equilibrium Bridge):
Once attached/in contact, the block executes SHM about $x_0 = \frac{mg}{k}$ with $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ and amplitude $A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + 2x_0 h}$!
  • Here $x_0 = 0.1\text{ m}$, $\omega = \sqrt{200/2} = 10\text{ rad/s}$, and $A = \sqrt{0.01 + 2(0.1)(0.4)} = \sqrt{0.09} = 0.3\text{ m}$.
  • Instant answers: $x_{\max} = x_0 + A = 0.1 + 0.3 = \mathbf{0.4\text{ m}}$, and $v_{\max} = \omega A = 10(0.3) = \mathbf{3.0\text{ m/s}}$!
Level 3 Potential Energy Curve & Stability Worked Example 9: Molecular Potential $U(x)$, Stability & Turning Points
A particle of mass $m = 2\text{ kg}$ moves along the positive $x$-axis ($x > 0$) in a conservative force field with potential energy function: $$U(x) = \frac{16}{x^2} - \frac{8}{x} \quad (\text{where } U \text{ is in joules and } x \text{ is in metres})$$ Find: (a) the force $F(x)$ and the equilibrium position $x_0$, (b) whether $x_0$ is stable or unstable, (c) the minimum speed needed at $x_0$ for the particle to escape to $x \to \infty$, and (d) the turning points of motion if the particle has total mechanical energy $E = -\dfrac{3}{4}\text{ J}$.

(a) Conservative Force & Equilibrium Position: Using $F(x) = -\dfrac{dU}{dx}$ with $U(x) = 16x^{-2} - 8x^{-1}$:

$$F(x) = -\left(-32x^{-3} + 8x^{-2}\right) = \frac{32}{x^3} - \frac{8}{x^2}$$

At equilibrium, $F(x_0) = 0$:

$$\frac{32}{x_0^3} = \frac{8}{x_0^2} \implies x_0 = \mathbf{4\text{ m}}$$

(b) Stability Classification: Compute the second derivative $\dfrac{d^2U}{dx^2} = -\dfrac{dF}{dx}$:

$$\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} = \frac{96}{x^4} - \frac{16}{x^3}$$

Evaluating at $x_0 = 4\text{ m}$:

$$\left.\frac{d^2U}{dx^2}\right|_{x=4} = \frac{96}{256} - \frac{16}{64} = \frac{3}{8} - \frac{2}{8} = +\frac{1}{8}\text{ J/m}^2 > 0$$

Because $\dfrac{d^2U}{dx^2} > 0$, $U(x)$ has a local minimum at $x_0 = 4\text{ m}$, confirming stable equilibrium.

(c) Escape Speed from $x_0$: Evaluate $U(x_0)$ and $U(\infty)$:

$$U_{\min} = U(4) = \frac{16}{4^2} - \frac{8}{4} = 1 - 2 = -1\text{ J}, \qquad U(\infty) = 0$$

To barely reach $x \to \infty$ with $v_\infty = 0$, total mechanical energy must be $E = 0$. By COME:

$$\frac{1}{2}mv_{\text{esc}}^2 + U(4) = 0 \implies \frac{1}{2}(2)v_{\text{esc}}^2 - 1 = 0 \implies v_{\text{esc}} = \mathbf{1.0\text{ m/s}}$$

(d) Turning Points for $E = -\dfrac{3}{4}\text{ J}$: At turning points, kinetic energy vanishes ($K = 0$), so $U(x) = E$:

$$\frac{16}{x^2} - \frac{8}{x} = -\frac{3}{4}$$

Multiplying through by $4x^2$ ($x > 0$):

$$64 - 32x = -3x^2 \implies 3x^2 - 32x + 64 = 0 \implies (3x - 8)(x - 8) = 0$$

The particle oscillates strictly between the turning points $x_{\min} = \mathbf{\dfrac{8}{3}\text{ m}}$ and $x_{\max} = \mathbf{8\text{ m}}$.

30-Second Exam Trick (Zero-Calculus Complete-the-Square Identity):
For any inverse-power potential $U(x) = \dfrac{A}{x^2} - \dfrac{B}{x}$ ($A, B > 0$), complete the square in $u = \frac{1}{x}$: $$U(x) = A\left(\frac{1}{x} - \frac{B}{2A}\right)^2 - \frac{B^2}{4A}$$ Because the squared term is $\ge 0$, you read off all three results instantaneously without differentiating:
  1. Minimum occurs at $\frac{1}{x_0} = \frac{B}{2A} \implies x_0 = \mathbf{\dfrac{2A}{B}} = \frac{32}{8} = 4\text{ m}$ (automatically stable!).
  2. Minimum potential energy $U_{\min} = \mathbf{-\dfrac{B^2}{4A}} = -\frac{64}{64} = -1\text{ J}$.
  3. Binding / Escape energy $= \mathbf{+\dfrac{B^2}{4A}} = 1\text{ J} \implies v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1)}{2}} = 1\text{ m/s}$.
