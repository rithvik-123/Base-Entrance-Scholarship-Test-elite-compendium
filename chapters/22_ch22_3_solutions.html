Q9 Level 2 Answer: (A)

Step 1 — Identify the Excited Orbit $n_2$: In the ground state ($n_1 = 1$), angular momentum is $L_1 = \frac{1h}{2\pi}$. An increase of $\Delta L = \frac{3h}{2\pi}$ gives:

$$L_2 = L_1 + \Delta L = \frac{1h}{2\pi} + \frac{3h}{2\pi} = \frac{4h}{2\pi} \implies \mathbf{n_2 = 4}$$

Q11 Level 2 Answer: (C)

Evaluation of Statement I (TRUE): Neutral Iron ($\text{Fe}, Z = 26$) has configuration $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^6 4s^2$. To form $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, remove two electrons from the outermost $4s$ shell, yielding $\mathbf{[\text{Ar}]\,3d^6}$. By Hund's rule, the six $3d$ electrons distribute across the five $d$-orbitals as $\uparrow\downarrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow$, leaving exactly $4$ unpaired electrons.

Evaluation of Statement II (FALSE): While $4s$ ($n+l = 4$) fills before $3d$ ($n+l = 5$) in neutral atoms, once electrons occupy $3d$, the $4s$ orbital lies radially farther from the nucleus and has higher energy than $3d$. Therefore, during ionization of ALL $3d$-block metals, electrons are ALWAYS removed from $4s$ BEFORE $3d$—the exact opposite of Statement II!

Hence, Statement I is true and Statement II is false → (C).

Q12 Level 2 Answer: (B)

Step 1 — Lattice Energy Scaling: $U \propto \frac{|q_+ q_-|}{r_+ + r_-}$. Because $\text{NaF}$ ($\text{Na}^+ \text{F}^-$) and $\text{MgO}$ ($\text{Mg}^{2+} \text{O}^{2-}$) have nearly identical interionic distances ($r_+ + r_- \approx 2.1\text{ Å}$):

$$\frac{U(\text{MgO})}{U(\text{NaF})} \approx \frac{|(+2)(-2)|}{|(+1)(-1)|} = \frac{4}{1} = 4$$

Because $\text{MgO}$ has roughly $4\times$ the lattice energy of $\text{NaF}$, it has a dramatically higher melting point ($\text{MgO} \approx 2852^\circ\text{C}$ vs. $\text{NaF} \approx 993^\circ\text{C}$).

Step 2 — Electrical Conductivity: In the solid state, ions are rigidly locked in the crystal lattice and cannot migrate, so both $\text{NaF}$ and $\text{MgO}$ are electrical insulators. Upon melting, ions become mobile and conduct electricity strongly. Hence, (B) is correct.

Q17 Level 2 Answer: (C)

All four chlorides share the identical $\text{Cl}^-$ anion, so covalent character depends solely on the cation's polarizing power ($\propto q_+ / r_+$). Comparing the cations $\text{K}^+, \text{Na}^+, \text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{Al}^{3+}$:

  • Charge increases: $+1\ (\text{K}^+, \text{Na}^+) \lt +2\ (\text{Mg}^{2+}) \lt +3\ (\text{Al}^{3+})$.
  • Ionic radius decreases sharply: $r(\text{K}^+) \gt r(\text{Na}^+) \gt r(\text{Mg}^{2+}) \gt r(\text{Al}^{3+})$.

Thus $\text{Al}^{3+}$ has by far the highest polarizing power, giving $\text{AlCl}_3$ the highest covalent character (indeed, anhydrous $\text{AlCl}_3$ sublimes at just $180^\circ\text{C}$ as covalent $\text{Al}_2\text{Cl}_6$ dimers, compared to $\text{NaCl}$ melting at $801^\circ\text{C}$). Hence, (C) is correct.

Q19 Level 3 Answer: (D)

Evaluation of Statement I (FALSE): Watch the wording: ONE single hydrogen atom! A single electron descending from $n = 5$ to $n = 1$ can make at most one hop per level ($5 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 3 \rightarrow 2 \rightarrow 1$), emitting a maximum of $n - 1 = 5 - 1 = \mathbf{4\text{ photons}}$ (NOT $10$!). Q24 Level 3 (Passage 2) Answer: (D)

Part 1 — Dipole Moment Comparison ($\text{NH}_3$ vs. $\text{NF}_3$): Both are trigonal pyramidal ($3\text{ bp}, 1\text{ lp}$). Chapter 22 Trap & Error Forensics

Trap Forensics #1: Applying Bohr's $Z^2/n^2$ Formula to Multi-Electron Atoms (e.g., Neutral $\text{He}$ vs. $\text{He}^+$)

The Fatal Blunder: A BEST MCQ asks for the first ionization energy of neutral Helium ($\text{He}, Z=2$), and students instinctively plug $Z = 2, n = 1$ into Bohr's formula to answer $13.6 \times 2^2 = 54.4\text{ eV}$!

Forensic Autopsy: Bohr's model assumes a single electron experiencing a pure bare-nucleus Coulomb force with zero inter-electronic repulsion. Neutral $\text{He}$ has two electrons that repel and screen each other, so its true first ionization energy is only $24.6\text{ eV}$—NOT $54.4\text{ eV}$! The value $54.4\text{ eV}$ belongs strictly to the second ionization energy (ionizing the 1-electron $\text{He}^+$ ion).

Antidote Rule: Before touching $r_n \propto n^2/Z$, $v_n \propto Z/n$, or $E_n = -13.6 Z^2/n^2$, perform the 1-Electron Passport Check: verify the species is strictly hydrogenic ($\text{H},\ \text{He}^+,\ \text{Li}^{2+},\ \text{Be}^{3+}$). Never apply Bohr formulas to neutral $\text{He}$, $\text{Li}^+$, or $\text{Be}^{2+}$.

Trap Forensics #2: Writing $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^4 4s^2$ for Chromium & Removing $3d$ Before $4s$ in Cations (See Q11 & Q14)

The Fatal Blunder: Blindly following the diagonal Aufbau chart without applying the two $Z \le 30$ stability exceptions ($\text{Cr}$ and $\text{Cu}$), OR removing $3d$ electrons before $4s$ electrons when writing cations like $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ($\text{Fe}^{2+} \neq [\text{Ar}]\,3d^4 4s^2$!).

Forensic Autopsy: Writing $\text{Cr} = [\text{Ar}]\,3d^4 4s^2$ gives $4$ unpaired electrons instead of the true $6$, and writing $\text{Cu} = [\text{Ar}]\,3d^9 4s^2$ drops the $l=2$ electron count from $10$ to $9$ (triggering Distractor A in Q14). Likewise, believing "$4s$ fills first so $3d$ leaves first" flips Statement II in Q11.

Antidote Rule: Tattoo two rules permanently into memory: (1) $\text{Cr}(24) = [\text{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1$ and $\text{Cu}(29) = [\text{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1$; (2) "First in ($4s$), First out ($4s$)"—electrons ALWAYS exit the highest principal shell $n$ first during ionization.

Trap Forensics #3: Assuming Solid Ionic Crystals Conduct Electricity & Ignoring $q_1 q_2$ in Lattice Energy (See Q12)

The Fatal Blunder: Reading "ionic compounds are made of charged ions" and jumping to the false conclusion that solid $\text{NaCl}$ or $\text{MgO}$ conducts electricity—or assuming $\text{NaF}$ and $\text{MgO}$ have similar melting points because their bond lengths are both $\sim 2.1\text{ Å}$.

Forensic Autopsy: Electrical current requires mobile charge carriers. In a solid ionic crystal, cations and anions are trapped in rigid electrostatic cages and can only vibrate in place, making solid ionic crystals insulators. Only melting (molten state) or dissolving in water frees the ions to carry current. Furthermore, doubling BOTH ion charges from $(\pm 1)$ to $(\pm 2)$ multiplies Coulombic Lattice Energy by $2 \times 2 = \mathbf{4\times}$ (not $2\times$!).

Antidote Rule: Solid ionic crystal $= \text{Insulator}$; Molten/Aqueous ionic compound $= \text{Strong Electrolyte}$. And always check the product $|q_+ q_-|$ first when comparing lattice energies.

Trap Forensics #4: Confusing Bond Polarity with Overall Molecular Polarity ($\text{CO}_2 / \text{CCl}_4$ vs. $\text{H}_2\text{O} / \text{SO}_2$) (See Q23 & Q24)

The Fatal Blunder: Seeing highly electronegative atoms like $\text{O}$ or $\text{Cl}$ bonded to $\text{C}$ and concluding that $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{CCl}_4$ must be polar molecules—or seeing $\text{SO}_2$ written like $\text{CO}_2$ and assuming $\text{SO}_2$ is also linear and non-polar!

Forensic Autopsy: Individual $\text{C=O}$ and $\text{C–Cl}$ bonds ARE strongly polar, but net molecular dipole moment $\vec{\mu}_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec{\mu}_i$ is a 3D vector sum. In $\text{CO}_2$ (linear, $0\text{ lp}$) and $\text{CCl}_4$ (tetrahedral, $0\text{ lp}$), symmetry forces exact vector cancellation ($\mu = 0$). By contrast, $\text{S}$ has $6$ valence electrons (vs. $4$ for $\text{C}$), leaving $1$ lone pair on $\text{S}$ in $\text{SO}_2$ ($\text{SN} = 3$), which bends the molecule ($\sim 119^\circ$) so dipoles cannot cancel ($\mu \neq 0$).

Antidote Rule: Never judge molecular polarity from formula similarity! Always compute $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}[V + M - C + A]$ to find lone pairs first: symmetric $0\text{-lp}$ molecules with identical terminal atoms ($\text{CO}_2, \text{BF}_3, \text{CCl}_4, \text{SF}_6$) have $\mu = 0$; bent/pyramidal molecules with lone pairs ($\text{H}_2\text{O}, \text{SO}_2, \text{NH}_3, \text{NF}_3$) have $\mu \neq 0$.

Trap Forensics #5: Single-Atom vs. Sample Spectral Lines & Missing Terminal Lone Pairs (See Q10, Q16, Q19)

The Fatal Blunder: Applying $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ blindly to a single hydrogen atom (Q19: writing $10$ instead of $n - 1 = 4$), or forgetting to count the $2\text{ lp}$ on each terminal $\text{O}$ and $3\text{ lp}$ on each terminal halogen when asked for total lone pairs in a molecule (Q16).

Forensic Autopsy: One single electron cannot take two parallel routes at the same time—it can make at most $(n - 1)$ sequential downward steps. Similarly, in Q16, counting only central-atom lone pairs misses $4$ lone pairs on $\text{CO}_2$'s oxygens and $12$ lone pairs on $\text{CCl}_4$'s chlorines, turning the answer $19$ into a wrong $3$.

Antidote Rule: Underline the exact noun in every counting question: "sample of atoms" $\rightarrow \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ vs. "one single atom" $\rightarrow n - 1$; and "central atom lone pairs" vs. "total lone pairs in the molecule" (remember every terminal $-\text{F}/\text{Cl}/\text{Br}/\text{I}$ carries $3\text{ lp}$ and every terminal $=\text{O}$ carries $2\text{ lp}$).
