20.1 Reflection at Plane Surfaces & Moving Mirrors

Core Principle • Laws of Reflection & Deviation

When a light ray strikes a smooth reflecting surface at an angle of incidence $i$ (measured strictly from the normal to the surface):

  1. The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  2. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence: $\angle r = \angle i$.

Angle of Deviation ($\delta$): The angle through which the ray is turned from its original straight path is:

$$\delta = 180^\circ - (i + r) = 180^\circ - 2i$$
  • Normal incidence ($i = 0^\circ$): Ray retraces its path ($\delta = 180^\circ$).
  • Grazing incidence ($i \to 90^\circ$): Ray passes nearly undeviated ($\delta \to 0^\circ$).

Key Plane-Mirror Theorems

  • Mirror Rotation Theorem: Keeping the incident ray fixed, if a plane mirror is rotated by angle $\theta$ about an axis lying in its plane (perpendicular to the plane of incidence), the normal rotates by $\theta$, so the new incidence angle changes by $\pm\theta$ and the reflected ray rotates by $2\theta$ in the same sense. (Conversely, rotating the incident ray by $\theta$ with a fixed mirror rotates the reflected ray by $\theta$ in the opposite sense.)
  • Minimum Mirror Height for Full-Length Image: By similar triangles from the eyes to the top of the head and the toes, a person of height $H$ requires a vertical plane mirror of minimum height $h_{\min} = \dfrac{H}{2}$, mounted with its bottom edge at half the eye-to-floor height—completely independent of the person's distance from the mirror.
  • Two Mirrors Inclined at Angle $\theta$ (Deviation by Double Reflection): A ray suffering one reflection at each mirror undergoes a net deviation $\delta_{\text{net}} = 360^\circ - 2\theta$ (independent of initial angle of incidence $i$, provided both reflections occur). For two perpendicular mirrors ($\theta = 90^\circ$), $\delta_{\text{net}} = 180^\circ$—the ray returns antiparallel (corner retro-reflector).

Number of Images Formed by Two Inclined Plane Mirrors

Compute $m = \dfrac{360^\circ}{\theta}$. Every image formed lies on a circle centered at the line of intersection of the two mirrors, passing through the object $O$:

Value of $m = 360^\circ/\theta$ Object Position Exact Number of Images ($n$) Physical Reason
Even Integer (e.g., $\theta = 90^\circ, 60^\circ, 45^\circ$) Any position between mirrors $n = m - 1$ The final two images from the two reflection chains strictly coincide on the circle.
Odd Integer (e.g., $\theta = 120^\circ, 72^\circ$) Symmetric (on angle bisector) $n = m - 1$ The final reflections land at $180^\circ$ (on the backward mirror extensions), terminating both chains early.
Odd Integer (e.g., $\theta = 120^\circ, 72^\circ$) Asymmetric (off bisector) $n = m$ No images coincide and one chain produces an extra distinct image in the blind zone behind both mirrors.
Non-Integer (e.g., $\theta = 50^\circ \implies m = 7.2$) General (depends on angles $\alpha, \beta$) Count via circle/cross-addition method (equals $\lfloor m \rfloor$ or $\lfloor m \rfloor + 1$) Start with $(\alpha, \beta)$ and cross-add $\theta$ until sums reach $\ge 180^\circ$; check if last two satisfy $\alpha_k + \beta_j + \theta = 360^\circ$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Object–Image Velocity Vector Formulas

Resolve the object position and mirror position into components perpendicular ($\perp$) and parallel ($\parallel$) to the mirror surface. Because the image lies strictly as far behind the mirror as the object lies in front:

$$\vec{r}_{I\perp} - \vec{r}_{M\perp} = -(\vec{r}_{O\perp} - \vec{r}_{M\perp}) \implies \vec{r}_{I\perp} = 2\vec{r}_{M\perp} - \vec{r}_{O\perp}$$ $$\vec{r}_{I\parallel} = \vec{r}_{O\parallel}$$

Differentiating both vector identities with respect to time $t$ yields the universal velocity laws for a translating plane mirror:

$$\vec{v}_{I\perp} = 2\vec{v}_{M\perp} - \vec{v}_{O\perp}, \qquad \vec{v}_{I\parallel} = \vec{v}_{O\parallel}$$

Crucial Insights: (1) Sliding a mirror parallel to its own plane ($\vec{v}_{M\parallel}$) has zero effect on image velocity. (2) If the mirror is stationary ($\vec{v}_M = \vec{0}$), the perpendicular velocity reverses ($\vec{v}_{I\perp} = -\vec{v}_{O\perp}$) while the parallel component stays identical.

20.2 Reflection at Spherical Mirrors

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Paraxial Approximation vs. Exact Marginal Ray

Consider a ray parallel to the principal axis striking a concave mirror of radius of curvature $R$ (center $C$, pole $P$) at point $M$, such that the normal $CM$ makes angle $\theta$ with the principal axis. By the law of reflection, $\angle CMF = \angle i = \theta$, and by alternate interior angles, $\angle MCF = \theta$. Thus $\triangle CFM$ is isosceles with $CF = FM$.

Dropping a perpendicular from $F$ to midpoint of $CM$ gives $CF\cos\theta = \dfrac{R}{2} \implies CF = \dfrac{R}{2\cos\theta}$. Hence the exact distance $x = PF$ from the pole where the reflected ray crosses the axis is:

$$x = R - CF = R - \frac{R}{2\cos\theta}$$
  • Paraxial Rays ($\theta \to 0 \implies \cos\theta \to 1$): All rays near the axis converge to a single sharp focus at $f = R - \dfrac{R}{2} = \dfrac{R}{2}$.
  • Marginal Rays (large $\theta$): Since $\cos\theta < 1$, $\dfrac{R}{2\cos\theta}> \dfrac{R}{2}$, so $x < \dfrac{R}{2}$—marginal rays cross closer to the pole than the paraxial focus. This defect is spherical aberration (eliminated by parabolic mirrors).

Strict New Cartesian Sign Convention

Treat the Pole $P$ (for mirrors) or Optical Center $O$ (for lenses) as the coordinate origin $(0, 0)$:

  1. Light-Direction Rule ($+x$): Distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive ($+$); distances measured opposite to incident light are negative ($-$).
  2. Height Rule ($+y$): Heights measured above the principal axis are positive ($+$); heights measured below are negative ($-$).
  3. Resulting Focal Length Signs (light traveling left $\to$ right):
    • Concave Mirror: Focus lies in front (left of $P$) $\implies R < 0,\ f < 0$.
    • Convex Mirror: Focus lies behind (right of $P$) $\implies R > 0,\ f > 0$.

Mirror Formula, Magnification & Axial Velocity

Master Spherical Mirror Equations

1. Mirror Formula:

$$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{2}{R}$$

2. Transverse (Lateral) Magnification ($m$): Multiplying the mirror formula by $u$ or $v$ gives three instant forms:

$$m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{v}{u} = \frac{f}{f - u} = \frac{f - v}{f}$$
  • Sign Diagnostics (for a real object): $m < 0 \implies$ Real & Inverted image; $m > 0 \implies$ Virtual & Erect image.
  • Instant Real vs. Virtual Shift Shortcut: A concave mirror gives equal magnification magnitude $|m|$ at two object positions symmetric around the focus $F$: $$u_{\text{real}} = f\left(1 + \frac{1}{|m|}\right), \qquad u_{\text{virtual}} = f\left(1 - \frac{1}{|m|}\right) \implies \Delta u = |u_{\text{real}} - u_{\text{virtual}}| = \frac{2|f|}{|m|}$$

3. Longitudinal (Axial) Magnification ($m_L$): Differentiating $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$ yields $-\frac{dv}{v^2} - \frac{du}{u^2} = 0$, so for a short object ($|du| \ll |u|$) lying along the principal axis:

$$m_L = \frac{dv}{du} = -\frac{v^2}{u^2} = -m^2$$

Note: $m_L$ is always negative—a mirror always reverses front-to-back orientation along the axis! For an extended rod of finite length (not small), compute $v_1$ and $v_2$ separately for both ends and take $L_I = |v_2 - v_1|$.

4. Image Velocity Along Principal Axis: Dividing $dv = -m^2\,du$ by $dt$ (relative to the mirror):

$$v_{I/M} = -m^2\,v_{O/M} \implies v_I - v_M = -m^2(v_O - v_M)$$

When the mirror is stationary ($v_M = 0$), $v_I = -m^2 v_O$—the image always moves opposite to the object along the axis!

20.3 Refraction at Plane Surfaces & Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

Core Principle • Refractive Index & Snell's Law

The absolute refractive index $\mu$ of a medium measures how much light slows down compared to vacuum ($c \approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$). Crucially, when light crosses from one medium into another, its frequency $\nu$ remains strictly invariant (fixed by the source), while speed $v$ and wavelength $\lambda$ both scale down by $\mu$:

$$\mu = \frac{c}{v} = \frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda}, \qquad \nu_1 = \nu_2$$

Snell's Law of Refraction: Across an interface separating media of indices $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$:

$$\mu_1 \sin i = \mu_2 \sin r \qquad \text{(invariant across parallel stacked slabs: } \mu_1\sin i_1 = \mu_2\sin i_2 = \mu_3\sin i_3 = \dots \text{)}$$
  • Rarer $\to$ Denser ($\mu_2 > \mu_1$): Bends toward normal ($r < i$); deviation $\delta=i - r$.
  • Denser $\to$ Rarer ($\mu_2 < \mu_1$): Bends away from normal ($r > i$); deviation $\delta = r - i$.

Apparent Depth, Normal Shift & Lateral Shift

  • Single Interface (Paraxial / Near-Normal Viewing): For an object at real distance $d$ inside medium $\mu_{\text{obj}}$ viewed by an observer in medium $\mu_{\text{obs}}$: $$d_{\text{app}} = d\left(\frac{\mu_{\text{obs}}}{\mu_{\text{obj}}}\right) = \frac{d}{\mu_{\text{rel}}}$$ Quick memory check: A coin underwater ($\mu_{\text{obj}} = 4/3$) viewed from air ($\mu_{\text{obs}} = 1$) looks shallower ($d_{\text{app}} = \frac{3}{4}d$); a bird in air viewed by a fish underwater looks higher ($h_{\text{app}} = \frac{4}{3}h$).
  • Normal Shift by a Parallel Slab of Thickness $t$: Looking normally through a slab of index $\mu$ placed in air ($\mu_0 = 1$) shifts any object behind it toward the observer (in the direction of light propagation) by: $$\Delta t = t\left(1 - \frac{1}{\mu}\right)$$ Notice $\Delta t$ is independent of the object's distance behind the slab! For $k$ stacked immiscible slabs in air, total apparent depth and net normal shift simply add linearly: $$d_{\text{app}} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{t_j}{\mu_j}, \qquad \Delta t_{\text{net}} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} t_j\left(1 - \frac{1}{\mu_j}\right)$$
  • Lateral Shift (Oblique Ray Through a Slab): An incident ray at angle $i$ emerges strictly parallel to its original direction ($e = i$), displaced sideways by perpendicular distance: $$d_{\text{lat}} = \frac{t\sin(i - r)}{\cos r}$$

Total Internal Reflection (TIR) & Circle of Illuminance (Snell's Window)

TIR occurs when both necessary conditions are simultaneously met: (1) Light travels from an optically denser medium ($\mu_d$) toward a rarer medium ($\mu_r$), and (2) the angle of incidence strictly exceeds the critical angle $C$ ($i > C$), where $C$ is the angle of incidence giving grazing refraction ($r = 90^\circ$):

$$\mu_d \sin C = \mu_r \sin 90^\circ \implies \sin C = \frac{\mu_r}{\mu_d} = \frac{1}{\mu_{\text{rel}}}$$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Circle of Illuminance (Snell's Window)

Consider a point source $S$ submerged at depth $h$ inside a liquid of index $\mu$ (below air, $\mu_0 = 1$). Rays striking the surface with $i \le C$ refract out into air; rays with $i > C$ undergo TIR back into the liquid. Thus light escapes only through a circular surface patch of radius $r$ centered directly above $S$:

$$\tan C = \frac{r}{h} \implies r = h\tan C = h\frac{\sin C}{\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 C}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{\mu^2 - 1}}$$

The area of the escape patch is $A = \pi r^2 = \dfrac{\pi h^2}{\mu^2 - 1}$, and the full apex angle of the escape cone is $2C = 2\sin^{-1}(1/\mu)$ (for water $\mu = 4/3$, $C \approx 48.6^\circ$, so a fish sees the entire $180^\circ$ sky compressed inside a $\approx 97.2^\circ$ overhead cone—Snell's Window!).

Figure 20.2: Total Internal Reflection bounds the escaping light from a submerged point source to a circular surface patch of radius $r = h/\sqrt{\mu^2 - 1}$.

High-Yield Applications of TIR

  • Optical Fibers: A high-index glass core ($\mu_1$) surrounded by a lower-index cladding ($\mu_2 < \mu_1$) guides light signals over kilometres with negligible loss via repeated TIR. The maximum acceptance half-angle $i_{\max}$ in air satisfies $\sin i_{\max}=\sqrt{\mu_1^2 - \mu_2^2}$ (called the Numerical Aperture).
  • $45^\circ\text{–}90^\circ\text{–}45^\circ$ Porro (Totally Reflecting) Prisms: Made of crown glass ($\mu \approx 1.5 \implies C \approx 41.8^\circ < 45^\circ$). Light hitting a leg normally strikes the hypotenuse at $i=45^\circ> C$, turning $90^\circ$ with nearly $100\%$ reflectivity (or $180^\circ$ when incident on the hypotenuse—used in binoculars and periscopes).
  • Mirage (Inferior Mirage): Hot desert/road air near the ground is optically rarer ($\mu$ decreases downward). Downward-directed skylight curves progressively away from the vertical until $i > C$, undergoing TIR upward so the observer sees an inverted shimmering "water pool" image of the sky.

20.4 Spherical Refraction, Thin Lenses, Newton's Formula & Silvered Lenses

Master Formula • Single Spherical Refracting Surface

When paraxial rays travel from medium $\mu_1$ (object side) into medium $\mu_2$ (image side) across a spherical boundary of radius of curvature $R$ (strict Cartesian signs!):

$$\frac{\mu_2}{v} - \frac{\mu_1}{u} = \frac{\mu_2 - \mu_1}{R}, \qquad m = \frac{\mu_1 v}{\mu_2 u}$$

Check: Setting $R \to \infty$ (plane interface) gives $\dfrac{\mu_2}{v} = \dfrac{\mu_1}{u} \implies v = u\dfrac{\mu_2}{\mu_1}$, instantly recovering the apparent-depth formula!

1. Lens Maker's Formula & Liquid-Immersion Flips

Applying the spherical-refraction formula sequentially at both surfaces (radii $R_1$ and $R_2$) of a thin lens of refractive index $\mu_l$ immersed in a surrounding medium of index $\mu_m$ yields the Lens Maker's Formula:

$$\frac{1}{f} = \left(\frac{\mu_l}{\mu_m} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)$$
  • Biconvex lens (in air): $R_1 > 0,\ R_2 < 0 \implies \left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right)> 0$. For an equiconvex lens ($R_1 = +R, R_2 = -R$): $\dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{2(\mu_l/\mu_m - 1)}{R}$.
  • Biconcave lens (in air): $R_1 < 0,\ R_2> 0 \implies \left(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\right) < 0$.
  • Medium Immersion Rules (High-Frequency Exam Trap!):
    • If $1 < \mu_m < \mu_l$: $\left(\frac{\mu_l}{\mu_m} - 1\right)$ remains positive but smaller $\implies$ nature stays the same, focal length increases ($|f_{\text{med}}| > |f_{\text{air}}|$). For glass ($\mu = 1.5$) in water ($\mu = 4/3$), $f_{\text{water}} = \mathbf{4 f_{\text{air}}}$!
    • If $\mu_m = \mu_l$: $\dfrac{1}{f} = 0 \implies f = \infty$. The lens behaves like a flat glass plate and becomes optically invisible.
    • If $\mu_m > \mu_l$: $\left(\frac{\mu_l}{\mu_m} - 1\right)$ turns negative $\implies$ nature flips! A biconvex lens behaves as a diverging lens, and an air bubble inside water behaves as a diverging lens!

2. Thin Lens Formula, Power & Newton's Formula ($x_1 x_2 = f^2$)

$$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}, \qquad m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = +\frac{v}{u} = \frac{f}{f + u} = \frac{f - v}{f}, \qquad P = \frac{1}{f\text{ (in m)}} = \frac{100}{f\text{ (in cm)}}\text{ (Dioptres, D)}$$

Caution on Power in a Medium: Technically, optical power in a medium of index $\mu_m$ is $P = \dfrac{\mu_m}{f}$, reducing to $P = \dfrac{1}{f}$ in air.

BEST Sample-Paper Direct Hit • Newton's Lens & Mirror Formula ($x_1 x_2 = f^2$)

Instead of measuring object and image distances from the optical center (or pole), measure them from the principal foci! For a real object placed at distance $x_1$ outside the first focus $F_1$ (so $u = -(f + x_1)$) forming a real image at distance $x_2$ outside the second focus $F_2$ (so $v = +(f + x_2)$):

$$\frac{1}{f + x_2} - \frac{1}{-(f + x_1)} = \frac{1}{f} \implies \frac{(f + x_1) + (f + x_2)}{(f + x_1)(f + x_2)} = \frac{1}{f}$$ $$2f^2 + f(x_1 + x_2) = f^2 + f(x_1 + x_2) + x_1 x_2 \implies \mathbf{x_1 x_2 = f^2}$$

Same Exact Form for Concave Mirrors: Setting $u = -(f + x_1)$ and $v = -(f + x_2)$ in $\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = -\frac{1}{f}$ gives the identical equation $x_1 x_2 = f^2$, along with transverse magnification $|m| = \dfrac{f}{x_1} = \dfrac{x_2}{f}$.

Minimum Real-Object-to-Real-Image Distance: Total separation $D = |u| + |v| = 2f + x_1 + x_2$. By AM–GM inequality, $x_1 + x_2 \ge 2\sqrt{x_1 x_2} = 2f$, with equality at $x_1 = x_2 = f$ ($u = -2f, v = +2f$). Thus $D_{\min} = \mathbf{4f}$!

3. Displacement Method, Lens Cutting & Lens Combinations

  • Bessel's Displacement Method: When a real object and screen are held fixed at separation $D > 4f$, two distinct lens positions separated by $d$ yield sharp real images on the screen (conjugate positions $u_1 = -\frac{D-d}{2}, v_1 = +\frac{D+d}{2}$ and $u_2 = -\frac{D+d}{2}, v_2 = +\frac{D-d}{2}$): $$f = \frac{D^2 - d^2}{4D}, \qquad m_1 m_2 = 1 \implies O = \sqrt{I_1 I_2}, \qquad \frac{I_1}{I_2} = \left(\frac{D + d}{D - d}\right)^2$$
  • Lens Cutting Rules (Equiconvex Lens of Focal Length $f$):
    • Horizontal Cut (along principal axis): Neither $R_1$ nor $R_2$ changes $\implies$ focal length remains $f$ and a complete image still forms at the same position, but its brightness (intensity) drops to half because aperture area is halved!
    • Vertical Cut (perpendicular to principal axis): Splits the equiconvex lens into two plano-convex halves ($R_2 = \infty$) $\implies$ power halves ($P' = P/2$) and focal length doubles ($f' = 2f$).
  • Thin Lenses in Contact & Separated by Distance $d$:
    • In contact ($d = 0$): $\dfrac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \dfrac{1}{f_1} + \dfrac{1}{f_2}$, $P_{\text{eq}} = P_1 + P_2$, $m_{\text{net}} = m_1 \cdot m_2$.
    • Separated by air gap $d$: $\dfrac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \dfrac{1}{f_1} + \dfrac{1}{f_2} - \dfrac{d}{f_1 f_2}$ (equivalent to $P_{\text{eq}} = P_1 + P_2 - d\,P_1 P_2$).

4. Silvered Lens Equivalent Mirror Shortcut

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge • Silvered Lens Power Law

When one face of a thin lens is silvered, light undergoes three optical events: Refraction through the lens ($P_l$) $\to$ Reflection at the silvered back mirror ($P_m$) $\to$ Second refraction back out through the lens ($P_l$). The entire assembly behaves as a single equivalent spherical mirror whose converging power is:

$$P_{\text{eq}} = 2P_{\text{lens}} + P_{\text{mirror}}$$

CRITICAL SIGN TRAP — Lens Power vs. Mirror Power: Converging power is always positive! For a lens, $P_{\text{lens}} = +\dfrac{1}{f_l}$ (convex $f_l > 0 \implies P_l > 0$). For a mirror, a concave mirror is converging yet has $f_m < 0$, so $P_{\text{mirror}}=\mathbf{-\dfrac{1}{f_m}}$ and $P_{\text{eq}}=\mathbf{-\dfrac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}}}$. Substituting these signs yields the exact Cartesian focal-length formula:

$$-\frac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{2}{f_l} - \frac{1}{f_m} \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad \frac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{f_m} - \frac{2}{f_l}$$

Two Instant Plano-Convex Special Cases (glass $\mu$, curved radius $R$):

  • Plane surface silvered ($f_m = \infty,\ f_l = \frac{R}{\mu - 1}$): $\dfrac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = -\dfrac{2(\mu - 1)}{R} \implies f_{\text{eq}} = -\dfrac{R}{2(\mu - 1)}$ (concave mirror).
  • Curved surface silvered ($f_m = -\frac{R}{2},\ f_l = \frac{R}{\mu - 1}$): $\dfrac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = -\dfrac{2}{R} - \dfrac{2(\mu - 1)}{R} = -\dfrac{2\mu}{R} \implies f_{\text{eq}} = -\dfrac{R}{2\mu}$ (stronger concave mirror).

20.5 Refraction, Dispersion Through a Prism & Atmospheric Optics

Core Principle • Exact Prism Geometry

Consider a triangular prism of refracting angle $A$ and refractive index $\mu$ in air. A ray enters face 1 at angle of incidence $i$, refracts at $r_1$, strikes face 2 internally at $r_2$, and emerges into air at angle $e$. From the quadrilateral formed by the two normals and the refracting apex $A$, and the exterior-angle theorem:

$$r_1 + r_2 = A, \qquad \delta = (i - r_1) + (e - r_2) = i + e - A$$

Principle of Reversibility ($\delta\text{-}i$ Symmetry): For any deviation $\delta > \delta_m$, there are two distinct incidence angles ($i_1$ and $i_2 = e_1$) that produce the exact same deviation—meaning $i_1 + i_2 = A + \delta$. The $\delta\text{-}i$ curve is an asymmetric U-shape (steeper on the left, gentler on the right).

Minimum Deviation ($\delta_m$), Thin Prisms & No-Emergence Limits

  1. Condition for Minimum Deviation ($\delta = \delta_m$): The two roots $i_1$ and $i_2$ coalesce into a unique minimum when the ray passes symmetrically through the prism (parallel to the base for an isosceles/equilateral prism): $$i = e = \frac{A + \delta_m}{2}, \qquad r_1 = r_2 = \frac{A}{2}$$ Substituting into Snell's Law ($\sin i = \mu\sin r_1$) yields the Exact Prism Formula: $$\mu = \frac{\sin\left(\dfrac{A + \delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\dfrac{A}{2}\right)}$$
  2. Thin (Small-Angle) Prism ($A \lesssim 10^\circ$): Using $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ (in radians), $\mu \approx \dfrac{(A + \delta)/2}{A/2} \implies$: $$\delta = (\mu - 1)A \qquad \text{(nearly independent of small } i\text{)}$$
  3. Condition for No Emergence ($A > 2C$) & Grazing Limits:
    • To emerge from face 2 without TIR, we must have $r_2 \le C$. The largest possible $r_1$ occurs at grazing incidence ($i = 90^\circ \implies r_{1,\max} = C$), giving the smallest possible $r_2$: $r_{2,\min} = A - C$.
    • No Emergence for ANY ray: If $A - C > C \iff \mathbf{A > 2C}$, even the grazing ray undergoes TIR at face 2—no ray can ever emerge!
    • Maximum Deviation ($\delta_{\max}$): Occurs at grazing incidence ($i = 90^\circ$) or grazing emergence ($e = 90^\circ$), giving $\delta_{\max} = 90^\circ + e_{\min} - A$.
    • Minimum Incidence Angle for Emergence (when $C < A \le 2C$): Setting $r_2 = C$ (grazing emergence $e = 90^\circ$) gives $r_{1,\min} = A - C$, so $\sin i_{\min} = \mu\sin(A - C)$. Any ray with $i < i_{\min}$ undergoes TIR at face 2.

Dispersion, Atmospheric Refraction & Rayleigh Scattering

  • Dispersion of White Light: By Cauchy's empirical relation ($\mu(\lambda) \approx a + b/\lambda^2$), shorter wavelengths experience a higher refractive index: $\lambda_v < \lambda_r \implies \mu_v> \mu_r \implies \delta_v > \delta_r$. Violet bends the most; red bends the least.
  • Angular Dispersion ($\theta$) & Dispersive Power ($\omega$): For a thin prism: $$\theta = \delta_v - \delta_r = (\mu_v - \mu_r)A, \qquad \omega = \frac{\theta}{\delta_y} = \frac{\mu_v - \mu_r}{\mu_y - 1}$$ Notice $\omega$ depends only on the prism material—not on angle $A$!
  • Atmospheric Refraction: Earth's atmosphere increases in optical density toward the ground, continuously bending starlight downward toward the normal:
    • Twinkling of Stars: Distant stars act as point sources; turbulent air pockets shift their apparent position and flux rapidly. Planets subtend larger angular discs (collections of many point sources whose fluctuations average out), so they do not twinkle.
    • Advanced Sunrise & Delayed Sunset: Bending around Earth's horizon makes the Sun visible $\approx 2\text{ minutes}$ before actual sunrise and $\approx 2\text{ minutes}$ after actual sunset (lengthening the apparent day by $\approx 4\text{ minutes}$).
  • Rayleigh Scattering ($I_{\text{scat}} \propto \dfrac{1}{\lambda^4}$): Valid when scatterer size $a \ll \lambda$ (air molecules). Since $\lambda_{\text{red}} \approx 1.8\,\lambda_{\text{blue}}$, blue light scatters $(1.8)^4 \approx 10\text{ times}$ more strongly than red—explaining the blue daytime sky and reddish sunrise/sunset (longer atmospheric path strips away blue light). Clouds contain large water droplets ($a \gg \lambda$) that scatter all wavelengths equally (Mie scattering), appearing white.

20.6 Human Eye & Defects of Vision

Core Principle • Accommodation & Normal Vision Range

The human eye focuses objects from infinity down to the near point ($D = 25\text{ cm}$ for a normal young adult) onto a fixed retina ($\approx 2.5\text{ cm}$ behind the crystalline lens) by adjusting lens curvature via ciliary muscles—a capability called Power of Accommodation.

  • Far Point (relaxed eye): $\infty$ $\implies$ minimum eye power $P_{\min} = \frac{1}{0.025\text{ m}} = 40\text{ D}$.
  • Near Point ($25\text{ cm}$, maximally strained): $P_{\max} = \frac{1}{0.025} + \frac{1}{0.25} = 44\text{ D}$.
  • Range of Accommodation: $\Delta P = P_{\max} - P_{\min} = \mathbf{4\text{ D}}$.

Level 1 Plane Mirrors & Velocity Vectors Worked Example 1: Inclined Mirrors & Moving Mirror Velocity
(a) Two plane mirrors are inclined at an angle $\theta = 72^\circ$. Find the exact number of images formed of a point object placed (i) symmetrically on the angle bisector, and (ii) asymmetrically at $20^\circ$ from one mirror.
(b) A point object moves with velocity $\vec{v}_O = (6\hat{i} + 4\hat{j})\text{ m/s}$ in front of a plane mirror lying in the $y\text{-}z$ plane (normal along $+x$). If the mirror simultaneously moves with velocity $\vec{v}_M = (-2\hat{i} + 5\hat{j})\text{ m/s}$, find the velocity vector $\vec{v}_I$ of the image.

(a) Number of Images for $\theta = 72^\circ$: First compute the ratio:

$$m = \frac{360^\circ}{72^\circ} = 5 \quad (\text{an odd integer})$$
  • (i) Symmetric position ($36^\circ$ from each mirror): For odd $m$ and symmetric placement, $n = m - 1 = 5 - 1 = \mathbf{4\text{ images}}$. (The reflections along the two chains reach $36^\circ, 108^\circ$ and terminate because $108^\circ + 72^\circ = 180^\circ$ lies on the mirror extensions.)
  • (ii) Asymmetric position ($20^\circ$ and $52^\circ$): For odd $m$ and asymmetric placement, $n = m = \mathbf{5\text{ images}}$. (Check by cross-adding $72^\circ$: Chain 1 gives $20^\circ \to 92^\circ \to 164^\circ$ [3 images]; Chain 2 gives $52^\circ \to 124^\circ$ [2 images]; total $= 3 + 2 = 5$ distinct images.)

(b) Image Velocity Vector: Because the mirror lies in the $y\text{-}z$ plane, the $\hat{i}$-direction is perpendicular ($\perp$) to the mirror and the $\hat{j}$-direction is parallel ($\parallel$) to the mirror.

  • Perpendicular component ($\hat{i}$): $$v_{Ix} = 2v_{Mx} - v_{Ox} = 2(-2) - 6 = -4 - 6 = -10\text{ m/s}$$
  • Parallel component ($\hat{j}$): Motion of the mirror parallel to its own plane ($+5\hat{j}$) does not shift the image: $$v_{Iy} = v_{Oy} = +4\text{ m/s}$$

Combining both components:

$$\vec{v}_I = \mathbf{(-10\hat{i} + 4\hat{j})\text{ m/s}}$$
Level 1 Spherical Mirrors & Magnification Worked Example 2: Dual Positions for Equal Magnification
A concave spherical mirror has a radius of curvature $R = 40\text{ cm}$. Determine the two object positions along the principal axis for which the image formed is magnified exactly $2$ times ($|m| = 2$). State the image distance $v$ and nature of the image in each case.

Step 1 — Establish Sign Convention: For a concave mirror, $R = -40\text{ cm} \implies f = \dfrac{R}{2} = -20\text{ cm}$. Use the direct magnification relation $m = \dfrac{f}{f - u}$.

Case 1 — Real & Inverted Image ($m = -2$):

$$-2 = \frac{-20}{-20 - u_1} \implies 40 + 2u_1 = -20 \implies 2u_1 = -60 \implies u_1 = \mathbf{-30\text{ cm}}$$

Image distance from $m = -v_1/u_1$:

$$v_1 = -m u_1 = -(-2)(-30) = \mathbf{-60\text{ cm}} \quad (\text{real, inverted, } 60\text{ cm in front of mirror})$$

Verification: $\dfrac{1}{-60} + \dfrac{1}{-30} = -\dfrac{3}{60} = -\dfrac{1}{20} = \dfrac{1}{f}$. Exact.

Case 2 — Virtual & Erect Image ($m = +2$):

$$+2 = \frac{-20}{-20 - u_2} \implies -40 - 2u_2 = -20 \implies -2u_2 = +20 \implies u_2 = \mathbf{-10\text{ cm}}$$

Image distance:

$$v_2 = -m u_2 = -(+2)(-10) = \mathbf{+20\text{ cm}} \quad (\text{virtual, erect, } 20\text{ cm behind mirror})$$

Verification: $\dfrac{1}{+20} + \dfrac{1}{-10} = -\dfrac{1}{20} = \dfrac{1}{f}$. Notice that $u_1 = -30\text{ cm}$ and $u_2 = -10\text{ cm}$ sit symmetrically $10\text{ cm}$ ($|f|/|m|$) on either side of the focus $F$ ($-20\text{ cm}$).

Level 1 Human Eye & Vision Correction Worked Example 3: Corrective Lens Powers for Myopia & Hypermetropia
(a) A myopic student cannot see distant objects clearly beyond $80\text{ cm}$ from the eye. Calculate the focal length, nature, and power of the corrective lens required to restore clear vision up to infinity.
(b) A hypermetropic person has a defective near point at $75\text{ cm}$. Calculate the focal length, nature, and power of the spectacle lens required to read a book comfortably at $25\text{ cm}$.

(a) Correction of Myopia: The corrective lens must take an object at $u = -\infty$ and form a virtual erect image at the student's far point $v = -80\text{ cm}$. Applying the thin lens formula:

$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{-80} - \frac{1}{-\infty} = -\frac{1}{80}\text{ cm}^{-1} \implies f = \mathbf{-80\text{ cm}} = -0.80\text{ m}$$

Since $f < 0$, a concave (diverging) lens is needed, with optical power:

$$P = \frac{1}{f\text{ (m)}} = \frac{1}{-0.80} = \mathbf{-1.25\text{ D}}$$

(b) Correction of Hypermetropia: The corrective lens must take a book at the normal near point $u = -25\text{ cm}$ and form a virtual erect image at the person's defective near point $v = -75\text{ cm}$:

$$\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{-75} - \frac{1}{-25} = \frac{-1 + 3}{75} = +\frac{2}{75}\text{ cm}^{-1} \implies f = \mathbf{+37.5\text{ cm}} = +0.375\text{ m}$$

Since $f > 0$, a convex (converging) lens is needed, with optical power:

$$P = \frac{100}{+37.5} = +\frac{8}{3}\text{ D} \approx \mathbf{+2.67\text{ D}}$$
Level 2: Exam-Real Application (Examples 4 – 6)
Level 2 Refraction & Apparent Depth Worked Example 4: Stacked Immiscible Slabs & Fish–Bird Sightlines
(a) A coin rests at the bottom of a tank holding a $12\text{ cm}$ layer of water ($\mu_1 = 4/3$) beneath a $15\text{ cm}$ layer of immiscible oil ($\mu_2 = 3/2$). Find the apparent depth and net upward normal shift of the coin when viewed nearly normally from air ($\mu_0 = 1$).
(b) A fish swims $24\text{ cm}$ below the flat surface of water ($\mu = 4/3$), directly beneath a bird hovering $36\text{ cm}$ above the water surface. Calculate (i) the apparent distance of the bird from the fish as seen by the fish, and (ii) the apparent distance of the fish from the bird as seen by the bird.

(a) Multi-Layer Apparent Depth & Normal Shift: Total real depth is $d_{\text{real}} = 12 + 15 = 27\text{ cm}$. Since apparent depths of parallel layers add directly:

$$d_{\text{app}} = \frac{t_1}{\mu_1} + \frac{t_2}{\mu_2} = \frac{12}{4/3} + \frac{15}{3/2} = 9 + 10 = \mathbf{19\text{ cm}}$$

The net upward normal shift is:

$$\Delta t = d_{\text{real}} - d_{\text{app}} = 27 - 19 = \mathbf{8\text{ cm}}$$

Check via shift sum: $\Delta t = 12(1 - \frac{3}{4}) + 15(1 - \frac{2}{3}) = 3 + 5 = 8\text{ cm}$. Exact.

(b) Fish–Bird Apparent Separations: Remember $d_{\text{app}} = d\left(\dfrac{\mu_{\text{obs}}}{\mu_{\text{obj}}}\right)$ applies only to the segment lying in the other medium!

  • (i) Bird seen by Fish ($\mu_{\text{obs}} = 4/3,\ \mu_{\text{obj}} = 1$): The bird's apparent height above the water surface is $h' = 36 \times \dfrac{4/3}{1} = 48\text{ cm}$. Adding the fish's own depth inside water ($24\text{ cm}$): $$D_{\text{bird/fish}} = 24 + 48 = \mathbf{72\text{ cm}}$$
  • (ii) Fish seen by Bird ($\mu_{\text{obs}} = 1,\ \mu_{\text{obj}} = 4/3$): The fish's apparent depth below the water surface is $d' = 24 \times \dfrac{1}{4/3} = 18\text{ cm}$. Adding the bird's own altitude in air ($36\text{ cm}$): $$D_{\text{fish/bird}} = 36 + 18 = \mathbf{54\text{ cm}}$$
Level 2 Lens Maker's & Displacement Method Worked Example 5: Liquid-Immersion Flip & Bessel's Displacement Method
(a) An equiconvex glass lens ($\mu_g = 1.5$) has a focal length of $+20\text{ cm}$ in air ($\mu_0 = 1$). Calculate its new focal length and optical nature when completely submerged in (i) water ($\mu_w = 4/3$), and (ii) carbon disulphide ($\mu_{\text{CS}_2} = 1.65$).
(b) In a Displacement Method setup, a glowing object and a screen are held fixed $D = 100\text{ cm}$ apart. Shifting a convex lens by $d = 20\text{ cm}$ between two conjugate positions yields two sharp images on the screen of heights $I_1 = 9\text{ cm}$ and $I_2 = 4\text{ cm}$. Find the focal length $f$ of the lens and the true height $O$ of the object.

(a) Liquid Immersion via Lens Maker's Formula: Let the geometry factor be $G = \left(\dfrac{1}{R_1} - \dfrac{1}{R_2}\right)$. In air:

$$\frac{1}{f_a} = (\mu_g - 1)G \implies \frac{1}{20} = (1.5 - 1)G = 0.5\,G \implies G = \frac{1}{10}\text{ cm}^{-1}$$
  • (i) In water ($\mu_w = 4/3$): $$\frac{1}{f_w} = \left(\frac{1.5}{4/3} - 1\right)G = \left(\frac{9}{8} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{10}\right) = +\frac{1}{80}\text{ cm}^{-1} \implies f_w = \mathbf{+80\text{ cm}}\ (\text{converging})$$
  • (ii) In carbon disulphide ($\mu_m = 1.65$): $$\frac{1}{f_m} = \left(\frac{1.5}{1.65} - 1\right)G = \left(\frac{10}{11} - 1\right)\left(\frac{1}{10}\right) = -\frac{1}{110}\text{ cm}^{-1} \implies f_m = \mathbf{-110\text{ cm}}\ (\text{flips to diverging!})$$

(b) Displacement Method: For fixed separation $D = 100\text{ cm}$ and lens shift $d = 20\text{ cm}$:

$$f = \frac{D^2 - d^2}{4D} = \frac{100^2 - 20^2}{4(100)} = \frac{10000 - 400}{400} = \frac{9600}{400} = \mathbf{+24\text{ cm}}$$

Since the two positions are optical conjugates ($u_1 = -40\text{ cm}, v_1 = +60\text{ cm}$ and $u_2 = -60\text{ cm}, v_2 = +40\text{ cm}$), their magnifications satisfy $|m_1|\cdot|m_2| = \dfrac{I_1}{O}\cdot\dfrac{I_2}{O} = 1$:

$$O = \sqrt{I_1 I_2} = \sqrt{9 \times 4} = \sqrt{36} = \mathbf{6\text{ cm}}$$

Self-consistency check: $|m_1| = \frac{60}{40} = 1.5 \implies I_1 = 1.5 \times 6 = 9\text{ cm}$, and $|m_2| = \frac{40}{60} = \frac{2}{3} \implies I_2 = \frac{2}{3} \times 6 = 4\text{ cm}$. Exact match!

Level 2 Prism Minimum Deviation & No Emergence Worked Example 6: Equilateral Prism Optics & Critical Apex Angle
(a) An equilateral glass prism ($A = 60^\circ$) produces a minimum deviation of $\delta_m = 30^\circ$ for monochromatic light in air ($c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$). Calculate: (i) the angle of incidence $i$ at minimum deviation, (ii) the refractive index $\mu$ of the glass, and (iii) the speed of light inside the prism.
(b) Another prism is made of high-index glass with $\mu = 2$. Determine the limiting refracting angle $A_{\max}$ above which no light ray can emerge from the second face for any angle of incidence on the first face.

(a) Equilateral Prism at Minimum Deviation ($A = 60^\circ,\ \delta_m = 30^\circ$):

  • (i) Angle of incidence ($i = e$): $$i = \frac{A + \delta_m}{2} = \frac{60^\circ + 30^\circ}{2} = \mathbf{45^\circ}$$
  • (ii) Refractive index ($\mu$): At minimum deviation, $r_1 = r_2 = \dfrac{A}{2} = 30^\circ$. By Snell's Law: $$\mu = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r_1} = \frac{\sin 45^\circ}{\sin 30^\circ} = \frac{1/\sqrt{2}}{1/2} = \mathbf{\sqrt{2}} \approx 1.414$$
  • (iii) Speed of light in the prism: $$v = \frac{c}{\mu} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{\sqrt{2}} = \mathbf{1.5\sqrt{2} \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} \approx 2.12 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$$

(b) Condition for No Emergence ($\mu = 2$): First find the glass–air critical angle $C$:

$$\sin C = \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{2} \implies C = 30^\circ$$

For any ray entering face 1 ($0^\circ \le i \le 90^\circ$), the internal refracted angle obeys $r_1 \le C = 30^\circ$. Therefore, the angle of incidence at face 2 obeys $r_2 = A - r_1 \ge A - C$. To guarantee TIR at face 2 for every ray (even the grazing ray $i = 90^\circ$), we require $r_{2,\min} > C$:

$$A - C > C \implies A > 2C = 2(30^\circ) = \mathbf{60^\circ}$$

Thus $A_{\max} = \mathbf{60^\circ}$; any prism with $A > 60^\circ$ blocks all rays from emerging through the second face.

Level 3: Rank-Booster & Olympiad Mastery (Examples 7 – 9)
Level 3 Newton's Formula & Moving Mirror Dynamics Worked Example 7: Newton's Formula, Axial Length & Moving Mirror Velocity
(a) A convex lens forms a real image of a real object placed $x_1 = 9\text{ cm}$ outside its first principal focus $F_1$, with the image located $x_2 = 16\text{ cm}$ outside its second principal focus $F_2$. Find the focal length $f$ and transverse magnification $m$.
(b) A tiny rod of length $L_O = 2\text{ mm}$ lies along the principal axis of a concave mirror of focal length $f = -20\text{ cm}$, centered at $u = -30\text{ cm}$ (light travels in the $+x$ direction). At this instant, the rod moves toward the mirror at $v_O = +3\text{ cm/s}$ while the mirror moves away from the rod at $v_M = +1\text{ cm/s}$. Find (i) the image length $L_I$, and (ii) the ground-frame velocity $v_I$ of the image.

(a) Newton's Formula (Full Derivation & Application): With $u = -(f + x_1) = -(f + 9)$ and $v = +(f + x_2) = +(f + 16)$, substituting into $\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$ yields $x_1 x_2 = f^2$:

$$f^2 = (9)(16) = 144 \implies f = \mathbf{+12\text{ cm}}$$

Hence $u = -(12 + 9) = -21\text{ cm}$ and $v = +(12 + 16) = +28\text{ cm}$, giving transverse magnification:

$$m = +\frac{v}{u} = \frac{+28}{-21} = \mathbf{-\frac{4}{3}}$$

(b) Concave Mirror Axial Magnification & Moving Mirror Velocity:

  • Step 1 — Transverse magnification at $u = -30\text{ cm},\ f = -20\text{ cm}$: $$m = \frac{f}{f - u} = \frac{-20}{-20 - (-30)} = \frac{-20}{+10} = -2$$
  • (i) Image length $L_I$: Since $L_O = 2\text{ mm} \ll |u|$, apply differential longitudinal magnification $m_L = -m^2 = -(-2)^2 = -4$: $$L_I = |m_L|\,L_O = 4 \times 2\text{ mm} = \mathbf{8\text{ mm}}$$
  • (ii) Ground-frame image velocity $v_I$: Transform object velocity into the mirror's rest frame: $$v_{O/M} = v_O - v_M = (+3) - (+1) = +2\text{ cm/s}$$ In the mirror's frame, $v_{I/M} = -m^2\,v_{O/M} = -(-2)^2(+2) = -8\text{ cm/s}$. Transforming back to the ground frame: $$v_I = v_M + v_{I/M} = (+1) + (-8) = \mathbf{-7\text{ cm/s}}\ (\text{i.e., } 7\text{ cm/s along } -x)$$
30-Second Exam Trick (Newton Magnification & Moving-Mirror Master Line):
1. From Newton's focal distances alone, skip $u$ and $v$ completely: $f = \sqrt{x_1 x_2}$ and $m = -\sqrt{\dfrac{x_2}{x_1}} = -\sqrt{\dfrac{16}{9}} = -\dfrac{4}{3}$!
2. For any moving spherical mirror along the axis, plug straight into the single line $v_I = (1 + m^2)v_M - m^2 v_O = (1 + 4)(1) - 4(3) = 5 - 12 = \mathbf{-7\text{ cm/s}}$.
Level 3 Silvered Lenses Worked Example 8: Silvered Plano-Convex Lens — Both Orientations
A plano-convex glass lens ($\mu = 1.5$) has one curved face of radius of curvature $R = 30\text{ cm}$. Calculate the equivalent focal length $f_{\text{eq}}$, mirror nature, and the exact object position $u_0$ along the principal axis for which the final image coincides with the object itself when:
(a) the flat (plane) face is silvered, and
(b) the curved (convex) face is silvered.

Step 1 — Focal length of the unsilvered plano-convex lens in air:

$$\frac{1}{f_l} = (\mu - 1)\left(\frac{1}{R} - \frac{1}{\infty}\right) = (1.5 - 1)\left(\frac{1}{30}\right) = +\frac{1}{60}\text{ cm}^{-1} \implies f_l = +60\text{ cm}$$

Use the equivalent mirror equation $\dfrac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \dfrac{1}{f_m} - \dfrac{2}{f_l}$ (derived from $P_{\text{eq}} = 2P_l + P_m$ with $P_m = -1/f_m$).

(a) Flat (Plane) Face Silvered: The silvered flat face acts as a plane mirror ($R_m = \infty \implies f_m = \infty$):

$$\frac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{\infty} - \frac{2}{60} = -\frac{1}{30}\text{ cm}^{-1} \implies f_{\text{eq}} = \mathbf{-30\text{ cm}}\ (\text{equivalent concave mirror})$$

For a spherical mirror, an object and its image coincide strictly at its center of curvature ($u_0 = 2f_{\text{eq}}$):

$$u_0 = 2(-30) = \mathbf{-60\text{ cm}}\ (60\text{ cm in front of the curved face})$$

Ray-trace verification: Placing $O$ at $u = -60\text{ cm} = -f_l$ makes refracted rays parallel inside the glass, so they strike the plane mirror normally and retrace straight back to $O$!

(b) Curved Face Silvered: Light enters the flat face and reflects internally off the curved face, which curves toward the incoming light—acting as a concave mirror of radius $R_m = -30\text{ cm} \implies f_m = \dfrac{R_m}{2} = -15\text{ cm}$:

$$\frac{1}{f_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{-15} - \frac{2}{60} = -\frac{2}{30} - \frac{1}{30} = -\frac{3}{30} = -\frac{1}{10}\text{ cm}^{-1} \implies f_{\text{eq}} = \mathbf{-10\text{ cm}}\ (\text{stronger concave mirror})$$

Self-coincidence occurs at the equivalent center of curvature:

$$u_0 = 2(-10) = \mathbf{-20\text{ cm}}\ (20\text{ cm in front of the flat face})$$

Ray-trace verification: Apparent position of the curved face's center of curvature ($30\text{ cm}$ behind flat interface) viewed through glass ($\mu = 1.5$) is $30 / 1.5 = 20\text{ cm}$ in front of the flat face—exact match!

30-Second Exam Trick (Silvered Plano-Convex Pair):
Lock in the two closed-form magnitudes for any plano-convex glass lens ($\mu, R$):
  • Plane silvered: $f_{\text{eq}} = -\dfrac{R}{2(\mu - 1)} = -\dfrac{30}{2(0.5)} = \mathbf{-30\text{ cm}}$  →  self-coincidence at $2|f_{\text{eq}}| = 60\text{ cm}$.
  • Curved silvered: $f_{\text{eq}} = -\dfrac{R}{2\mu} = -\dfrac{30}{2(1.5)} = \mathbf{-10\text{ cm}}$  →  self-coincidence at $2|f_{\text{eq}}| = 20\text{ cm}$.
Level 3 TIR Window & Grazing Prism Emergence Worked Example 9: Snell's Window Area & Minimum Prism Incidence Angle
(a) A point light bulb rests at the bottom of a wide tank containing water ($\mu = 4/3$) of depth $h = \sqrt{7}\text{ m}$. Find the radius $r$ and surface area $A_{\text{win}}$ of the Circle of Illuminance on the water surface (express area in terms of $\pi$), and state the minimum radius of an opaque circular disc floated on the surface to completely block all escaping light.
(b) A glass prism of refracting angle $A = 75^\circ$ has refractive index $\mu = \sqrt{2}$. Calculate the minimum angle of incidence $i_{\min}$ on the first face so that the light ray just emerges from the second face without undergoing total internal reflection.

(a) Circle of Illuminance (Snell's Window): At the water–air boundary, critical angle $C$ satisfies $\sin C = \dfrac{1}{\mu} = \dfrac{3}{4}$. Computing $\cos C$ and $\tan C$:

$$\cos C = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 C} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{9}{16}} = \frac{\sqrt{7}}{4} \implies \tan C = \frac{\sin C}{\cos C} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{7}}$$

From the right triangle of depth $h = \sqrt{7}\text{ m}$ and surface radius $r$:

$$r = h\tan C = \sqrt{7}\left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{7}}\right) = \mathbf{3\text{ m}}$$

Surface area of the bright circular patch:

$$A_{\text{win}} = \pi r^2 = \mathbf{9\pi\text{ m}^2}\ (\approx 28.29\text{ m}^2)$$

Because all rays striking outside radius $r$ automatically suffer TIR back into the water, an opaque circular disc centered directly above the bulb needs a minimum radius of exactly $r_{\text{disc}} = \mathbf{3\text{ m}}$ to block all escaping light.

(b) Minimum Incidence Angle for Grazing Emergence ($A = 75^\circ,\ \mu = \sqrt{2}$):

  • Step 1 — Critical angle $C$: $\sin C = \dfrac{1}{\mu} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \implies C = 45^\circ$. Since $C < A < 2C$ ($45^\circ < 75^\circ < 90^\circ$), emergence is possible only above a threshold $i_{\min}$.
  • Step 2 — Grazing emergence condition at face 2: Light barely emerges when $e = 90^\circ \implies r_2 = C = 45^\circ$.
  • Step 3 — Internal angle at face 1: From $r_1 + r_2 = A$: $$r_{1,\min} = A - C = 75^\circ - 45^\circ = 30^\circ$$
  • Step 4 — Snell's Law at face 1: $$1\cdot\sin i_{\min} = \mu\sin r_{1,\min} = \sqrt{2}\sin 30^\circ = \sqrt{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \implies i_{\min} = \mathbf{45^\circ}$$
30-Second Exam Trick (Two Direct One-Liners):
1. Snell's Window: Memorize $r = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{\mu^2 - 1}}$; for water ($\mu = 4/3$), $\sqrt{\mu^2 - 1} = \dfrac{\sqrt{7}}{3} \implies r = \dfrac{3h}{\sqrt{7}}$ instantly!
2. Prism Emergence Threshold: Whenever $C < A \le 2C$, jump straight to $\sin i_{\min}=\mu\sin(A - C)=\sqrt{\mu^2 - 1}\sin A - \cos A$. Here $\sqrt{2}\sin(75^\circ - 45^\circ)=\sqrt{2}\sin 30^\circ=1/\sqrt{2} \implies i_{\min}=\mathbf{45^\circ}$.
