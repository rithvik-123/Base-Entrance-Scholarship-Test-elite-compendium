1. Newton's Three Laws, Linear Momentum, Impulse & Variable-Mass Thrust

First-Principles Foundation: Newton's Three Laws of Motion
  1. First Law (Law of Inertia & Definition of Inertial Frames): Every body preserves its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line ($\vec{v} = \text{constant}$) unless acted upon by a non-zero net external force. Deep Physical Role: The First Law is not merely a special case ($F = 0$) of the Second Law; it defines Inertial Reference Frames—frames in which unforced particles have zero acceleration. Mass ($m$) is the quantitative measure of translational inertia.
  2. Second Law (Momentum Rate Law): In an inertial frame, the net external force acting on a system equals the instantaneous rate of change of its linear momentum $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$: $$\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} = \frac{d(m\vec{v})}{dt} = m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} + \vec{v}\frac{dm}{dt}$$ For a system of constant mass ($\frac{dm}{dt} = 0$), this reduces to $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = m\vec{a}$. Crucially, acceleration $\vec{a}$ is an instantaneous effect: it depends solely on the net force acting at that exact instant, carrying zero memory of past forces.
  3. Third Law (Action–Reaction Law): Whenever Body A exerts a force $\vec{F}_{BA}$ on Body B, Body B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force $\vec{F}_{AB} = -\vec{F}_{BA}$ on Body A.
Critical BEST Trap: Why Action–Reaction Pairs NEVER Cancel

A true Newton Third-Law pair must satisfy four strict criteria simultaneously:

  1. Different Bodies: $\vec{F}_{BA}$ acts on B while $\vec{F}_{AB}$ acts on A. Because forces can only cancel when summed on the same Free Body Diagram, an action–reaction pair can never cancel each other to prevent motion.
  2. Equal Magnitude & Opposite Direction: $|\vec{F}_{AB}| = |\vec{F}_{BA}|$ at every instant, even when one body is accelerating or vastly more massive than the other (a mosquito hitting a speeding truck experiences the exact same force magnitude as the truck!).
  3. Simultaneous Existence: Neither force precedes the other; both appear and vanish at the exact same instant.
  4. Same Physical Nature: Gravitational pairs with gravitational; normal contact pairs with normal contact; friction pairs with friction. Classic MCQ Trap: For a book resting on a table, its weight $mg$ (Earth pulling book gravitationally) and the normal force $N$ (table pushing book electromagnetically) are equal and opposite on the same body—they are an equilibrium pair, NOT a Third-Law pair! The Third-Law partner of $mg$ is the book pulling Earth upward; the partner of $N$ is the book pressing down on the table.

Impulse–Momentum Theorem & Rebound Sign-Reversal Trap

Integrating Newton's Second Law $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}$ over a finite collision interval $[t_1, t_2]$ defines Impulse ($\vec{J}$):

$$\vec{J} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec{F}_{\text{net}}\,dt = \vec{p}_f - \vec{p}_i = \Delta \vec{p} = \text{Signed Area under the } F\text{-}t \text{ graph}$$

The average force during contact duration $\Delta t$ is $\vec{F}_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{\vec{J}}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{\Delta \vec{p}}{\Delta t}$. This explains why cricketers pull their hands backward while catching: $\Delta \vec{p}$ is fixed, so increasing $\Delta t$ lowers $F_{\text{avg}}$ proportionally.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Variable-Mass Thrust ($F_{\text{thrust}} = v_{\text{rel}}\dfrac{dm}{dt}$)

When mass enters or leaves a system continuously at rate $\mu_m = \dfrac{dm}{dt}$ with relative speed $v_{\text{rel}}$, the momentum transferred per unit time generates a continuous thrust force:

$$F_{\text{thrust}} = v_{\text{rel}}\left|\frac{dm}{dt}\right|$$
  • Water Jet Striking a Stationary Wall Normally: A jet of density $\rho$, cross-section $A$, and speed $v$ delivers mass at rate $\dfrac{dm}{dt} = \rho A v$.
    • If water splashes parallel to the wall (zero normal rebound): $F_{\text{wall}} = v\dfrac{dm}{dt} = \mathbf{\rho A v^2}$.
    • If water rebounds straight back at speed $v$: $F_{\text{wall}} = (2v)\dfrac{dm}{dt} = \mathbf{2\rho A v^2}$.
  • Sand Falling Vertically onto a Moving Conveyor Belt: Sand drops gently ($v_x = 0$) at rate $\dfrac{dm}{dt}$ onto a horizontal belt moving at constant velocity $v$. Each kilogram of sand must gain horizontal momentum $+v$, requiring the motor to supply an extra horizontal force: $$F_{\text{belt}} = v\frac{dm}{dt}$$ (Notice the power delivered by the motor is $P = Fv = v^2\frac{dm}{dt}$, which is twice the rate of kinetic energy gain $\frac{1}{2}v^2\frac{dm}{dt}$—the missing half dissipates as friction heat when sand accelerates up to belt speed!)

2. Free Body Diagrams (FBD), Normal Reaction, Massive Ropes & Spring–String Cuts

Systematic 5-Step FBD Isolation Protocol
  1. Isolate the Body: Mentally draw a closed boundary around the single object (or chosen sub-system) of interest.
  2. Insert Field Forces First: Draw weight $m\vec{g}$ vertically downward from the center of mass (and $\vec{F}_{\text{pseudo}}$ if working in an accelerating frame).
  3. Walk Around the Boundary for Contact Forces: Every physical touch produces a force: surfaces exert a pushing Normal Reaction ($N$) perpendicular to the interface and Friction ($f$) tangent to it; taut strings exert a pulling Tension ($T$) strictly away from the body along the string; springs exert $kx$ opposing stretch/compression.
  4. NEVER Draw Forces Exerted BY the Body on Others: Only forces acting ON the isolated body belong on its FBD. Never draw "$ma$" as a real force alongside real forces in an inertial frame.
  5. Resolve & Write Equations: Align one axis along the direction of acceleration $\vec{a}$ and the other perpendicular to it, then write $\sum F_{\parallel} = ma$ and $\sum F_{\perp} = 0$.

Apparent Weight in Elevators & Stacked Coins

A weighing machine does not measure gravitational weight $mg$; it strictly reads the Normal Reaction ($N$) exerted on the person standing on its pan (often displayed in $\text{kg-wt}$ as $N/g$). For a passenger of mass $m$ in a lift:

  • Stationary or Constant Velocity ($a = 0$): $N = mg$.
  • Accelerating Upward OR Decelerating while Moving Downward ($\vec{a}$ upward): $N - mg = ma \implies \mathbf{N = m(g + a)}$ (heavier).
  • Accelerating Downward OR Decelerating while Moving Upward ($\vec{a}$ downward): $mg - N = ma \implies \mathbf{N = m(g - a)}$ (lighter).
  • Free Fall (Broken Cable, $a = g$ downward): $N = m(g - g) = \mathbf{0}$ (true apparent weightlessness).
  • Stacked Coins Rule: In a stationary stack of $n$ identical coins of mass $m$ each, the normal force on the $k$-th coin from the bottom exerted by the $(k+1)$-th coin above it equals the total weight of all $(n - k)$ coins resting strictly above that interface: $N = (n - k)mg$.

Massless Strings vs. Massive Ropes ($T(x)$ Distribution)

In an ideal massless string ($m_{\text{string}} = 0$) passing over frictionless massless pulleys, $T_{\text{right}} - T_{\text{left}} = 0 \cdot a = 0$, so tension $T$ is strictly uniform everywhere along a continuous segment.

In a uniform massive rope of total mass $M$ and length $L$ pulled along a smooth horizontal surface by force $F$ at one end ($x = 0$), tension is NOT constant! First find the common acceleration $a = \dfrac{F}{M}$ (or $a = \dfrac{F}{M+m}$ if attached to a trailing block $m$). Next, make an imaginary cut at distance $x$ from the pulled end. The trailing segment of length $(L - x)$ has mass $m_{\text{tail}} = M\left(\dfrac{L - x}{L}\right)$ and is pulled forward solely by internal tension $T(x)$:

$$T(x) = m_{\text{tail}} \cdot a = M\left(\frac{L - x}{L}\right)\left(\frac{F}{M}\right) \implies \mathbf{T(x) = F\left(1 - \frac{x}{L}\right)}$$

Tension drops linearly from $T(0) = F$ at the pulled end to $T(L) = 0$ at the free tail. Similarly, for a uniform heavy rope of mass $M$ and length $L$ hanging vertically from a ceiling, tension at distance $y$ above the bottom free end supports the weight below it: $T(y) = \left(\dfrac{M}{L}y\right)g$.

Lami's Theorem (3-Force Concurrent Equilibrium)

When a particle is in static equilibrium ($\sum \vec{F} = \vec{0}$) under exactly three coplanar, concurrent forces $\vec{F}_1, \vec{F}_2, \vec{F}_3$ forming a closed vector triangle, the Law of Sines yields Lami's Theorem:

$$\frac{F_1}{\sin\alpha} = \frac{F_2}{\sin\beta} = \frac{F_3}{\sin\gamma}$$

where $\alpha$ is the angle strictly between $\vec{F}_2$ and $\vec{F}_3$, $\beta$ is between $\vec{F}_3$ and $\vec{F}_1$, and $\gamma$ is between $\vec{F}_1$ and $\vec{F}_2$ ($\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 360^\circ$). Use Lami's Theorem whenever a bob hangs from two asymmetric angled strings to solve for both tensions in two lines without simultaneous $x,y$ algebra.

Signature Olympiad Trap: Spring Cutting vs. String Cutting

When a support is abruptly snipped at $t = 0$, ideal strings and ideal springs obey opposite continuity laws during the infinitesimal instant $t = 0^+$:

  • Inextensible String Tension ($T$) → Discontinuous: String tension is a rigid constraint force requiring zero macroscopic stretch. The instant a string is cut (or goes slack), its tension collapses instantaneously to zero ($T \to 0$)—and tensions in other connected strings can jump discontinuously to satisfy the new constraints.
  • Ideal Spring Force ($F_s = kx$) → Continuous: A spring's force depends solely on its physical elongation $x$. Because a body of finite mass cannot teleport instantaneously ($\Delta x = 0$ over $\Delta t \to 0$), the spring's extension $x$—and therefore its force $kx$—remains strictly unchanged immediately after cutting another string or support!

3. Connected Bodies, Atwood Machines & Multi-Pulley Constraints (Olympiad Bridge)

Standard Connected-Body Systems (First-Principles System Method)

Whenever connected blocks share the exact same acceleration magnitude $a$, treat the entire assembly as a single 1D "stretched-out chain" first to grab $a$ in 5 seconds, then isolate one block to extract internal Normal ($N$) or Tension ($T$):

  • Blocks in Contact on Smooth Floor: Force $F$ pushes $m_1$ against $m_2$: $$a = \frac{F}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad N_{12} = m_2 a = \left(\frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\right)F$$ Notice: Swapping which side $F$ pushes changes $N_{12}$ even though $a$ stays identical!
  • Simple Vertical Atwood Machine ($m_2 > m_1$ over fixed massless pulley): $$m_2 g - T = m_2 a, \quad T - m_1 g = m_1 a \implies \mathbf{a = \left(\frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_2 + m_1}\right)g}, \qquad \mathbf{T = \frac{2m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}g}$$ Ceiling Hook Clamp Force: Two downward string segments pull on the pulley axle, so the ceiling support force is $R = 2T = \dfrac{4m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}g$ (always strictly less than $(m_1 + m_2)g$ whenever $m_1 \neq m_2$ because the center of mass accelerates downward!).
  • Inclined Atwood Machine ($m_1$ on smooth incline $\theta$, $m_2$ hanging vertically): $$a = \frac{m_2 g - m_1 g\sin\theta}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad T = \frac{m_1 m_2 g(1 + \sin\theta)}{m_1 + m_2}$$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Multi-Pulley Constraints (2 Rigorous Methods)

When a pulley itself moves (a movable pulley), connected blocks no longer share the same acceleration! You must derive the kinematic link between $a_1$ and $a_2$ before writing Newton's Second Law.

Method 1 — String-Length Conservation ($\sum l_i = L = \text{const}$): Measure every block's coordinate from fixed stationary anchors (ceiling or fixed pulley axle), express total string length $L$ in terms of those coordinates, and differentiate twice with respect to time ($d^2 L/dt^2 = 0$). For a movable pulley with one fixed end and two parallel segments pulling mass $B$ while one segment pulls mass $A$ (Figure 18.2):

$$L = (x_{\text{edge}} - x_A) + y_B + (y_B - y_{\text{ceil}}) + \text{wrap} = \text{const} \implies -x_A + 2y_B = \text{const}$$ $$\text{Differentiate twice: } -\ddot{x}_A + 2\ddot{y}_B = 0 \implies \mathbf{a_A = 2a_B}$$

Method 2 — The Virtual Work Shortcut ($\sum \vec{T}\cdot\vec{a} = 0$): Because an ideal inextensible string neither stores nor dissipates mechanical energy, the net total work done by all internal string tensions across the system is identically zero: $\sum \vec{T}_i \cdot d\vec{r}_i = 0$. Differentiating gives $\sum \vec{T}_i \cdot \vec{v}_i = 0$, and for straight-line motion:

$$\mathbf{\sum \vec{T}_i \cdot \vec{a}_i = 0}$$

Instant Check on Figure 18.2: Block A feels tension $T$ parallel to $a_A$ ($+T a_A$); movable pulley/Block B feels upward tension $2T$ opposite to downward $a_B$ ($-2T a_B$). Summing to zero: $T a_A - 2T a_B = 0 \implies a_A = 2a_B$ in 3 seconds!

Figure 18.2: Movable-pulley system showing why a $2:1$ tension advantage always enforces an exact $1:2$ acceleration ratio ($a_A = 2a_B$).
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Wedge–Block Normal-Contact Constraint

Whenever two rigid bodies remain in flush surface contact without digging into each other or separating, their velocity and acceleration components perpendicular to the contact surface must be identical:

$$a_{\text{block},\perp} = a_{\text{wedge},\perp}$$

Classic Application: A vertical rod (constrained to move purely downward at $a_y$) rests on a smooth triangular wedge of inclination $\theta$ sliding horizontally to the right at $a_x$. Projecting both accelerations along the normal to the incline:

$$a_y \cos\theta = a_x \sin\theta \implies \mathbf{a_y = a_x \tan\theta}$$

Notice that the exact same constraint follows immediately from geometry: as the wedge shifts right by $\Delta x$, the point under the rod drops by $\Delta y = \Delta x \tan\theta$.

4. Friction, Limiting Cases, Minimum Pull & Two-Block Slipping

Friction is the electromagnetic contact force acting tangentially along an interface to oppose relative slipping (or the tendency of relative slipping) between two touching surfaces. Crucially, friction opposes relative motion—not necessarily absolute motion! (When you walk forward or when a lower block carries an upper block forward, static friction points strictly in the direction of motion!)

Static vs. Kinetic Friction & The Self-Adjusting Law
  • Static Friction ($f_s$) — No Relative Slipping: Static friction is a self-adjusting constraint force whose magnitude matches whatever tangential push tries to initiate slipping, strictly up to a maximum ceiling called Limiting Friction ($f_{s,\max}$): $$0 \le f_s \le f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N$$ Deadly Exam Trap: NEVER write $f_s = \mu_s N$ automatically! First compute $f_{\text{req}}$ needed to keep the block stationary; only if $f_{\text{req}} > \mu_s N$ does slipping begin.
  • Kinetic Friction ($f_k$) — Active Relative Slipping: Once slipping starts, microscopic cold-welds shear continuously and friction drops slightly to a nearly constant value independent of relative speed (at moderate speeds): $$f_k = \mu_k N \qquad (\text{typically } \mu_k < \mu_s)$$ (If a problem states only a single coefficient $\mu$, take $\mu_s = \mu_k = \mu$.)
Figure 18.3: Classic friction response curve. In the static zone ($F_{\text{app}} \le \mu_s N$), $f_s = F_{\text{app}}$ along a $45^\circ$ line; beyond the limiting peak, friction drops to $f_k = \mu_k N$.

Angle of Friction, Angle of Repose & Minimum Force to Move a Block

  • Total Contact Force ($R$) & Angle of Friction ($\lambda$): The surface exerts both normal $N$ and friction $f$, whose vector resultant is $R = \sqrt{N^2 + f^2}$. At the verge of slipping ($f = \mu_s N$), the maximum angle $\lambda$ made by $R$ with the normal is the Angle of Friction: $$\tan\lambda = \frac{f_{s,\max}}{N} = \mathbf{\mu_s}, \qquad R_{\max} = N\sqrt{1 + \mu_s^2}$$
  • Angle of Repose ($\theta_r$): The steepest incline angle at which a block remains at rest without sliding down. Balancing components along/normal to the incline ($mg\sin\theta_r = f_{s,\max} = \mu_s mg\cos\theta_r$) yields: $$\tan\theta_r = \mu_s \implies \mathbf{\theta_r = \lambda}$$ On a rough incline of angle $\theta$:
    • If $\theta \le \theta_r$ ($\tan\theta \le \mu_s$): Block stays at rest ($a = 0$), and $f_s = mg\sin\theta$ (NOT $\mu_s mg\cos\theta$!).
    • If $\theta > \theta_r$: Block slides downward with $a_{\text{down}} = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$. If projected up the incline, both gravity and friction oppose motion: $a_{\text{up}} = -g(\sin\theta + \mu_k\cos\theta)$.
  • Pulling vs. Pushing a Lawn-Roller & Minimum Force $F_{\min}$: Suppose force $F$ acts at angle $\theta$ with the horizontal on a block of mass $m$:
    • Pulling upward at angle $\theta$: Vertical lift reduces normal force to $N = mg - F\sin\theta$. Impending motion requires $F\cos\theta = \mu_s(mg - F\sin\theta)$, giving: $$F_{\text{pull}}(\theta) = \frac{\mu_s mg}{\cos\theta + \mu_s\sin\theta}$$ Since the denominator $D(\theta) = \cos\theta + \mu_s\sin\theta$ has maximum value $\sqrt{1 + \mu_s^2}$ at $\tan\theta = \mu_s$ (angle of friction!), the exact minimum force needed to move the block is: $$\mathbf{F_{\min} = \frac{\mu_s mg}{\sqrt{1 + \mu_s^2}} = mg\sin\lambda} \qquad \text{at } \theta = \lambda = \tan^{-1}(\mu_s)$$
    • Pushing downward at angle $\theta$: Downward component increases normal force to $N = mg + F\sin\theta$, giving: $$F_{\text{push}}(\theta) = \frac{\mu_s mg}{\cos\theta - \mu_s\sin\theta}$$ Clearly $F_{\text{pull}} < F_{\text{push}}$ for every $\theta > 0$ (pulling is always easier than pushing!). Even more dramatically, if $\cos\theta \le \mu_s\sin\theta$ (i.e., $\cot\theta \le \mu_s$, or $\theta \ge 90^\circ - \lambda$), the denominator turns zero or negative—the block self-locks and cannot be moved by any finite push!
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Two-Block Stacked Friction Protocol

Consider Block A ($m_A$) stacked on top of Block B ($m_B$), with friction $\mu_1$ between A and B and a smooth floor ($\mu_2 = 0$) below B. An external horizontal force $F$ pulls one of the blocks. To find the maximum force $F_{\max}$ before relative slipping begins:

  1. Assume "Moving Together" First: Compute the common acceleration $a_{\text{common}} = \dfrac{F}{m_A + m_B}$.
  2. Isolate the Passive (Unpulled) Block: Identify which block has no external force $F$ acting directly on it—it is accelerated strictly by the interface friction $f$!
  3. Enforce the Friction Ceiling: Require $f_{\text{req}} = m_{\text{passive}}\,a_{\text{common}} \le f_{\max} = \mu_1 m_A g$. Hence the critical common acceleration and critical force are: $$a_{\text{crit}} = \frac{\mu_1 m_A g}{m_{\text{passive}}}, \qquad \mathbf{F_{\max} = (m_A + m_B)\,a_{\text{crit}}}$$
  4. If $F > F_{\max}$ (Relative Slipping): Friction locks at $f_k = \mu_k m_A g$. Solve each block's FBD independently: $a_{\text{passive}} = f_k / m_{\text{passive}}$ and $a_{\text{pulled}} = (F - f_k) / m_{\text{pulled}}$.

5. Out-of-Portion Bridge: Non-Inertial Frames & Pseudo-Force

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Deriving $\vec{F}_{\text{pseudo}} = -m\vec{a}_{\text{frame}}$

Consider a particle of mass $m$ observed from a non-inertial reference frame (such as an accelerating car, lift, or sliding wedge) whose acceleration relative to the ground (inertial frame) is $\vec{a}_{\text{frame}}$. By kinematics of relative acceleration:

$$\vec{a}_{\text{ground}} = \vec{a}_{\text{rel}} + \vec{a}_{\text{frame}}$$

Multiply both sides by $m$ and recall Newton's Second Law in the inertial ground frame ($\vec{F}_{\text{real}} = m\vec{a}_{\text{ground}}$):

$$\vec{F}_{\text{real}} = m\vec{a}_{\text{rel}} + m\vec{a}_{\text{frame}} \implies \mathbf{\vec{F}_{\text{real}} + (-m\vec{a}_{\text{frame}}) = m\vec{a}_{\text{rel}}}$$

Thus, Newton's Second Law can still be used inside an accelerating frame provided we add one fictitious bookkeeping vector—the Pseudo-Force:

$$\mathbf{\vec{F}_{\text{pseudo}} = -m\vec{a}_{\text{frame}}}$$
  • Magnitude: Mass of the observed object ($m$) times acceleration of the reference frame ($a_{\text{frame}}$).
  • Direction: Strictly opposite to $\vec{a}_{\text{frame}}$.
  • No Third-Law Partner: Pseudo-force is an artifact of non-inertial observation, NOT a physical interaction between two bodies, so it has no Newton Third-Law reaction pair.

Three Signature Non-Inertial Applications

  1. Pendulum / Plumb Bob Inside a Horizontally Accelerating Vehicle: In the car's frame (accelerating forward at $\vec{a}$), the hanging bob experiences real weight $mg$ downward, string tension $T$ along the tilted cord, and horizontal backward pseudo-force $ma$. For equilibrium at tilt angle $\theta$ backward from vertical: $$T\sin\theta = ma, \qquad T\cos\theta = mg \implies \mathbf{\tan\theta = \frac{a}{g}}, \qquad \mathbf{T = m\sqrt{g^2 + a^2}}$$ Effective Gravity Shortcut: Combine gravity and pseudo-force into $\vec{g}_{\text{eff}} = \vec{g} - \vec{a}_{\text{frame}}$, tilted backward by $\theta = \tan^{-1}(a/g)$ with magnitude $g_{\text{eff}} = \sqrt{g^2 + a^2}$. The pendulum simply aligns along $\vec{g}_{\text{eff}}$!
  2. Effective Gravity Inside Vertical Elevators: For a lift accelerating vertically at $a$, $\vec{g}_{\text{eff}} = \vec{g} - \vec{a}_{\text{lift}}$ gives $g_{\text{eff}} = g + a$ (lift accelerating upward) or $g_{\text{eff}} = g - a$ (lift accelerating downward). Exam Superpower: Inside a lift, every standard gravity formula (apparent weight $N = m g_{\text{eff}}$, Atwood tension $T = \frac{2m_1 m_2}{m_1+m_2}g_{\text{eff}}$, simple pendulum period $2\pi\sqrt{L/g_{\text{eff}}}$) works immediately by replacing $g \to g_{\text{eff}}$!
  3. Smooth Accelerating Wedge Keeping a Block Stationary (Figure 18.5): For a block $m$ to stay at rest relative to a smooth wedge of inclination $\theta$ accelerating horizontally to the right at $a$, resolve $\vec{F}_{\text{pseudo}} = ma$ (to the left) and $mg$ (downward) along and perpendicular to the incline:
    • Along incline (zero slip): $ma\cos\theta = mg\sin\theta \implies \mathbf{a = g\tan\theta}$.
    • Perpendicular to incline: $N = mg\cos\theta + ma\sin\theta = mg\cos\theta + mg\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta} = \mathbf{\dfrac{mg}{\cos\theta} = mg\sec\theta}$.
    • Required external horizontal push on wedge ($M$) + block ($m$): $\mathbf{F = (M + m)g\tan\theta}$.

Level 1 Impulse Rebound & Water Jet Thrust Worked Example 1: Sign-Reversal Impulse & Continuous Fluid Impact
(a) A $0.4\text{ kg}$ cricket ball moving horizontally at $15\text{ m/s}$ strikes a rigid vertical wall normally and rebounds directly backward at $10\text{ m/s}$. If the ball remains in contact with the wall for $\Delta t = 0.02\text{ s}$, find the magnitude of the impulse imparted by the wall and the average force exerted by the wall.
(b) A horizontal water jet of cross-sectional area $A = 4\text{ cm}^2$ and speed $v = 10\text{ m/s}$ strikes a vertical wall normally and splashes downward parallel to the wall without rebounding. Taking water density $\rho = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$, find the steady horizontal force exerted on the wall.

Part (a) — Rebounding Ball (Watch the Sign Reversal!):

Lock the sign convention: take the initial velocity direction (toward the wall) as $+x$. Then $u = +15\text{ m/s}$ and $v = -10\text{ m/s}$.

$$\Delta p = m(v - u) = 0.4(-10 - 15) = 0.4(-25) = -10\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$$

The negative sign confirms the impulse acts away from the wall. Its magnitude is $J = |\Delta p| = \mathbf{10\text{ N}\cdot\text{s}}$.

Average force exerted by the wall during $\Delta t = 0.02\text{ s}$:

$$F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{10}{0.02} = \mathbf{500\text{ N}}$$

Trap Check: Dropping the negative sign on $v$ would produce $0.4(15 - 10) = 2\text{ N}\cdot\text{s}$ and $100\text{ N}$—five times too small!

Part (b) — Continuous Water Jet:

Convert area to SI units: $A = 4\text{ cm}^2 = 4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$. Mass of water hitting the wall per second:

$$\frac{dm}{dt} = \rho A v = (1000)(4 \times 10^{-4})(10) = 4\text{ kg/s}$$

Since water loses its entire normal velocity ($v \to 0$) with no rebound, the normal force on the wall is:

$$F = v\frac{dm}{dt} = (10)(4) = \mathbf{40\text{ N}}$$

Taking $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$, find (a) the normal reaction $N_{AB}$ between $A$ and $B$, (b) the normal reaction $N_{BC}$ between $B$ and $C$, (c) the normal reaction $N_{\text{floor}}$ exerted by the elevator floor on $C$, and (d) all three normal reactions if the elevator cable snaps into free fall.

  • (c) Isolated FBD of Block $C$ ($5\text{ kg}$): Forces are $N_{\text{floor}}$ upward, reaction $N_{BC} = 60\text{ N}$ downward, and $m_C g = 50\text{ N}$ downward: $$N_{\text{floor}} - 60 - 50 = (5)(2) \implies N_{\text{floor}} = 110 + 10 = \mathbf{120\text{ N}}$$ Combined-system check on $(A + B + C)$: $N_{\text{floor}} = 10(12) = 120\text{ N}$.

Level 1 Self-Adjusting Static Friction Trap Worked Example 3: Testing Below, At, and Above Limiting Friction
A $5\text{ kg}$ wooden block rests on a rough horizontal floor where $\mu_s = 0.5$ and $\mu_k = 0.4$ ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$). Determine the friction force $f$ and acceleration $a$ of the block when a horizontal pulling force $F$ is applied with magnitude: (a) $F = 18\text{ N}$, (b) $F = 25\text{ N}$, and (c) $F = 35\text{ N}$.

Step 1 — Compute Normal Reaction and Both Friction Thresholds:

Vertical equilibrium gives $N = mg = 5 \times 10 = 50\text{ N}$.

$$f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N = 0.5 \times 50 = 25\text{ N}, \qquad f_k = \mu_k N = 0.4 \times 50 = 20\text{ N}$$

Step 2 — Evaluate Each Case Strictly Against $f_{s,\max} = 25\text{ N}$:

  • (a) $F = 18\text{ N}$: Since $18\text{ N} < f_{s,\max} = 25\text{ N}$, the block does not move. Static friction self-adjusts to cancel $F$ exactly: $$f = \mathbf{18\text{ N}}, \qquad a = \mathbf{0\text{ m/s}^2}$$ (Never report $25\text{ N}$—that would imply the block accelerates backward!)
  • (b) $F = 25\text{ N}$: Applied force matches $f_{s,\max}$ exactly (verge of slipping): $$f = \mathbf{25\text{ N}}, \qquad a = \mathbf{0\text{ m/s}^2}$$
  • (c) $F = 35\text{ N}$: Since $35\text{ N} > 25\text{ N}$, static lockup breaks and the block slides. Friction drops instantaneously to kinetic friction $f_k = 20\text{ N}$: $$f = \mathbf{20\text{ N}}, \qquad a = \frac{F - f_k}{m} = \frac{35 - 20}{5} = \mathbf{3\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Level 2 Massive Rope Tension Distribution Worked Example 4: Heavy Rope Pulling a Block Along a Smooth Floor
A block of mass $m = 6\text{ kg}$ resting on a smooth horizontal floor is connected to a uniform heavy rope of mass $M = 4\text{ kg}$ and length $L = 2\text{ m}$. A constant horizontal pulling force $F = 50\text{ N}$ is applied to the free right end of the rope. Find (a) the acceleration of the system, (b) the tension at the point where the rope attaches to the block, (c) the tension at the midpoint of the rope, and (d) the tension at a distance $y = 0.5\text{ m}$ from the pulled right end.

Step 1 — (a) System Acceleration:

Treat the block + entire rope as one rigid 1D system of total mass $M_{\text{total}} = m + M = 6 + 4 = 10\text{ kg}$:

$$a = \frac{F}{m + M} = \frac{50}{10} = \mathbf{5\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Step 2 — (b) Tension at the Block–Rope Junction:

Isolate the $6\text{ kg}$ block alone. The sole horizontal force pulling it forward is $T_{\text{block}}$:

$$T_{\text{block}} = m a = 6 \times 5 = \mathbf{30\text{ N}}$$

Step 3 — (c) Tension at the Midpoint ($1\text{ m}$ from either end):

Make a mental cut at the midpoint. The mass trailing behind the cut consists of the $6\text{ kg}$ block plus half the rope ($2\text{ kg}$), totalling $8\text{ kg}$:

$$T_{\text{mid}} = (6 + 2)(5) = \mathbf{40\text{ N}}$$

Step 4 — (d) Tension at $y = 0.5\text{ m}$ from the Pulled End:

The linear mass density of the rope is $\lambda = \frac{M}{L} = \frac{4}{2} = 2\text{ kg/m}$. The front segment of length $0.5\text{ m}$ has mass $m_{\text{front}} = 2 \times 0.5 = 1\text{ kg}$, while the trailing mass (block + $1.5\text{ m}$ of rope) is $6 + 3 = 9\text{ kg}$.

  • From trailing side: $T = m_{\text{tail}} a = 9 \times 5 = \mathbf{45\text{ N}}$.
  • Independent check from front segment: $F - T = m_{\text{front}} a \implies 50 - T = (1)(5) \implies T = \mathbf{45\text{ N}}$. Confirmed!
Level 2 Spring vs. String Cutting Trap Worked Example 5: Instantaneous Accelerations After Cutting a String
Two blocks $A$ ($m_1 = 2\text{ kg}$, upper) and $B$ ($m_2 = 4\text{ kg}$, lower) hang vertically in equilibrium from a rigid ceiling ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$). Find the instantaneous accelerations (magnitude and direction) of both $A$ and $B$ immediately after the string is cut in each setup:
Case I: Ceiling — Spring — Block $A$ — String — Block $B$ (cut the lower string).
Case II: Ceiling — String — Block $A$ — Spring — Block $B$ (cut the upper string).

Case I (Ceiling — Spring — $A$ — String — $B$; Cut Lower String):

  1. Before cut (equilibrium): Lower string holds $B$ alone $\implies T = m_2 g = 40\text{ N}$. Upper spring holds $(A + B)$ $\implies F_s = (m_1 + m_2)g = 60\text{ N}$.
  2. Immediately after cut ($t = 0^+$): String tension drops instantaneously to $T = 0$, while spring force stays unchanged at $F_s = 60\text{ N}$.
  3. New FBDs at $t = 0^+$:
    • Block $B$: Only weight $m_2 g = 40\text{ N}$ acts downward $\implies a_B = \dfrac{40}{4} = \mathbf{10\text{ m/s}^2\text{ downward}}$.
    • Block $A$: Spring pulls up with $60\text{ N}$, weight pulls down with $m_1 g = 20\text{ N}$: $$60 - 20 = 2 a_A \implies a_A = \frac{40}{2} = \mathbf{20\text{ m/s}^2\text{ upward}}$$

Case II (Ceiling — String — $A$ — Spring — $B$; Cut Upper String):

  1. Before cut (equilibrium): Lower spring holds $B$ alone $\implies F_s = m_2 g = 40\text{ N}$. Upper string holds $(A + B)$ $\implies T = 60\text{ N}$.
  2. Immediately after cut ($t = 0^+$): Upper string tension vanishes ($T = 0$), while the stretched lower spring still pulls $B$ upward with $40\text{ N}$ and pulls $A$ downward with $40\text{ N}$!
  3. New FBDs at $t = 0^+$:
    • Block $B$: Upward $F_s = 40\text{ N}$ balances downward $m_2 g = 40\text{ N} \implies a_B = \mathbf{0\text{ m/s}^2}$ (momentarily suspended in mid-air!).
    • Block $A$: Both weight ($20\text{ N}$) and spring force ($40\text{ N}$) pull downward: $$20 + 40 = 2 a_A \implies a_A = \frac{60}{2} = \mathbf{30\text{ m/s}^2\text{ downward}}$$

Center-of-Mass Sanity Check for Case II: With the ceiling string cut, the entire system is in free fall, so $a_{\text{COM}} = \dfrac{m_1 a_A + m_2 a_B}{m_1 + m_2} = \dfrac{2(30) + 4(0)}{6} = 10\text{ m/s}^2 = g$. Exact!

Level 2 Minimum Pulling Force & Self-Locking Push Worked Example 6: Pulling vs. Pushing a Heavy Crate at an Angle
A heavy crate of mass $m = 10\text{ kg}$ rests on a rough horizontal floor with $\mu_s = \dfrac{3}{4} = 0.75$ ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$; recall the $3\text{-}4\text{-}5$ triangle: $\sin 37^\circ = \frac{3}{5}$, $\cos 37^\circ = \frac{4}{5}$, $\tan 37^\circ = \frac{3}{4}$).
(a) Find the minimum pulling force $F_{\min}$ required to just start moving the crate, and the angle $\theta$ above the horizontal at which it must be applied.
(b) Find the pushing force required to just move the crate if pushed downward at the same angle $37^\circ$ below the horizontal.
(c) Above what downward pushing angle $\theta_{\text{lock}}$ does it become physically impossible to move the crate, no matter how large the push?

(a) Minimum Pulling Force:

When pulling at angle $\theta$ above the horizontal, vertical equilibrium gives $N = mg - F\sin\theta$, and impending horizontal motion requires $F\cos\theta = \mu_s N = \mu_s(mg - F\sin\theta)$:

$$F(\cos\theta + \mu_s\sin\theta) = \mu_s mg \implies F = \frac{\mu_s mg}{\cos\theta + \mu_s\sin\theta}$$

Minimizing $F$ requires maximizing $\cos\theta + \mu_s\sin\theta$, whose maximum is $\sqrt{1 + \mu_s^2}$ attained at $\tan\theta = \mu_s$:

$$\tan\theta = \frac{3}{4} \implies \theta = \mathbf{37^\circ}, \qquad \sqrt{1 + \mu_s^2} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{9}{16}} = \frac{5}{4}$$ $$F_{\min} = \frac{\mu_s mg}{\sqrt{1 + \mu_s^2}} = \frac{(3/4)(100)}{5/4} = \frac{75}{5/4} = \mathbf{60\text{ N}}$$

Check: $N = 100 - 60(0.6) = 64\text{ N}$; $f_{s,\max} = 0.75(64) = 48\text{ N}$; horizontal pull $= 60(0.8) = 48\text{ N}$. Exact!

(b) Pushing at $37^\circ$ Below Horizontal:

Now $N = mg + F\sin 37^\circ$, so $F\cos 37^\circ = \mu_s(mg + F\sin 37^\circ)$:

$$F_{\text{push}} = \frac{\mu_s mg}{\cos 37^\circ - \mu_s\sin 37^\circ} = \frac{75}{0.8 - 0.75(0.6)} = \frac{75}{0.8 - 0.45} = \frac{75}{0.35} = \mathbf{\frac{1500}{7}\text{ N} \approx 214.3\text{ N}}$$

Pushing at $37^\circ$ requires more than 3.5 times the minimum pulling force!

(c) Self-Locking Angle:

Motion becomes impossible whenever the denominator $\cos\theta - \mu_s\sin\theta \le 0$:

$$\tan\theta \ge \frac{1}{\mu_s} = \frac{4}{3} \implies \theta_{\text{lock}} = \mathbf{53^\circ}$$

Any push steeper than $53^\circ$ below horizontal increases limiting friction faster than it increases forward push, locking the crate permanently.

Level 3 Movable Multi-Pulley Constraint Worked Example 7: Table Block Coupled to a Hanging Movable Pulley
In the movable multi-pulley setup of Figure 18.2, Block $A$ ($m_1 = 2\text{ kg}$) rests on a smooth horizontal table and is connected by a light inextensible string passing over a fixed edge pulley $P_1$ and around a light frictionless movable pulley $P_2$ to a fixed ceiling anchor. Block $B$ ($m_2 = 12\text{ kg}$) hangs from the axle of $P_2$. Released from rest with $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$, find (a) the acceleration $a_B$ of Block $B$, (b) the acceleration $a_A$ of Block $A$, and (c) the string tension $T$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 — Constraint Relation (String-Length Conservation): Let $x_A$ measure Block $A$'s distance from the left wall (so table string length is $L_0 - x_A$) and $y_B$ measure movable pulley $P_2$'s vertical depth below the table/ceiling line. Total constant string length is:

$$(L_0 - x_A) + y_B + y_B + \text{const} = L \implies 2y_B - x_A = \text{const}$$

Differentiating twice with respect to time gives the exact kinematic link:

$$a_A = 2a_B$$

Step 2 — Isolated Newton Second-Law Equations: Since the string and pulleys are massless and frictionless, the same tension $T$ acts throughout the string.

  • FBD of Block $A$ (horizontal): $T = m_1 a_A = 2a_A$. Substituting $a_A = 2a_B$ gives $T = 4a_B$.
  • FBD of Movable Pulley $P_2$ + Block $B$ (vertical, downward positive): Two vertical string strands pull upward with total force $2T$, while weight $m_2 g = 120\text{ N}$ pulls downward: $$m_2 g - 2T = m_2 a_B \implies 120 - 2T = 12a_B$$

Step 3 — Solve Simultaneously: Substitute $T = 4a_B$ into the equation for $B$:

$$120 - 2(4a_B) = 12a_B \implies 120 = 20a_B \implies \mathbf{a_B = 6\text{ m/s}^2}$$ $$\mathbf{a_A = 2a_B = 12\text{ m/s}^2}, \qquad \mathbf{T = 4a_B = 24\text{ N}}$$

Verification: On $B$, net force $= 120 - 2(24) = 72\text{ N} = 12 \times 6$. On $A$, net force $= 24\text{ N} = 2 \times 12$. Exact!

30-Second BEST Exam Shortcut: Virtual Work + Inertia Reflection

1) Read tension ratio directly from the diagram: $T$ pulls $A$, $2T$ pulls $B$. By Virtual Work ($\sum \vec{T}\cdot\vec{a} = 0$), acceleration ratio is inverted: $a_A = 2a_B$ (multiplier $n = 2$).

2) Reflecting any mass moving at $n\times$ the speed of the target block multiplies its effective inertia by $n^2$ ($2^2 = 4$):

$$a_B = \frac{\text{Net Driving Force}}{m_2 + n^2 m_1} = \frac{120}{12 + 4(2)} = \frac{120}{20} = \mathbf{6\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Immediately $a_A = 2(6) = \mathbf{12\text{ m/s}^2}$ and $T = 2(12) = \mathbf{24\text{ N}}$.

Level 3 Two-Block Stacked Friction Worked Example 8: Slipping Thresholds & Post-Slip Accelerations
Block $A$ ($m_A = 2\text{ kg}$) rests on top of Block $B$ ($m_B = 4\text{ kg}$), which rests on a smooth horizontal floor (as in Figure 18.4). The coefficient of friction between $A$ and $B$ is $\mu_s = \mu_k = 0.5$ ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$).
(a) A horizontal force $F$ pulls the lower block $B$. Find the maximum force $F_{\max}$ for which both blocks move together without slipping.
(b) If $F = 38\text{ N}$ is applied to the lower block $B$, find the individual accelerations $a_A$ and $a_B$.
(c) Now remove the force from $B$ and apply a horizontal pull $F'$ to the upper block $A$ instead. Find the new maximum force $F'_{\max}$ before relative slipping begins.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

First compute the maximum possible friction force between $A$ and $B$. From vertical equilibrium of $A$, $N_1 = m_A g = 2 \times 10 = 20\text{ N}$:

$$f_{\max} = \mu N_1 = 0.5 \times 20 = 10\text{ N}$$

(a) Force $F$ Applied to Lower Block $B$:

Suppose both blocks move together with common acceleration $a = \dfrac{F}{m_A + m_B} = \dfrac{F}{6}$. Isolating upper block $A$, the only horizontal force acting on $A$ is static friction $f$ directed forward:

$$f = m_A a = 2a \le f_{\max} = 10\text{ N} \implies a_{\max} = \frac{10}{2} = 5\text{ m/s}^2$$

Therefore, the maximum pull on $B$ without slipping is:

$$F_{\max} = (m_A + m_B)a_{\max} = (2 + 4)(5) = \mathbf{30\text{ N}}$$

(b) When $F = 38\text{ N}$ on Lower Block $B$:

Because $38\text{ N} > 30\text{ N}$, the blocks slip relative to each other and friction saturates at $f = f_k = 10\text{ N}$ (forward on $A$, backward on $B$):

  • For Block $A$: $a_A = \dfrac{f_k}{m_A} = \dfrac{10}{2} = \mathbf{5\text{ m/s}^2}$.
  • For Block $B$: $F - f_k = m_B a_B \implies 38 - 10 = 4a_B \implies a_B = \dfrac{28}{4} = \mathbf{7\text{ m/s}^2}$.

Physical Consistency Check: Lower block $B$ accelerates faster ($7\text{ m/s}^2 > 5\text{ m/s}^2$), so $B$ slides forward beneath $A$, confirming that friction indeed opposes $B$ backward and drives $A$ forward!

(c) Force $F'$ Applied to Upper Block $A$:

If both move together at $a = \frac{F'}{6}$, now lower block $B$ is driven forward solely by interface friction $f \le 10\text{ N}$:

$$f = m_B a = 4a \le 10\text{ N} \implies a'_{\max} = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5\text{ m/s}^2$$ $$F'_{\max} = (m_A + m_B)a'_{\max} = (6)(2.5) = \mathbf{15\text{ N}}$$
30-Second BEST Exam Shortcut: The "Passive-Block" Formula

On a smooth floor, always spot the unpulled (passive) block—it sets the ceiling acceleration $a_{\text{crit}} = f_{\max} / m_{\text{passive}}$:

  • Pull $B$ $\implies$ passive is $A$ ($2\text{ kg}$): $F_{\max} = (2+4)\times\frac{10}{2} = \mathbf{30\text{ N}}$.
  • Pull $A$ $\implies$ passive is $B$ ($4\text{ kg}$): $F'_{\max} = (2+4)\times\frac{10}{4} = \mathbf{15\text{ N}}$.

Notice the exact ratio: $F_{\max} / F'_{\max} = m_B / m_A = 4/2 = 2$!

Level 3 Non-Inertial Wedge & Pseudo-Force Worked Example 9: Keeping a Block Stationary on a Smooth Accelerating Wedge
A smooth triangular wedge of mass $M = 8\text{ kg}$ and inclination $\theta = 37^\circ$ ($\sin 37^\circ = \frac{3}{5}$, $\cos 37^\circ = \frac{4}{5}$, $\tan 37^\circ = \frac{3}{4}$) rests on a smooth horizontal floor. A small block of mass $m = 2\text{ kg}$ is placed on its inclined face ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$). Find:
(a) the horizontal acceleration $a$ required for the wedge so that the block remains completely stationary relative to the wedge,
(b) the normal reaction $N$ exerted by the wedge on the block, and
(c) the horizontal external force $F$ that must be pushed onto the wedge.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Inertial Ground Frame & Non-Inertial Verification):

Method A — Inertial Ground Frame (FBD of Block $m$): Only two real forces act on $m$: gravity $mg = 20\text{ N}$ vertically downward and normal reaction $N$ perpendicular to the incline (making angle $37^\circ$ with the vertical). Because the block rides along horizontally with the wedge, $a_x = a$ and $a_y = 0$:

  • Vertical Equilibrium ($a_y = 0$): $$N\cos 37^\circ = mg \implies N\left(\frac{4}{5}\right) = 20 \implies \mathbf{N = 25\text{ N}}$$
  • Horizontal Newton's 2nd Law ($a_x = a$): $$N\sin 37^\circ = ma \implies 25\left(\frac{3}{5}\right) = 2a \implies 15 = 2a \implies \mathbf{a = 7.5\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Method B — Non-Inertial Wedge Frame (Pseudo-Force Check): Add $\vec{F}_{\text{pseudo}} = ma$ horizontally to the left (Figure 18.5). Balancing components along and perpendicular to the incline:

$$ma\cos 37^\circ = mg\sin 37^\circ \implies a = g\tan 37^\circ = 10\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) = \mathbf{7.5\text{ m/s}^2}$$ $$N = mg\cos 37^\circ + ma\sin 37^\circ = 20(0.8) + 2(7.5)(0.6) = 16 + 9 = \mathbf{25\text{ N}}$$

(c) External Horizontal Force $F$ on Wedge: Since $M$ and $m$ move together horizontally at $a = 7.5\text{ m/s}^2$ on a frictionless floor:

$$F = (M + m)a = (8 + 2)(7.5) = \mathbf{75\text{ N}}$$

Isolated Wedge FBD Check: Wedge feels $F$ right and horizontal reaction $N\sin 37^\circ = 15\text{ N}$ left, so $F - 15 = M a = 8(7.5) = 60 \implies F = 75\text{ N}$. Confirmed!

30-Second BEST Exam Shortcut: Tilted Effective Gravity $\vec{g}_{\text{eff}}$

Inside the accelerating wedge, effective gravity $\vec{g}_{\text{eff}} = \vec{g} - \vec{a}$ must point strictly perpendicular to the smooth slope so no tangential component pulls the block. Immediately:

1) $a = g\tan\theta = 10\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) = \mathbf{7.5\text{ m/s}^2}$.
2) $g_{\text{eff}} = g\sec\theta = 10\left(\frac{5}{4}\right) = 12.5\text{ m/s}^2 \implies N = m g_{\text{eff}} = 2(12.5) = \mathbf{25\text{ N}}$.
3) $F = (M + m)g\tan\theta = 10(7.5) = \mathbf{75\text{ N}}$.
