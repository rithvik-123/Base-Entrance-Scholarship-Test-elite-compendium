Similar Triangles & Olympiad Geometry

One idea powers this chapter: parallel lines and equal angles force equal ratios. We start from areas, derive Thales, the similarity criteria, the right-triangle altitude relations and the angle-bisector theorems, then climb to Stewart, Ceva, Menelaus and Mass Points. Every shortcut is proved before it is used.

Chapter Map & Notation

Time split: Part 1 (this file) ≈ 2.5 h · Part 2 practice ≈ 3 h · Part 3 solutions & error forensics ≈ 1.5 h.

Notation: $[XYZ]$ = area of $\triangle XYZ$; $a=BC,\ b=CA,\ c=AB$ (side opposite each vertex); $s=\tfrac{a+b+c}{2}$; $k$ = similarity ratio; a cevian is a segment from a vertex to a point on the opposite side (or its extension).

Engine of the chapter: every ratio problem is solved by one of three tools — (i) similar triangles / BPT, (ii) area ratios, (iii) mass points. Learn to switch between them.

1 · Basic Proportionality Theorem, Midpoint Theorem & Trapezium Traps

Theorem 1.1 — Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales / BPT)

A line parallel to one side of a triangle, meeting the other two sides at distinct points, divides those sides in the same ratio.

$$DE \parallel BC \;\Longrightarrow\; \dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}$$

Proof (areas only). Join $BE$ and $CD$.

  1. $\triangle ADE$ and $\triangle BDE$ have the same height (from $E$ to line $AB$), so $\dfrac{[ADE]}{[BDE]}=\dfrac{AD}{DB}$.
  2. $\triangle ADE$ and $\triangle CED$ have the same height (from $D$ to line $AC$), so $\dfrac{[ADE]}{[CED]}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}$.
  3. $\triangle BDE$ and $\triangle CED$ share base $DE$ and lie between the parallels $DE$ and $BC$, so their heights are equal and $[BDE]=[CED]$.
  4. The right-hand sides of steps 1 and 2 have equal numerators-over-denominators, so $\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}$. $\blacksquare$
Fig. 1.1 — Thales configuration ("A-pattern"). $DE\parallel BC$ with $AD:DB=AE:EC=2:3$, hence $DE=\tfrac25\,BC$ (Section 2).
Theorem 1.2 — Converse of BPT

If a line meets two sides of a triangle and divides them in the same ratio ($\tfrac{AD}{DB}=\tfrac{AE}{EC}$), it is parallel to the third side.

Proof. Suppose not. Draw through $D$ the line $DE'\parallel BC$ meeting $AC$ at $E'$. By Theorem 1.1, $\tfrac{AD}{DB}=\tfrac{AE'}{E'C}$, so $\tfrac{AE'}{E'C}=\tfrac{AE}{EC}$. Add $1$ to both sides: $\tfrac{AE'+E'C}{E'C}=\tfrac{AE+EC}{EC}$, i.e. $\tfrac{AC}{E'C}=\tfrac{AC}{EC}$, so $E'C=EC$ and $E'=E$. Hence $DE\parallel BC$. $\blacksquare$

Equivalent forms (add 1 to both sides): $\dfrac{AB}{DB}=\dfrac{AC}{EC}$ and $\dfrac{AD}{AB}=\dfrac{AE}{AC}$ — use whichever has the unknown.

Theorem 1.3 — Midpoint Theorem and its converse

The segment joining the midpoints $D,E$ of $AB,AC$ is parallel to $BC$ and $DE=\tfrac12 BC$. Conversely, a line through the midpoint of one side parallel to a second side bisects the third side.

Proof. $\tfrac{AD}{DB}=1=\tfrac{AE}{EC}$, so $DE\parallel BC$ (Theorem 1.2). Then $\angle ADE=\angle ABC$ (corresponding) and $\angle A$ is common, so $\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC$ (AA, proved in Section 2) with ratio $\tfrac{AD}{AB}=\tfrac12$; hence $DE=\tfrac12BC$. Converse: if $DE\parallel BC$ and $AD=DB$, Theorem 1.1 gives $AE=EC$. $\blacksquare$

Trap Alert — BPT
  • $\dfrac{DE}{BC}=\dfrac{AD}{AB}$ (part-to-whole), never $\dfrac{AD}{DB}$ (part-to-part).
  • Ratios are measured from the same vertex on both sides: $\tfrac{AD}{DB}=\tfrac{AE}{EC}$. The mixture $\tfrac{AD}{EC}$ means nothing.
  • The converse needs both ratios in the same orientation. $\tfrac{AD}{DB}=\tfrac{EC}{AE}$ does not imply $DE\parallel BC$.
Theorem 1.4 — Diagonals of a Trapezium

In trapezium $ABCD$ with $AB\parallel CD$, $AB=p$, $CD=q$, diagonals meeting at $O$:

  1. $\dfrac{AO}{OC}=\dfrac{BO}{OD}=\dfrac{p}{q}$
  2. $\dfrac{[AOB]}{[COD]}=\dfrac{p^2}{q^2}$, $\;[AOD]=[BOC]$, $\;[AOD]^2=[AOB]\cdot[COD]$
  3. The line through $O$ parallel to the bases meets legs $AD,BC$ at $P,Q$ with $PO=OQ$ and $PQ=\dfrac{2pq}{p+q}$ (harmonic mean).

Proof. (1) $\angle OAB=\angle OCD$ and $\angle OBA=\angle ODC$ (alternate angles), so $\triangle OAB\sim\triangle OCD$ (AA) with ratio $\tfrac pq$.

(2) Areas of similar triangles scale as $k^2$ (Theorem 2.5). $\triangle ABD$ and $\triangle ABC$ have the same base $AB$ and lie between the same parallels, so $[ABD]=[ABC]$; subtracting $[AOB]$ gives $[AOD]=[BOC]$. Also $\tfrac{[AOD]}{[AOB]}=\tfrac{OD}{OB}=\tfrac{[COD]}{[BOC]}$ (same apex, bases on $BD$), so $[AOD]\cdot[BOC]=[AOB]\cdot[COD]$, and with $[AOD]=[BOC]$ the third relation follows.

(3) $\tfrac{AO}{AC}=\tfrac{p}{p+q}$. In $\triangle ADC$, $PO\parallel DC$, so $\tfrac{PO}{DC}=\tfrac{AO}{AC}$, giving $PO=\tfrac{pq}{p+q}$. In $\triangle BCD$, $OQ\parallel DC$, so $\tfrac{OQ}{DC}=\tfrac{BO}{BD}=\tfrac{p}{p+q}$, giving $OQ=\tfrac{pq}{p+q}$. $\blacksquare$

Fig. 1.2 — Trapezium $ABCD$, $AB=p$, $CD=q$. $PQ\parallel AB$ passes through $O$; $PO=OQ=\tfrac{pq}{p+q}$.
Trap Alert — Trapezium
  • $AO:OC=AB:CD$ (the bases), not $AD:BC$ (the legs).
  • Segment ratio is $k$, but area ratio of the "base triangles" is $k^2$. The two "side triangles" $AOD,BOC$ are always equal in area.
  • The parallel through $O$ has length equal to the harmonic mean $\tfrac{2pq}{p+q}$, not the arithmetic mean $\tfrac{p+q}{2}$ (that one is the mid-line).
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Intercept Theorem

BPT generalises: three or more parallel lines cut every transversal in proportional segments. If parallels $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ cut one transversal in $x:y$ they cut every transversal in $x:y$. In NTSE problems, spotting "parallel + two transversals" instantly gives a ratio without any construction. When no parallel is given, draw one — through a point on a cevian, parallel to the base — and BPT does the rest.

2 · Similarity Criteria, Correspondence & Ratios of Linear Elements and Areas

Definition & Theorems 2.1–2.3 — Similarity Criteria

$\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR$ means $\angle A=\angle P,\ \angle B=\angle Q,\ \angle C=\angle R$ and $\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=\tfrac{BC}{QR}=\tfrac{CA}{RP}=k$. For triangles either half implies the other:

  • AA: two pairs of equal angles.
  • SAS: $\angle A=\angle P$ and $\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=\tfrac{AC}{PR}$ (the equal angle is included).
  • SSS: $\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=\tfrac{BC}{QR}=\tfrac{CA}{RP}$.

Common construction. Mark $D$ on ray $PQ$ with $PD=AB$ and $E$ on ray $PR$ with $PE=AC$.

Proof of AA. Since $\angle P=\angle A$, $\triangle PDE\cong\triangle ABC$ by SAS, so $\angle PDE=\angle B=\angle Q$. Corresponding angles are equal, so $DE\parallel QR$. By BPT (in $\triangle PQR$ if $D,E$ fall inside the sides, in $\triangle PDE$ with line $QR$ if they fall outside), $\tfrac{PD}{PQ}=\tfrac{PE}{PR}$, i.e. $\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=\tfrac{AC}{PR}$. Repeating at vertex $Q$ gives $\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=\tfrac{BC}{QR}$. $\blacksquare$

Proof of SAS. With the same $D,E$: $\tfrac{PD}{PQ}=\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=\tfrac{AC}{PR}=\tfrac{PE}{PR}$ (given). Both points lie inside or both outside, so the converse of BPT gives $DE\parallel QR$, hence $\angle PDE=\angle Q$ and $\angle PED=\angle R$. But $\triangle PDE\cong\triangle ABC$ (SAS), so $\angle B=\angle Q$, $\angle C=\angle R$: AA applies. $\blacksquare$

Proof of SSS. The same ratio equality gives $DE\parallel QR$, so $\triangle PDE\sim\triangle PQR$ (AA) and $\tfrac{DE}{QR}=\tfrac{PD}{PQ}=\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=\tfrac{BC}{QR}$ (given). Hence $DE=BC$, so $\triangle PDE\cong\triangle ABC$ (SSS), giving $\angle A=\angle P$ and $\angle B=\angle PDE=\angle Q$: AA applies. $\blacksquare$

SAS One angle + the two sides enclosing it in the same ratio $AB\cdot AC$ type products; overlapping triangles sharing an angle

SSA Does not work in general "Two sides and a non-included angle are proportional" (a trap)
Counter-example — why SSA fails

Fix $\angle A=30^\circ$ and $AB=8$. The distance from $B$ to the other ray from $A$ is $8\sin30^\circ=4$. Choose $BC=5$ with $4<5<8$: the circle of radius $5$ about $B$ cuts that ray at two points $C_1,C_2$ (here $AC=4\sqrt3\pm3$). Triangles $ABC_1$ and $ABC_2$ share $\angle A$, $AB$ and $BC$ (the "SSA" data), but $AC_1\ne AC_2$. Any similarity matching $AB\leftrightarrow AB$ and $BC\leftrightarrow BC$ would have $k=1$, i.e. congruence — contradiction. Safe cases: the angle is right (RHS) or the side opposite it is at least as long as the other given side.

Correspondence Rule

$\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR$ pairs $A\!\leftrightarrow\!P,\ B\!\leftrightarrow\!Q,\ C\!\leftrightarrow\!R$ in the order written. So $AB\!\leftrightarrow\!PQ$, $BC\!\leftrightarrow\!QR$, $CA\!\leftrightarrow\!RP$. Sides opposite equal angles correspond. Writing $\triangle ABC\sim\triangle QPR$ is a different statement. In overlapping figures, identify the equal angles first, then write the names — never copy names from the picture.

Theorem 2.4 — Corresponding Linear Elements

If $\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR$ with ratio $k$, then every pair of corresponding linear elements has ratio $k$: sides, perimeters, altitudes, medians, angle bisectors, inradii, circumradii.

Proof. Altitudes: with $AD\perp BC$, $PS\perp QR$: $\triangle ABD\sim\triangle PQS$ (AA: $\angle B=\angle Q$, right angles), so $\tfrac{AD}{PS}=\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=k$. Medians $AM,PN$: $\angle B=\angle Q$ and $\tfrac{AB}{PQ}=\tfrac{BC/2}{QR/2}=\tfrac{BM}{QN}$, so $\triangle ABM\sim\triangle PQN$ (SAS) and $\tfrac{AM}{PN}=k$. Angle bisectors: $\angle B=\angle Q$ and $\angle BAD=\tfrac12\angle A=\tfrac12\angle P=\angle QPS$ (AA), ratio $k$. Perimeter: sum of three sides each scaled by $k$. Inradius, circumradius: $r=\tfrac{[ABC]}{s}$ and $R=\tfrac{abc}{4[ABC]}$; with areas scaling by $k^2$ (below) we get $\tfrac{k^2}{k}=k$ and $\tfrac{k^3}{k^2}=k$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2.5 — Ratio of Areas $$\dfrac{[ABC]}{[PQR]}=\left(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}\right)^2=k^2$$

Proof: $[ABC]=\tfrac12\,BC\cdot AD$ and $[PQR]=\tfrac12\,QR\cdot PS$, so the ratio is $\tfrac{BC}{QR}\cdot\tfrac{AD}{PS}=k\cdot k$. $\blacksquare$ (Same-height triangles: areas ∝ bases. Same-base triangles: areas ∝ heights.)

Theorem 2.6 — Triangles Sharing an Angle

If $D\in AB$, $E\in AC$ then $\dfrac{[ADE]}{[ABC]}=\dfrac{AD}{AB}\cdot\dfrac{AE}{AC}$.

Proof: $\tfrac{[ADE]}{[ABE]}=\tfrac{AD}{AB}$ (same apex $E$) and $\tfrac{[ABE]}{[ABC]}=\tfrac{AE}{AC}$ (same apex $B$); multiply. $\blacksquare$

Quantity (ratio of $\triangle ABC$ to $\triangle PQR$) Ratio
Sides, perimeter, semi-perimeter $k$
Altitudes, medians, angle bisectors $k$
Inradius $r$, circumradius $R$ $k$
Area $k^2$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Two Reflex Patterns

A-pattern: $DE\parallel BC$ inside $\triangle ABC$ gives $\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC$ (common angle + corresponding angles). X-pattern: two parallel segments $AB\parallel CD$ joined by crossing lines through $O$ give $\triangle OAB\sim\triangle OCD$ (alternate + vertical angles). Whenever a problem hands you a ratio of lengths and asks for a ratio of areas (or vice versa), square or root it — and check that the ratio is of corresponding elements.

3 · Right-Triangle Altitude Theorem, Pythagoras & Crossing Poles

Theorem 3.1 — Altitude on the Hypotenuse

In right $\triangle ABC$ with $\angle C=90^\circ$, $CD\perp AB$, let $a=BC$, $b=CA$, $c=AB$, $h=CD$, $x=BD$, $y=AD$ (so $x+y=c$). Then

$$h^2=xy,\qquad a^2=cx,\qquad b^2=cy,\qquad h=\dfrac{ab}{c},\qquad \dfrac1{h^2}=\dfrac1{a^2}+\dfrac1{b^2}$$

Each leg is the geometric mean of the hypotenuse and the part of it adjacent to that leg.

Fig. 3.1 — $\angle C=90^\circ$, $CD\perp AB$. $x=BD$ (touches leg $a$), $y=AD$ (touches leg $b$). Three similar triangles: $\triangle ABC\sim\triangle CBD\sim\triangle ACD$.

Proof. (i) $\triangle CBD$ and $\triangle ABC$: $\angle B$ common, $\angle CDB=\angle ACB=90^\circ$ ⇒ AA, so $\tfrac{BC}{AB}=\tfrac{BD}{BC}$, i.e. $a^2=cx$. (ii) $\triangle ACD\sim\triangle ABC$ (common $\angle A$, right angles) gives $\tfrac{AC}{AB}=\tfrac{AD}{AC}$, i.e. $b^2=cy$. (iii) $\angle BCD=90^\circ-\angle B=\angle A$ and $\angle ACD=\angle B$, so $\triangle BCD\sim\triangle CAD$ (with $B\to C,\ C\to A,\ D\to D$), giving $\tfrac{BD}{CD}=\tfrac{CD}{AD}$, i.e. $h^2=xy$. (iv) Area two ways: $\tfrac12ab=\tfrac12ch$, so $h=\tfrac{ab}{c}$, then $\tfrac1{h^2}=\tfrac{c^2}{a^2b^2}=\tfrac{a^2+b^2}{a^2b^2}=\tfrac1{a^2}+\tfrac1{b^2}$ (using Pythagoras below). $\blacksquare$

Pythagoras for free: adding (i) and (ii): $a^2+b^2=c(x+y)=c^2$.

Converse of Pythagoras. If $a^2+b^2=c^2$, build a right triangle with legs $a,b$; its hypotenuse is $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=c$. The two triangles are congruent by SSS, so the original has a right angle opposite $c$. $\blacksquare$ Useful triples: $(3,4,5),(5,12,13),(8,15,17),(7,24,25),(20,21,29),(9,40,41)$ and their multiples.

Theorem 3.2 — Crossing Poles / Ladders (Harmonic Height)

Two vertical poles of heights $a$ and $b$ stand on level ground, any distance $d$ apart. Wires join the top of each to the foot of the other and cross at height $h$ above the ground. Then

$$\dfrac1h=\dfrac1a+\dfrac1b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h=\dfrac{ab}{a+b},\qquad\text{independent of } d.$$
Fig. 3.2 — Wires $AQ$ and $BP$ cross at $X$; $XF\perp PQ$, $XF=h$. Pole $AP=a$, pole $BQ=b$.

Proof. Let $PF=u$, $FQ=v$, $u+v=d$. $XF\parallel AP$, so $\triangle QXF\sim\triangle QAP$ and $\tfrac ha=\tfrac vd$. Likewise $XF\parallel BQ$, so $\triangle PXF\sim\triangle PBQ$ and $\tfrac hb=\tfrac ud$. Adding, $h\!\left(\tfrac1a+\tfrac1b\right)=\tfrac{u+v}{d}=1$. $\blacksquare$ Also $u:v=a:b$: the foot $F$ lies nearer the shorter pole.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Poles Are a Trapezium

$APQB$ is a trapezium with parallel sides $a,b$; $X$ is its diagonal intersection. The line through $X$ parallel to the bases has total length $\tfrac{2ab}{a+b}$ (Theorem 1.4) and $X$ is its midpoint, so $XF=\tfrac{ab}{a+b}$. Crossing ladders in an alley, intersecting wires between buildings and "two cevians in a trapezium" are one problem in three costumes. Also, $\tfrac1h=\tfrac1a+\tfrac1b$ is the formula for two resistors in parallel — a memory aid.

4 · Angle Bisector Theorems & Bisector Length

Theorem 4.1 — Internal Angle Bisector Theorem

If $AD$ bisects $\angle A$ with $D$ on $BC$, then $\dfrac{BD}{DC}=\dfrac{AB}{AC}=\dfrac cb$, so $BD=\dfrac{ac}{b+c}$, $DC=\dfrac{ab}{b+c}$. The converse also holds.

Proof. Through $C$ draw $CE\parallel AD$ meeting $BA$ produced at $E$. Then $\angle AEC=\angle BAD$ (corresponding) and $\angle ACE=\angle DAC$ (alternate). Since $\angle BAD=\angle DAC$, $\angle AEC=\angle ACE$, so $AE=AC$. BPT in $\triangle BEC$ (with $AD\parallel EC$): $\tfrac{BD}{DC}=\tfrac{BA}{AE}=\tfrac{AB}{AC}$. $\blacksquare$ Converse: if $\tfrac{BD}{DC}=\tfrac{AB}{AC}$, choose $E$ on $BA$ produced with $AE=AC$; the converse of BPT gives $AD\parallel EC$, and the same angle chase shows $AD$ bisects $\angle A$.

Theorem 4.2 — External Angle Bisector Theorem

If $AB\ne AC$ and the bisector of the exterior angle at $A$ meets line $BC$ at $D'$, then $\dfrac{BD'}{CD'}=\dfrac{AB}{AC}$. (If $AB>AC$, $D'$ lies beyond $C$.)

Proof. Let $AB>AC$, extend $BA$ to $P$ beyond $A$. Through $C$ draw $CE\parallel AD'$ meeting $AB$ at $E$. Then $\angle AEC=\angle PAD'$ (corresponding) and $\angle ACE=\angle CAD'$ (alternate); as $\angle PAD'=\angle CAD'$, $AE=AC$. BPT in $\triangle BAD'$: $\tfrac{BE}{EA}=\tfrac{BC}{CD'}$, i.e. $\tfrac{AB-AC}{AC}=\tfrac{BC}{CD'}$, so $CD'=\tfrac{BC\cdot AC}{AB-AC}$ and $BD'=BC+CD'=\tfrac{BC\cdot AB}{AB-AC}$. Divide: $\tfrac{BD'}{CD'}=\tfrac{AB}{AC}$. $\blacksquare$ Since the internal and external bisectors are perpendicular (they bisect a linear pair), $\angle DAD'=90^\circ$.

Theorem 4.3 — Length of the Angle Bisector $$l_a^2=AD^2=bc-mn,\quad m=BD,\ n=DC;\qquad l_a=\dfrac{2\sqrt{bc\,s(s-a)}}{b+c}$$

Proof. Extend $AD$ to meet the circumcircle of $\triangle ABC$ again at $E$. $\angle BAD=\angle EAC$ (bisector) and $\angle ABD=\angle AEC$ (same arc $AC$), so $\triangle ABD\sim\triangle AEC$ and $\tfrac{AB}{AE}=\tfrac{AD}{AC}$, i.e. $AB\cdot AC=AD\cdot AE=AD(AD+DE)=AD^2+AD\cdot DE$. Chords $AE$ and $BC$ meet at $D$: $\triangle ADB\sim\triangle CDE$ gives $AD\cdot DE=BD\cdot DC=mn$. So $bc=AD^2+mn$. Substituting $m=\tfrac{ac}{b+c}$, $n=\tfrac{ab}{b+c}$: $l_a^2=bc\!\left[1-\tfrac{a^2}{(b+c)^2}\right]=\tfrac{bc(b+c+a)(b+c-a)}{(b+c)^2}=\tfrac{4bc\,s(s-a)}{(b+c)^2}$. $\blacksquare$ (Stewart in Section 5 gives the same result.)

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Harmonic Division & the Incentre

$D$ (internal) and $D'$ (external) divide $BC$ in the same ratio $AB:AC$, with $\angle DAD'=90^\circ$. So $A$ lies on the circle with diameter $DD'$ — the Apollonius circle, the locus of points whose distances from $B$ and $C$ are in the fixed ratio $c:b$. Another big shortcut: the incentre $I$ lies on $AD$, and applying Theorem 4.1 to the bisector $BI$ in $\triangle ABD$ gives $\dfrac{AI}{ID}=\dfrac{AB}{BD}=\dfrac{b+c}{a}$.

5 · Stewart's Theorem & Apollonius's Median Theorem

Theorem 5.1 — Stewart's Theorem

In $\triangle ABC$ let $D$ lie on $BC$ with $BD=m$, $DC=n$, $a=m+n$, and cevian $AD=d$. Then

$$b^2m+c^2n=a\,(d^2+mn)$$

Memory line: "man + dad = bmb + cnc" i.e. $mna+d^2a=b^2m+c^2n$. Note the cross pairing: each side squared multiplies the segment next to the other vertex ($b=CA$ with $m=BD$; $c=AB$ with $n=DC$).

Fig. 5.1 — Stewart configuration: $BD=m$, $DC=n$, $AD=d$, $AB=c$, $AC=b$.

Proof. Drop $AH\perp BC$, let $h=AH$ and let $p=DH$ (signed: positive towards $C$). Pythagoras in the three right triangles: $d^2=h^2+p^2$, $c^2=h^2+(m+p)^2=d^2+m^2+2mp$, $b^2=h^2+(n-p)^2=d^2+n^2-2np$. Multiply the second by $n$, the third by $m$ and add: $nc^2+mb^2=(m+n)d^2+mn(m+n)+2mnp-2mnp=a(d^2+mn)$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 5.2 — Apollonius's Median Theorem (special case)

Put $m=n=\tfrac a2$ (so $D$ is the midpoint and $d=m_a$): $\;b^2+c^2=2\!\left(m_a^2+\dfrac{a^2}{4}\right)$, hence

$$m_a=\tfrac12\sqrt{2b^2+2c^2-a^2},\qquad m_a^2+m_b^2+m_c^2=\tfrac34\,(a^2+b^2+c^2).$$

Check by Stewart: for the angle bisector $m=\tfrac{ac}{b+c}$, $n=\tfrac{ab}{b+c}$: $b^2m+c^2n=\tfrac{abc(b+c)}{b+c}=abc$, so $abc=a(d^2+mn)$ and $d^2=bc-mn$ — Theorem 4.3 recovered.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — When to Reach for Stewart

Stewart is the "length of a cevian" machine: use it when you know all three sides and the ratio $m:n$, or when you know a cevian and two sub-lengths and want a side. Sanity checks before trusting an answer: $D\to B$ ($m=0$, $d=c$) and $D\to C$ ($n=0$, $d=b$) must reproduce $c^2a=ac^2$ and $b^2a=ab^2$. The centroid $G$ divides each median $2:1$ (proved by Ceva/mass points below), so $AG=\tfrac23m_a$ — a key link in Example 9.

6 · Ceva's Theorem & Menelaus's Theorem

Theorem 6.1 — Ceva's Theorem

Let $D,E,F$ be interior points of $BC,CA,AB$. The cevians $AD,BE,CF$ are concurrent if and only if

$$\dfrac{BD}{DC}\cdot\dfrac{CE}{EA}\cdot\dfrac{AF}{FB}=1.$$

Area form: at the concurrency point $P$, $\dfrac{BD}{DC}=\dfrac{[ABP]}{[ACP]}$, $\dfrac{CE}{EA}=\dfrac{[BCP]}{[BAP]}$, $\dfrac{AF}{FB}=\dfrac{[CAP]}{[CBP]}$.

Fig. 6.1 — Concurrent cevians: $BD:DC=1:3$, $CE:EA=2:1$, $AF:FB=3:2$. Check: $\tfrac13\cdot2\cdot\tfrac32=1$.

Proof (⇒). If $P$ lies on $AD$: $\tfrac{[ABD]}{[ACD]}=\tfrac{BD}{DC}$ and $\tfrac{[PBD]}{[PCD]}=\tfrac{BD}{DC}$ (same heights). When $\tfrac xy=\tfrac zw$, also $\tfrac{x-z}{y-w}=\tfrac xy$; hence $\tfrac{BD}{DC}=\tfrac{[ABD]-[PBD]}{[ACD]-[PCD]}=\tfrac{[ABP]}{[ACP]}$. Similarly $\tfrac{CE}{EA}=\tfrac{[BCP]}{[BAP]}$ and $\tfrac{AF}{FB}=\tfrac{[CAP]}{[CBP]}$. Multiply: every area cancels, product $=1$.

Proof (⇐). Let $AD$ and $BE$ meet at $P$ and let $CP$ meet $AB$ at $F'$. By (⇒), $\tfrac{BD}{DC}\tfrac{CE}{EA}\tfrac{AF'}{F'B}=1$. Comparing with the hypothesis, $\tfrac{AF'}{F'B}=\tfrac{AF}{FB}$, so $F'=F$ (a ratio fixes a unique point on a segment). $\blacksquare$

Ceva in Action — Four Famous Concurrencies
  • Medians: all ratios $1$ ⇒ centroid $G$.
  • Internal bisectors: $\tfrac cb\cdot\tfrac ac\cdot\tfrac ba=1$ ⇒ incentre.
  • Altitudes (acute triangle): $BD=c\cos B$, $DC=b\cos C$, etc.; product $\tfrac{c\cos B}{b\cos C}\cdot\tfrac{a\cos C}{c\cos A}\cdot\tfrac{b\cos A}{a\cos B}=1$ ⇒ orthocentre.
  • Incircle touch-points: equal tangent lengths make the product $1$ ⇒ Gergonne point (Example 7).
Theorem 6.2 — Menelaus's Theorem

A line (not through a vertex) meets the lines $BC,CA,AB$ at $D,E,F$. Then, with unsigned lengths,

$$\dfrac{AF}{FB}\cdot\dfrac{BD}{DC}\cdot\dfrac{CE}{EA}=1.$$

The transversal meets either two sides and one extended side, or all three extended sides. Converse: if the product is $1$ and an odd number (1 or 3) of $D,E,F$ lie on extensions, then $D,E,F$ are collinear.

Fig. 6.2 — Transversal $FED$: $AF:FB=1:1$, $CE:EA=1:3$, $CD:BC=1:2$ so $BD:DC=3:1$. Check: $1\cdot3\cdot\tfrac13=1$.

Proof. Drop perpendiculars from $A,B,C$ to the transversal, of lengths $p,q,r$. Right triangles $B\!\cdot\!D$ and $C\!\cdot\!D$ (feet on the line) share $\angle$ at $D$, so they are similar (AA): $\tfrac{BD}{DC}=\tfrac qr$. In the same way $\tfrac{CE}{EA}=\tfrac rp$ and $\tfrac{AF}{FB}=\tfrac pq$. The product is $\tfrac pq\cdot\tfrac qr\cdot\tfrac rp=1$. $\blacksquare$

Zig-zag mnemonic: walk $A\to F\to B\to D\to C\to E\to A$; write each step as (distance to the point)/(distance onward). The product of the three fractions is $1$.

7 · Mass Point Geometry

Theorem 7.1 — Mass Point Rules
  1. If $D\in BC$ with $BD:DC=m:n$, give $B$ mass $n$ and $C$ mass $m$ (heavier on the shorter side). Then $w_B\cdot BD=w_C\cdot DC$, and $D$ carries mass $w_B+w_C$.
  2. With masses $w_A,w_B,w_C$ the cevians through $A,B,C$ meet at one point $P$ (the centre of mass) and $\dfrac{AP}{PD}=\dfrac{w_B+w_C}{w_A}$.
  3. Areas: $[PBC]:[PCA]:[PAB]=w_A:w_B:w_C$.

Proof (weighted averages). Let $W=w_A+w_B+w_C$ and define $P=\tfrac{w_AA+w_BB+w_CC}{W}$ and $D=\tfrac{w_BB+w_CC}{w_B+w_C}$. Then $D-B=\tfrac{w_C}{w_B+w_C}(C-B)$, so $D\in BC$ with $\tfrac{BD}{DC}=\tfrac{w_C}{w_B}$. Also $P=\tfrac{w_AA+(w_B+w_C)D}{W}$, so $P\in AD$ with $\tfrac{AP}{PD}=\tfrac{w_B+w_C}{w_A}$. The same argument works from $B$ and $C$, so all three cevians pass through $P$ (this also reproves Ceva). Areas: $\tfrac{[PBC]}{[ABC]}=\tfrac{PD}{AD}=\tfrac{w_A}{W}$. $\blacksquare$

Fig. 7.1 — $BD:DC=2:3$, $AE:EC=3:2$. Masses $A=4,B=9,C=6$ give $D=15$, $E=10$; hence $AP:PD=15:4$ and $BP:PE=10:9$.
Mass-Point Cookbook (30 seconds)
  1. On cevian $AD$: $BD:DC=2:3$ ⇒ $B=3$, $C=2$, $D=5$.
  2. On cevian $BE$: $AE:EC=3:2$ needs $w_A\cdot3=w_C\cdot2$. Vertex $C$ already has mass $2$, but $w_A$ would be $\tfrac43$ — so scale the first set by 3: $B=9$, $C=6$, $D=15$; then $w_A=4$, $E=w_A+w_C=10$.
  3. Read off: $AP:PD=w_D:w_A=15:4$; $BP:PE=w_E:w_B=10:9$.

Rules of thumb: scale so a shared vertex gets one mass; masses give ratios only (use $\tfrac{AP}{AD}$ with a known length to get lengths); a line cutting an extended side is a Menelaus job (or use negative masses).

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Centroid, Incentre, Gergonne in One Line Each

Equal masses $(1,1,1)$ ⇒ $AG:GD=2:1$ (centroid). Masses $(a,b,c)$ ⇒ $AI:ID=(b+c):a$ (incentre). Masses $\left(\tfrac1{s-a},\tfrac1{s-b},\tfrac1{s-c}\right)$ ⇒ Gergonne point. Concurrency of any three cevians is exactly the statement that consistent masses exist, which is Ceva.

Formula Vault — Chapter 12
  • BPT: $DE\parallel BC\Rightarrow\tfrac{AD}{DB}=\tfrac{AE}{EC}$; $\tfrac{AD}{AB}=\tfrac{AE}{AC}=\tfrac{DE}{BC}$. Midpoint: $DE=\tfrac12BC$.
  • Trapezium: $\tfrac{AO}{OC}=\tfrac{AB}{CD}$; $[AOB]:[COD]=p^2:q^2$; $[AOD]=[BOC]$; $[AOD]^2=[AOB][COD]$; $PQ=\tfrac{2pq}{p+q}$.
  • Similarity: AA / SAS / SSS (SSA fails). Linear elements $\times k$; area $\times k^2$; $\tfrac{[ADE]}{[ABC]}=\tfrac{AD\cdot AE}{AB\cdot AC}$.
  • Right triangle: $h^2=xy$, $a^2=cx$, $b^2=cy$, $h=\tfrac{ab}{c}$, $\tfrac1{h^2}=\tfrac1{a^2}+\tfrac1{b^2}$; poles/ladders: $h=\tfrac{ab}{a+b}$ ($u:v=a:b$).
  • Angle bisector: $\tfrac{BD}{DC}=\tfrac cb$; $BD=\tfrac{ac}{b+c}$; external: $\tfrac{BD'}{CD'}=\tfrac cb$; $l_a^2=bc-mn=\tfrac{4bc\,s(s-a)}{(b+c)^2}$; $\tfrac{AI}{ID}=\tfrac{b+c}{a}$.
  • Stewart: $b^2m+c^2n=a(d^2+mn)$. Apollonius: $m_a^2=\tfrac{2b^2+2c^2-a^2}{4}$; $\sum m^2=\tfrac34\sum a^2$; $AG=\tfrac23m_a$.
  • Ceva: $\tfrac{BD}{DC}\tfrac{CE}{EA}\tfrac{AF}{FB}=1$ (concurrent). Menelaus: same product $=1$ (collinear, odd # external).
  • Mass points: $\tfrac{AP}{PD}=\tfrac{w_B+w_C}{w_A}$; $[PBC]:[PCA]:[PAB]=w_A:w_B:w_C$.
  • Perpendicular medians (from $A$ and $B$): $a^2+b^2=5c^2$ (derived in Example 9).

Worked Examples — Level 1 → Level 3

LEVEL 1 · WARMUP — one theorem, direct application
Level 1BPT & Converse Example 1

In $\triangle ABC$, $D\in AB$ and $E\in AC$ with $DE\parallel BC$. Given $AD=6$, $DB=9$, $AE=8$, $BC=25$ (all in cm), find (a) $EC$, (b) $DE$. (c) Points $F\in AB$, $G\in AC$ have $AF=3$, $AG=4$. Is $FG\parallel BC$?

(a) BPT: $\tfrac{AD}{DB}=\tfrac{AE}{EC}\Rightarrow\tfrac69=\tfrac8{EC}\Rightarrow EC=12$ cm.

(b) $AB=15$. $\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC$ (common $\angle A$; $\angle ADE=\angle ABC$, corresponding). So $\tfrac{DE}{BC}=\tfrac{AD}{AB}=\tfrac6{15}=\tfrac25\Rightarrow DE=10$ cm. (Trap: $\tfrac{DE}{BC}\ne\tfrac{AD}{DB}$.)

(c) $AC=8+12=20$, so $FB=15-3=12$ and $GC=20-4=16$. $\tfrac{AF}{FB}=\tfrac3{12}=\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac{AG}{GC}=\tfrac4{16}=\tfrac14$. Equal ratios ⇒ by the converse of BPT, $FG\parallel BC$ (and $FG=\tfrac3{15}\cdot25=5$ cm).

Answers: $EC=12$, $DE=10$, yes $FG\parallel BC$.

Level 1Similarity Ratios Example 2

$\triangle ABC$ has $AB=6$, $BC=8$, $CA=10$. $\triangle PQR\sim\triangle ABC$ (in this order) with $PQ=9$. Find $QR$, $RP$, the perimeter of $\triangle PQR$, $[ABC]:[PQR]$, and the inradius of $\triangle PQR$.

Order of naming gives $AB\leftrightarrow PQ$, so $k=\tfrac{PQ}{AB}=\tfrac96=\tfrac32$ (from $ABC$ to $PQR$).

$QR=8\cdot\tfrac32=12$, $RP=10\cdot\tfrac32=15$, perimeter $=9+12+15=36$ (check: $24\cdot\tfrac32=36$).

$6^2+8^2=100=10^2$, so $\triangle ABC$ is right-angled at $B$ with area $\tfrac12\cdot6\cdot8=24$. Area scales by $k^2=\tfrac94$, so $[PQR]=54$ (check: $\tfrac12\cdot9\cdot12=54$). Hence $[ABC]:[PQR]=24:54=4:9$.

Inradius of $ABC$: $r=\tfrac{[ABC]}{s}=\tfrac{24}{12}=2$; for $PQR$: $r=2\cdot\tfrac32=3$ (check: $\tfrac{54}{18}=3$).

Answers: $QR=12$, $RP=15$, perimeter $36$, areas $4:9$, inradius $3$.

Level 1Altitude Theorem Example 3

In $\triangle ABC$, $\angle C=90^\circ$ and $CD\perp AB$ with $D\in AB$, $AD=9$, $DB=16$. Find $CD$, $AC$, $BC$ and verify $\tfrac1{CD^2}=\tfrac1{AC^2}+\tfrac1{BC^2}$.

$AB=9+16=25$. $CD^2=AD\cdot DB=144\Rightarrow CD=12$. $AC^2=AB\cdot AD=25\cdot9=225\Rightarrow AC=15$. $BC^2=AB\cdot DB=25\cdot16=400\Rightarrow BC=20$.

Check Pythagoras: $15^2+20^2=625=25^2$. Reciprocal relation: $\tfrac1{225}+\tfrac1{400}=\tfrac{16+9}{3600}=\tfrac{25}{3600}=\tfrac1{144}=\tfrac1{12^2}$ ✓.

Answers: $CD=12$, $AC=15$, $BC=20$.

LEVEL 2 · EXAM-REAL — two or three ideas combined
Level 2Trapezium + Crossing Poles Example 4

In trapezium $ABCD$ (Fig. 1.2), $AB\parallel CD$, $AB=15$, $CD=10$, diagonals meet at $O$. The line through $O$ parallel to $AB$ meets $AD$ at $P$ and $BC$ at $Q$. (a) Find $AO:OC$. (b) Find $PQ$. (c) If $[COD]=32$, find $[AOB]$, $[AOD]$ and $[ABCD]$. (d) Two vertical poles $15$ m and $10$ m tall have wires from the top of each to the foot of the other. Find the height of the crossing point and relate it to (b).

(a) $\triangle OAB\sim\triangle OCD$ (AA, alternate angles) ⇒ $\tfrac{AO}{OC}=\tfrac{AB}{CD}=\tfrac32$.

(b) $\tfrac{AO}{AC}=\tfrac35$. In $\triangle ADC$, $PO\parallel DC$ ⇒ $PO=\tfrac35\cdot10=6$. In $\triangle BCD$, $OQ\parallel DC$ and $\tfrac{BO}{BD}=\tfrac35$ ⇒ $OQ=6$. So $PQ=12$ (check: $\tfrac{2\cdot15\cdot10}{25}=12$).

(c) $[AOB]=[COD]\cdot\left(\tfrac32\right)^2=32\cdot\tfrac94=72$. $\triangle AOD$ and $\triangle AOB$ have apex $A$ and bases $OD,OB$ on $BD$: $[AOD]=72\cdot\tfrac{OD}{OB}=72\cdot\tfrac23=48$, and $[BOC]=[AOD]=48$. Total $[ABCD]=72+32+48+48=200$.

(d) $h=\tfrac{15\cdot10}{15+10}=6$ m. In the trapezium picture the poles are the parallel sides, so the vertical segment through the crossing point has length $PQ=12$ and the crossing point is its midpoint: $h=\tfrac{12}{2}=6$ ✓.

Answers: $3:2$; $12$; $72,48,200$; $6$ m.

Level 2Angle Bisectors Example 5

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB=15$, $AC=12$, $BC=18$. The internal bisector of $\angle A$ meets $BC$ at $D$ and the external bisector meets $BC$ produced at $D'$. Find $BD$, $DC$, $AD$, $CD'$ and $DD'$.

Internal. $\tfrac{BD}{DC}=\tfrac{AB}{AC}=\tfrac{15}{12}=\tfrac54$, so $BD=18\cdot\tfrac59=10$, $DC=8$. Bisector length: $AD^2=AB\cdot AC-BD\cdot DC=180-80=100\Rightarrow AD=10$. (Check by formula: $s=22.5$, $\tfrac{2\sqrt{180\cdot22.5\cdot4.5}}{27}=\tfrac{2\cdot135}{27}=10$ ✓.)

External. $AB>AC$, so $D'$ lies beyond $C$. $\tfrac{BD'}{CD'}=\tfrac54$ and $BD'=18+CD'$, so $4(18+CD')=5\,CD'\Rightarrow CD'=72$, $BD'=90$. Then $DD'=DC+CD'=8+72=80$.

Bonus check: $\angle DAD'=90^\circ$, so $AD'^2=80^2-10^2=6300$.

Answers: $BD=10$, $DC=8$, $AD=10$, $CD'=72$, $DD'=80$.

Level 2Stewart & Apollonius Example 6

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB=13$, $BC=14$, $CA=15$. $D$ lies on $BC$ with $BD:DC=5:2$. Find $AD$, and also the median $AM$ to $BC$.

$BD=10$, $DC=4$. Take $a=14$, $b=CA=15$, $c=AB=13$, $m=BD=10$, $n=DC=4$. Stewart: $b^2m+c^2n=225\cdot10+169\cdot4=2926$ and $a(d^2+mn)=14(d^2+40)$. So $d^2+40=209$, $d^2=169$, $AD=13$.

Cross-check: area by Heron $=\sqrt{21\cdot8\cdot7\cdot6}=84$, so the altitude from $A$ is $12$, foot $H$ with $BH=\sqrt{13^2-12^2}=5$. $D$ is $5$ beyond $H$, so $AD^2=12^2+5^2=169$ ✓. (Here $AD=AB$: $H$ is the midpoint of $BD$.)

Median (Apollonius): $AM^2=\tfrac{2\cdot225+2\cdot169-196}{4}=\tfrac{592}{4}=148$, so $AM=2\sqrt{37}$.

Answers: $AD=13$, $AM=2\sqrt{37}$.

LEVEL 3 · RANK-BOOSTER — full derivation, then the 30-second shortcut
Level 3Ceva · Gergonne Point Example 7

The incircle of $\triangle ABC$ touches $BC,CA,AB$ at $D,E,F$. (a) Prove that $AD,BE,CF$ are concurrent. (b) For $AB=13$, $BC=14$, $CA=15$ and common point $P$, find $AP:PD$.

Full derivation. (a) Tangents from a point to a circle are equal: $AE=AF=x$, $BD=BF=y$, $CD=CE=z$. Then $\tfrac{BD}{DC}\cdot\tfrac{CE}{EA}\cdot\tfrac{AF}{FB}=\tfrac yz\cdot\tfrac zx\cdot\tfrac xy=1$ with all three points interior, so by the converse of Ceva the cevians are concurrent. $\blacksquare$

(b) $s=21$, so $x=s-a=7$, $y=s-b=6$, $z=s-c=8$ (check: $x+y=13=c$ ✓). Thus $BD=6$, $DC=8$, $CE=8$, $EA=7$. Menelaus in $\triangle ADC$ with transversal $B$–$P$–$E$ (zig-zag $A\to P\to D\to B\to C\to E\to A$): $\tfrac{AP}{PD}\cdot\tfrac{DB}{BC}\cdot\tfrac{CE}{EA}=1\Rightarrow\tfrac{AP}{PD}\cdot\tfrac6{14}\cdot\tfrac87=1\Rightarrow\tfrac{AP}{PD}=\tfrac{14\cdot7}{6\cdot8}=\tfrac{49}{24}$.

30-Second Shortcut (mass points). Masses are the reciprocals of the tangent lengths: $A:B:C=\tfrac17:\tfrac16:\tfrac18$. Multiply by $168$: $A=24$, $B=28$, $C=21$. Then $D=B+C=49$ and $AP:PD=w_D:w_A=49:24$. (Check: $BD:DC=w_C:w_B=21:28=6:8$ ✓.)

Answer: concurrent (Gergonne point); $AP:PD=49:24$.

Level 3Menelaus ×3 · Mass Points · Areas Example 8

In Fig. 6.1, $BD:DC=1:3$, $CE:EA=2:1$, $AF:FB=3:2$ and $AD,BE,CF$ meet at $P$. Find $AP:PD$, $BP:PE$, $CP:PF$ and $[PBC]:[PCA]:[PAB]$.

Full derivation (Menelaus). Let $BD=1,DC=3$ (so $BC=4$), $CE=2,EA=1$ ($CA=3$), $AF=3,FB=2$ ($AB=5$).

  1. $\triangle ADC$, transversal $B$–$P$–$E$: $\tfrac{AP}{PD}\cdot\tfrac{DB}{BC}\cdot\tfrac{CE}{EA}=1\Rightarrow\tfrac{AP}{PD}\cdot\tfrac14\cdot2=1\Rightarrow AP:PD=2:1$.
  2. $\triangle BEC$, transversal $A$–$P$–$D$ (zig-zag $B\to P\to E\to A\to C\to D\to B$): $\tfrac{BP}{PE}\cdot\tfrac{EA}{AC}\cdot\tfrac{CD}{DB}=1\Rightarrow\tfrac{BP}{PE}\cdot\tfrac13\cdot3=1\Rightarrow BP:PE=1:1$.
  3. $\triangle AFC$, transversal $B$–$P$–$E$ ($B$ lies on $AF$ produced): $\tfrac{AB}{BF}\cdot\tfrac{FP}{PC}\cdot\tfrac{CE}{EA}=1\Rightarrow\tfrac52\cdot\tfrac{FP}{PC}\cdot2=1\Rightarrow FP:PC=1:5$, i.e. $CP:PF=5:1$.
  4. Areas (same base $BC$ etc.): $\tfrac{[PBC]}{[ABC]}=\tfrac{PD}{AD}=\tfrac13$, $\tfrac{[PCA]}{[ABC]}=\tfrac{PE}{BE}=\tfrac12$, $\tfrac{[PAB]}{[ABC]}=\tfrac{PF}{CF}=\tfrac16$. Ratio $=2:3:1$.
30-Second Shortcut (mass points). $BD:DC=1:3\Rightarrow B:C=3:1$. $CE:EA=2:1\Rightarrow w_C\cdot2=w_A\cdot1\Rightarrow A=2$ (with $C=1$). Check $AF:FB=3:2$: $w_A\cdot3=w_B\cdot2\Rightarrow B=3$ ✓ — consistent masses are Ceva. With $(A,B,C)=(2,3,1)$: $AP:PD=(3+1):2=2:1$, $BP:PE=(2+1):3=1:1$, $CP:PF=(2+3):1=5:1$, and $[PBC]:[PCA]:[PAB]=w_A:w_B:w_C=2:3:1$.

Answers: $2:1$, $1:1$, $5:1$, $2:3:1$.

Level 3Apollonius · Perpendicular Medians Example 9

In $\triangle ABC$ the medians $AD$ (to $BC$) and $BE$ (to $CA$) are perpendicular. If $BC=22$ and $CA=19$, find $AB$.

Full derivation. Let $G$ be the centroid; $AG=\tfrac23m_a$, $BG=\tfrac23m_b$ (medians divide $2:1$). Since $AD\perp BE$, $\angle AGB=90^\circ$, so $AG^2+BG^2=AB^2=c^2$, i.e. $\tfrac49(m_a^2+m_b^2)=c^2$. Apollonius: $m_a^2=\tfrac{2b^2+2c^2-a^2}{4}$ and $m_b^2=\tfrac{2a^2+2c^2-b^2}{4}$, so $m_a^2+m_b^2=\tfrac{a^2+b^2+4c^2}{4}$. Therefore $\tfrac{a^2+b^2+4c^2}{9}=c^2\Rightarrow a^2+b^2=5c^2$.

With $a=22$, $b=19$: $484+361=845=5c^2\Rightarrow c^2=169\Rightarrow AB=13$.

Verification: $m_a^2=\tfrac{2\cdot361+2\cdot169-484}{4}=144$, $AG=8$; $m_b^2=\tfrac{945}{4}$, $BG^2=\tfrac49\cdot\tfrac{945}{4}=105$; $AG^2+BG^2=64+105=169=c^2$ ✓. The sides $13,19,22$ also satisfy the triangle inequality ($13+19>22$).

30-Second Shortcut. Medians to sides $a$ and $b$ are perpendicular $\iff a^2+b^2=5c^2$, where $c$ is the third side (the one joining the two vertices the medians start from). Read: $c^2=\tfrac{22^2+19^2}{5}=\tfrac{845}{5}=169$, so $AB=13$.

Answer: $AB=13$.
