Number Theory & Modular Arithmetic

From Euclid’s Division Lemma and osculator divisibility to Euler’s Totient Theorem, Wilson’s Theorem, Chinese Remainder Theorem, non-decimal base systems, and Olympiad inequality techniques for surds and indices.

Target Study Time: 8 Hours Level: Class 10 → NTSE Stage 2 → IOQM-Lite High-Weightage BEST Paper 1 Core NAT (000–999) + MCQ Mastery

1. Divisibility Rules, Osculators & Composite Divisors

Every integer $N$ written in base $10$ with digits $d_k d_{k-1} \dots d_1 d_0$ is a polynomial evaluated at $x = 10$:

$$N = d_k \cdot 10^k + d_{k-1} \cdot 10^{k-1} + \dots + d_1 \cdot 10 + d_0 = \sum_{i=0}^{k} d_i \cdot 10^i$$

Every divisibility test in elementary and Olympiad arithmetic is simply a consequence of reducing powers of $10$ modulo the divisor $d$. Rather than memorizing isolated tricks, master the underlying modular invariant.

First-Principles Core: Standard Power-of-10 Divisibility
  • Divisibility by $2^k$ and $5^k$: Because $10^k = 2^k \cdot 5^k \equiv 0 \pmod{2^k}$ and $\pmod{5^k}$, every term $d_i 10^i$ with $i \ge k$ vanishes. Thus $N$ is divisible by $2^k$ (or $5^k$) if and only if the number formed by its last $k$ digits is divisible by $2^k$ (or $5^k$). For example, divisibility by $8 = 2^3$ depends strictly on the last $3$ digits; divisibility by $16 = 2^4$ depends on the last $4$ digits.
  • Divisibility by $3$ and $9$: Since $10 \equiv 1 \pmod 9$, we have $10^i \equiv 1^i = 1 \pmod 9$ for all $i \ge 0$. Therefore: $$N = \sum_{i=0}^{k} d_i \cdot 10^i \equiv \sum_{i=0}^{k} d_i \pmod 9$$ The remainder of $N$ modulo $9$ (and modulo $3$) equals the remainder of its digit sum modulo $9$ (and modulo $3$).
  • Divisibility by $11$: Since $10 \equiv -1 \pmod{11}$, we have $10^i \equiv (-1)^i \pmod{11}$. Therefore: $$N \equiv d_0 - d_1 + d_2 - d_3 + \dots + (-1)^k d_k \pmod{11}$$ Crucial detail: the sign starts positive at the units digit $d_0$. The alternating sum gives not just divisibility, but the exact remainder modulo $11$ (add $11$ if negative).

1.1 The Osculator (Seed-Number) Method for Primes $7, 13, 17, 19, 23, 29$

How do we test divisibility by a prime $p$ ending in $1, 3, 7,$ or $9$? Write any integer as $N = 10q + u$, where $u$ is the units digit and $q$ is the truncated number formed by removing $u$. Since $\gcd(10, p) = 1$, there exists a unique smallest-magnitude integer $k$ (positive or negative) satisfying:

$$10k \equiv 1 \pmod p$$

Multiplying $N = 10q + u$ by $k$ (which preserves divisibility by $p$ because $\gcd(k, p) = 1$) gives:

$$kN = (10k)q + ku \equiv q + ku \pmod p$$

Therefore, $p \mid (10q + u) \iff p \mid (q + ku)$. The multiplier $k$ is called the osculator of $p$. Repeating this operation reduces a large number by one digit per step.

Warning: Osculators Test Divisibility, NOT Direct Remainders

Because $q + ku \equiv kN \pmod p$ (not $N \pmod p$), the final small number preserves zero-ness ($0 \pmod p$), but its non-zero remainder is scaled by $k^m \pmod p$ after $m$ steps. Never read a non-zero osculator output directly as the remainder of $N$ divided by $p$.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Block-Grouping Rules ($1001$, $999$, $99$)

For large $6$- to $15$-digit BEST/IOQM numbers, digit-by-digit osculators are too slow. Use block-grouping based on key factorizations of $10^m \pm 1$:

  • The $1001 = 7 \times 11 \times 13$ Rule: Since $10^3 = 1000 \equiv -1 \pmod{1001}$, partition $N$ into $3$-digit blocks $B_0, B_1, B_2, \dots$ from right to left. Then: $$N \equiv B_0 - B_1 + B_2 - B_3 + \dots \pmod{1001}$$ This single alternating sum simultaneously gives the exact remainder modulo $7$, $11$, $13$, and $1001$. Immediate Olympiad corollary: any $6$-digit number of the form $\overline{abcabc} = \overline{abc} \times 1001$ is always divisible by $7, 11,$ and $13$.
  • The $999 = 27 \times 37$ Rule: Since $10^3 \equiv 1 \pmod{999}$, the plain sum of $3$-digit blocks $B_0 + B_1 + B_2 + \dots$ preserves the exact remainder modulo $27$, $37$, and $999$.
  • The $99 = 9 \times 11$ Rule: Since $10^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{99}$, the plain sum of $2$-digit blocks from the right preserves the exact remainder modulo $99$ (and hence modulo $9$ and $11$).

1.2 Composite Divisors: The Coprime Factorization Criterion

A composite divisor $D$ can be decomposed into factors $D = a \cdot b$. However, $a \mid N$ and $b \mid N$ implies $(ab) \mid N$ if and only if $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ (pairwise coprime factors). In general, $a \mid N$ and $b \mid N \iff \operatorname{lcm}(a, b) \mid N$.

  • Valid splits: $12 = 3 \times 4$, $18 = 2 \times 9$, $24 = 3 \times 8$, $36 = 4 \times 9$, $45 = 5 \times 9$, $72 = 8 \times 9$, $88 = 8 \times 11$, $99 = 9 \times 11$, $132 = 3 \times 4 \times 11$.
  • Invalid split trap: Splitting $24$ into $4 \times 6$ fails because $\gcd(4, 6) = 2 \neq 1$; for instance, $12$ is divisible by both $4$ and $6$, yet $24 \nmid 12$.

2. Power Cyclicity, Units Digit & Last Two Digits ($\bmod\, 100$)

2.1 Units Digit Cyclicity ($\bmod\, 10$)

The units digit of $N^k$ depends exclusively on the units digit $u = N \bmod 10$. Because $\mathbb{Z}_{10}$ has only $10$ residues, the sequence $u^1, u^2, u^3, \dots \pmod{10}$ must cycle periodically. Crucially, the period of every decimal digit divides $4$:

$4, 9$ $4 \to 6$;   $9 \to 1$ $2$ Odd $k \implies 4, 9$;   Even $k \implies 6, 1$
$2, 3, 7, 8$ $2,4,8,6$;   $3,9,7,1$;   $7,9,3,1$;   $8,4,2,6$ $4$ Let $r = k \bmod 4$. If $r = 0$, use $u^4$; else $u^r$

2.2 Last Two Digits ($\bmod\, 100$): Exhaustive Case-by-Case Derivation

Finding the last two digits of $a^n$ is identical to computing $a^n \pmod{100}$. Because $100 = 4 \times 25$, every base falls into one of four clean structural classes:

Master Toolkit for Last Two Digits ($a^n \bmod 100$)
  1. Bases ending in $1$: Write $a = 10t + 1$. By the Binomial Theorem: $$(10t + 1)^n = 1 + \binom{n}{1}(10t) + \binom{n}{2}(10t)^2 + \dots \equiv 1 + 10tn \pmod{100}$$ Every term from $k = 2$ onward has $10^2 = 100$ as a factor. Therefore:
    • Units digit $= 1$.
    • Tens digit $= (t \times n) \bmod 10$ (multiply the base’s tens digit by the exponent’s units digit).
    Example: $41^{73}$ has tens digit $(4 \times 3) \bmod 10 = 2$, so $41^{73} \equiv 21 \pmod{100}$.
  2. Bases ending in $3, 7,$ or $9$: Convert the base into a number ending in $1$ using $3^4 = 81$, $7^4 = 2401 \equiv 01$, or $9^2 = 81$, then apply Case 1:
    • $7^{4k} = (7^4)^k \equiv (01)^k = 01 \pmod{100}$.
    • $3^{4k} = (81)^k \implies$ tens digit is $(8k) \bmod 10$.
    • For general $a$ ending in $3, 7, 9$, note that $a^4$ always ends in $1$, and even better, $a^{20} \equiv 01 \pmod{100}$ for every integer coprime to $10$ (proved in Section 4 via CRT: $\phi(4) = 2$, $\phi(25) = 20$, $\operatorname{lcm}(2, 20) = 20$).
  3. Bases ending in $5$: Write $a = 10t + 5$. For $n = 2$: $$(10t + 5)^2 = 100t(t+1) + 25 \equiv 25 \pmod{100}$$ Since $25^m \equiv 25 \pmod{100}$, every even power ($n \ge 2$) ends in $25$. For odd $n = 2m + 1 \ge 3$: $$(10t + 5)^{2m+1} \equiv 25(10t + 5) = 250t + 125 \equiv 50t + 25 \pmod{100}$$ Hence, for $n \ge 2$: $(10t+5)^n$ ends in $75$ if and only if both $t$ and $n$ are odd; in all other cases it ends in $25$.
  4. Even Bases (Powers of $2$ & Factor Splitting): Compute $2^{10} = 1024 \equiv 24 \pmod{100}$. Notice $24^2 = 576 \equiv 76 \pmod{100}$, $76^2 = 5776 \equiv 76 \pmod{100}$, and $76 \times 24 = 1824 \equiv 24 \pmod{100}$. Thus: $$2^{10 \times \text{odd}} \equiv 24 \pmod{100}, \qquad 2^{10 \times \text{even}} \equiv 76 \pmod{100}$$ Furthermore, because $76 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$ and $76 \equiv 1 \pmod{25}$, multiplying any multiple of $4$ by $76$ leaves it invariant modulo $100$: $$76 \times 2^m \equiv 2^m \pmod{100} \quad (\text{for all } m \ge 2)$$ For any general even base $(2b)^n = 2^n \cdot b^n$, compute $2^n \pmod{100}$ and $b^n \pmod{100}$ separately and multiply their last two digits modulo $100$.

3. Modular Arithmetic, Negative Remainders & The Binomial Trick

By Euclid’s Division Lemma, for any integer $a$ and positive integer $m$, there exist unique integers $q$ (quotient) and $r$ (canonical remainder) such that:

$$a = mq + r, \qquad 0 \le r \lt m$$

We write $a \equiv b \pmod m$ (read “$a$ is congruent to $b$ modulo $m$”) if $m \mid (a - b)$, equivalently if $a$ and $b$ leave the exact same remainder when divided by $m$.

Laws of Congruence & The Cancellation Trap

If $a \equiv b \pmod m$ and $c \equiv d \pmod m$, then:

  • Addition / Subtraction: $a \pm c \equiv b \pm d \pmod m$.
  • Multiplication: $ac \equiv bd \pmod m$.
  • Positive Integer Powers: $a^k \equiv b^k \pmod m$ for all integers $k \ge 1$.
  • Division / Cancellation Law (CRITICAL TRAP): You cannot blindly divide both sides of a congruence by $c$. The exact law is: $$ca \equiv cb \pmod m \iff a \equiv b \pmod{\frac{m}{\gcd(c, m)}}$$ Only when $\gcd(c, m) = 1$ does the modulus stay $m$. Example: $2 \cdot 5 \equiv 2 \cdot 2 \pmod 6$ reduces to $5 \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, NOT $5 \equiv 2 \pmod 6$.

3.1 Negative Remainders & Common-Factor Scaling

When a positive remainder $r$ is close to $m$, replacing $r$ with $r - m$ yields a tiny negative residue whose high powers are effortless to evaluate. At the end of a calculation, convert any negative residue back to canonical form $[0, m-1]$ by adding a suitable multiple of $m$.

Common-Factor Cancellation Identity: If both the dividend $A$ and divisor $B$ share a positive common factor $k$, write $A = kA'$ and $B = kB'$. From Euclid’s lemma $A' = B'q + r'$ ($0 \le r' \lt B'$), multiplying through by $k$ gives $A = Bq + kr'$ with $0 \le kr' \lt B$. Therefore:

$$\operatorname{Rem}\!\left(\frac{k \cdot A'}{k \cdot B'}\right) = k \cdot \operatorname{Rem}\!\left(\frac{A'}{B'}\right)$$

Always remember to multiply back by the cancelled factor $k$ at the final step! Forgetting to restore $k$ is the single most frequent NAT error in BEST Paper 1.

3.2 The Binomial Remainder Trick & Polynomial Divisibility

To find $(km \pm a)^n \pmod m$, expand via the Binomial Theorem:

$$(km \pm a)^n = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \binom{n}{j} (km)^{n-j} (\pm a)^j$$

Every term except $j = n$ carries at least one factor of $m$, leaving:

$$(km \pm a)^n \equiv (\pm a)^n \pmod m$$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Higher-Order Binomial Truncation ($\bmod\, m^2$)

When the modulus is a prime square $p^2$ (like $49$, $121$, $169$) or $m^2$, expand $(kp \pm 1)^n$ and retain the last two terms ($j = 0$ and $j = 1$), since every term with $j \ge 2$ contains $p^2$:

$$(kp + 1)^n \equiv 1 + nkp \pmod{p^2}, \qquad (kp - 1)^n \equiv (-1)^n + (-1)^{n-1} nkp \pmod{p^2}$$

Algebraic Divisibility Identities (Factor Theorem in Disguise):

  • $a^n - b^n$ is divisible by $(a - b)$ for all positive integers $n$.
  • $a^n - b^n$ is divisible by $(a + b)$ for all even positive integers $n$.
  • $a^n + b^n$ is divisible by $(a + b)$ for all odd positive integers $n$.

4. Fermat’s Little Theorem, Wilson’s Theorem & Euler’s Totient Theorem

When an exponent reaches thousands or appears inside a power tower, repeated squaring is too slow. Three classical theorems collapse enormous exponents in a single step.

Theorem 4.1: Fermat’s Little Theorem (FLT)

Let $p$ be a prime number and $a$ be any integer. Then:

$$a^p \equiv a \pmod p$$

If, in addition, $\gcd(a, p) = 1$ (i.e., $p \nmid a$), we may cancel $a$ to obtain the exam workhorse form:

$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$$

First-Principles Proof Sketch: Consider the $p - 1$ non-zero multiples $\{a, 2a, 3a, \dots, (p-1)a\}$. Because $\gcd(a, p) = 1$, no two are congruent modulo $p$ and none is $0 \pmod p$. Hence they are merely a permutation of $\{1, 2, \dots, p-1\} \pmod p$. Multiplying all elements of both sets gives:

$$a^{p-1} (p-1)! \equiv (p-1)! \pmod p$$

Since $p$ is prime, $\gcd((p-1)!, p) = 1$, so cancelling $(p-1)!$ yields $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$.

4.1 Euler’s Totient Function $\phi(n)$ & Euler’s Theorem

What if the modulus $n$ is composite? Euler generalized Fermat’s Little Theorem by replacing the set $\{1, 2, \dots, p-1\}$ with the set of integers in $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$ that are coprime to $n$. The count of such integers is Euler’s Totient Function $\phi(n)$.

If the prime factorization of $n$ is $n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$, then by Inclusion-Exclusion:

$$\phi(n) = n \left(1 - \frac{1}{p_1}\right)\left(1 - \frac{1}{p_2}\right)\cdots\left(1 - \frac{1}{p_k}\right)$$

Key properties: $\phi(p) = p - 1$ for prime $p$; $\phi(p^e) = p^e - p^{e-1} = p^{e-1}(p-1)$; and $\phi(mn) = \phi(m)\phi(n)$ whenever $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.

Theorem 4.2: Euler’s Totient Theorem

For any positive integer $n$ and any integer $a$ satisfying $\gcd(a, n) = 1$:

$$a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$$

Exam Rule: Whenever $\gcd(a, n) = 1$, you may reduce the exponent $E$ modulo $\phi(n)$: $a^E \equiv a^{E \bmod \phi(n)} \pmod n$. If $\gcd(a, n) \neq 1$, Euler’s Theorem DOES NOT APPLY—split the modulus using CRT or use common-factor cancellation instead!

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Wilson’s Theorem & Factorial Corollaries

Theorem 4.3 (Wilson’s Theorem): An integer $p \ge 2$ is prime if and only if:

$$(p - 1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$$

Why it holds: Modulo a prime $p$, every residue $x \in \{1, 2, \dots, p-1\}$ has a unique multiplicative inverse $x^{-1}$. The only residues that are their own inverses satisfy $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod p \implies p \mid (x-1)(x+1) \implies x \equiv 1$ or $x \equiv p-1$. The remaining $p - 3$ residues pair up into inverse couples whose product is $1$, leaving $(p-1)! \equiv 1 \cdot (p-1) \equiv -1 \pmod p$.

Three High-Frequency BEST/IOQM Corollaries (for odd prime $p$):

  1. $(p - 2)! \equiv 1 \pmod p$: From $(p-1)(p-2)! \equiv -1 \implies (-1)(p-2)! \equiv -1 \pmod p$.
  2. $(p - 3)! \equiv \dfrac{p - 1}{2} \pmod p$: From $(p-1)(p-2)(p-3)! \equiv -1 \implies (-1)(-2)(p-3)! \equiv p - 1 \implies 2(p-3)! \equiv p - 1 \pmod p$. Since $p$ is odd, dividing by $2$ gives $\dfrac{p-1}{2}$.
  3. Composite Factorial Collapse: For every composite integer $n \gt 4$, $(n - 1)! \equiv 0 \pmod n$ (if $n = ab$ with $2 \le a \lt b \lt n$, both $a$ and $b$ appear as distinct factors in $(n-1)!$; if $n = p^2$ with $p \ge 3$, both $p$ and $2p$ appear in $(n-1)!$). The sole composite exception is $n = 4$, where $3! = 6 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$.

5. Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT) & Splitting Composite Moduli

Suppose a problem asks for the remainder of a giant expression divided by a composite modulus like $72$, $91$, $100$, or $899$. Evaluating directly modulo $899$ is painful, because $899$ is neither prime nor small. The Chinese Remainder Theorem lets us shatter $m$ into pairwise coprime pieces, solve each tiny piece independently, and stitch the answers back together uniquely.

Theorem 5.1: Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT)

Let $m_1, m_2, \dots, m_k$ be positive integers that are pairwise coprime ($\gcd(m_i, m_j) = 1$ for $i \neq j$), and let $M = m_1 m_2 \cdots m_k$. Then the simultaneous system of congruences:

$$x \equiv r_1 \pmod{m_1}, \quad x \equiv r_2 \pmod{m_2}, \quad \dots, \quad x \equiv r_k \pmod{m_k}$$

has a unique solution modulo $M$.

Constructive Formula: Define $M_i = M / m_i$ and let $y_i$ be the modular inverse of $M_i$ modulo $m_i$ (so $M_i y_i \equiv 1 \pmod{m_i}$). Then:

$$x \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{k} r_i M_i y_i \pmod M$$
Figure 1.2: Every residue $x \in \{0, 1, \dots, 11\}$ occupies a unique coordinate $(x \bmod 3,\, x \bmod 4)$ because $\gcd(3, 4) = 1$.

5.1 Exam-Speed CRT: The Successive Substitution Method

In a timed BEST exam, solving $2$ or $3$ congruences via successive substitution is faster and less error-prone than memorizing the formula:

  1. Start with the congruence having the largest modulus: write $x = m_1 k + r_1$.
  2. Substitute into the second congruence: $m_1 k + r_1 \equiv r_2 \pmod{m_2}$.
  3. Solve for the smallest non-negative integer $k = k_0 \pmod{m_2}$, giving $k = m_2 t + k_0$.
  4. Substitute back to obtain $x = m_1 m_2 t + (m_1 k_0 + r_1)$, which combines both congruences into a single congruence modulo $m_1 m_2$. Repeat if a third modulus exists.

6. HCF & LCM with Same & Different Remainders

For any two positive integers $a$ and $b$, let $g = \gcd(a, b) = \operatorname{HCF}(a, b)$. Then we can write:

$$a = gx, \qquad b = gy, \qquad \text{where } \gcd(x, y) = 1$$

From this coprime decomposition, $\operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = gxy$, which immediately proves the classical identity:

$$\operatorname{HCF}(a, b) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a, b) = g \cdot (gxy) = (gx)(gy) = a \times b$$
Critical Trap: Two Numbers Only!

The product rule $\operatorname{HCF} \times \operatorname{LCM} = \text{Product}$ holds strictly for two positive integers. For three numbers $a, b, c$: $$\operatorname{HCF}(a,b,c) \times \operatorname{LCM}(a,b,c) \neq abc \quad \text{(in general)}$$ For example, with $2, 4, 6$: $\operatorname{HCF} = 2$, $\operatorname{LCM} = 12$, so $2 \times 12 = 24 \neq 2 \times 4 \times 6 = 48$. The true three-variable identity is $\operatorname{LCM}(a,b,c) = \dfrac{abc \cdot \gcd(a,b,c)}{\gcd(a,b)\gcd(b,c)\gcd(c,a)}$.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Euclidean Algorithm & Bézout’s Identity

For any integers $a, b$ with $b \gt 0$, $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$. Even more generally, $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(a - kb, b)$ for any integer $k$. In Olympiad algebra, this lets you find the HCF of polynomial expressions immediately! For instance, to find $\gcd(2n + 1, 3n + 2)$:

$$\gcd(2n + 1,\, 3n + 2) = \gcd(2n + 1,\, (3n + 2) - (2n + 1)) = \gcd(2n + 1,\, n + 1) = \gcd((2n+1) - 2(n+1),\, n+1) = \gcd(-1,\, n+1) = 1$$

Bézout’s Identity: For any integers $a, b$ not both zero, there exist integers $x, y$ such that $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$. Consequently, the linear Diophantine equation $ax + by = c$ has integer solutions if and only if $\gcd(a, b) \mid c$.

7. Positional Base Systems (Conversion, Arithmetic & Divisibility)

In a positional number system with integer base (radix) $b \ge 2$, the permissible digit set is $\{0, 1, 2, \dots, b - 1\}$ (for $b \gt 10$, letters $A = 10, B = 11, \dots, F = 15$ represent digits). A number with integer part $d_k \dots d_1 d_0$ and fractional part $f_1 f_2 \dots$ has base-$10$ value:

$$(d_k \dots d_1 d_0 . f_1 f_2 \dots)_b = \sum_{i=0}^{k} d_i b^i + \sum_{j \ge 1} f_j b^{-j}$$
Core Conversion & Arithmetic Algorithms
  • Base-$10 \to$ Base-$b$ (Integer Part): Repeatedly divide by $b$ and record the remainders. Read the remainders from bottom (last) to top (first).
  • Base-$10 \to$ Base-$b$ (Fractional Part): Repeatedly multiply the fractional part by $b$ and record the integer part produced at each step. Read from top (first) to bottom (last).
  • Power-Base Grouping ($b \leftrightarrow b^k$): To convert directly between binary ($2$) and octal ($8 = 2^3$) or hexadecimal ($16 = 2^4$), group binary digits in blocks of $3$ or $4$ starting from the radix point—never pass through base $10$.
  • Base-$b$ Addition & Subtraction:
    • Addition: Whenever a column sum $S \ge b$, write down $S \bmod b$ and carry $\lfloor S / b \rfloor$ to the next column left.
    • Subtraction: Whenever a top digit is smaller than the bottom digit, borrowing $1$ from the left column adds $b$ (not $10$!) to the current column.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Universal Divisibility Laws in Base $b$

Our base-$10$ divisibility rules for $9$ and $11$ are just special cases of universal base-$b$ theorems:

  • Divisibility by $(b - 1)$: Since $b \equiv 1 \pmod{b - 1}$, we have $b^i \equiv 1 \pmod{b - 1}$. Thus $(d_k \dots d_1 d_0)_b \equiv \sum_{i=0}^k d_i \pmod{b - 1}$. A base-$b$ number is divisible by $(b - 1)$ (and any factor of $b - 1$) if and only if the sum of its base-$b$ digits is divisible by $(b - 1)$.
  • Divisibility by $(b + 1)$: Since $b \equiv -1 \pmod{b + 1}$, $(d_k \dots d_1 d_0)_b \equiv d_0 - d_1 + d_2 - \dots \pmod{b + 1}$. Divisibility by $(b + 1)$ is governed by the alternating sum of its base-$b$ digits.
  • Even/Odd Parity Trap in Odd Bases: In an odd base (e.g., base $7$ or base $9$), $b - 1$ is even, so $2 \mid (b - 1)$. Therefore, a number in an odd base is even if and only if the sum of its digits is even—NOT merely because its last digit is even! For example, $(11)_7 = 7 + 1 = 8$ is even despite ending in $1$.

8. Surds & Indices: Laws, Denesting & Comparison Techniques

Before comparing expressions, eliminate the #1 notation trap in indices: power towers are right-associative.

$$a^{b^c} = a^{(b^c)} \neq (a^b)^c = a^{bc}$$

For example, $2^{3^2} = 2^9 = 512$, whereas $(2^3)^2 = 8^2 = 64$.

Five-Weapon Arsenal for Comparing Surds & Large Indices
  1. HCF of Exponents (Large Powers): To compare $a^m$ and $b^n$, let $g = \gcd(m, n)$ and rewrite as $(a^{m/g})^g$ vs $(b^{n/g})^g$. Compare the inner bases directly. Example: $2^{60} = (2^5)^{12} = 32^{12}$ vs $3^{48} = (3^4)^{12} = 81^{12} \implies 2^{60} \lt 3^{48}$.
  2. LCM of Radical Orders (Pure Surds): To compare $\sqrt[m]{a}$ and $\sqrt[n]{b}$ ($a, b \gt 0$), let $L = \operatorname{lcm}(m, n)$ and raise both to power $L$: compare $a^{L/m}$ vs $b^{L/n}$.
  3. Constant-Sum Surd Rule ($\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$ with $a + b = S$): Squaring gives $(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})^2 = S + 2\sqrt{ab}$. Since $S$ is fixed, the pair with the larger product $ab$ (i.e., closer $a$ and $b$) gives the larger sum.
  4. Constant-Difference Rationalization ($\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}$ with $a - b = d \gt 0$): Multiply by the conjugate: $$\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b} = \frac{a - b}{\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}} = \frac{d}{\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}}$$ With a fixed positive numerator $d$, larger $a, b$ produce a larger denominator and therefore a SMALLER difference.
  5. Denesting Compound Radicals: Whenever $x, y \gt 0$ satisfy $x + y = A$ and $xy = B$: $$\sqrt{A \pm 2\sqrt{B}} = \left|\sqrt{x} \pm \sqrt{y}\right|$$ If the coefficient of $\sqrt{B}$ is not $2$, force a $2$ by multiplying and dividing inside the outer radical by $2$ (e.g., $\sqrt{3 + \sqrt{5}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{6 + 2\sqrt{5}}{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{5} + 1}{\sqrt{2}}$).
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Comparing $a^b$ vs $b^a$ via the $f(x) = x^{1/x}$ Curve

How do you compare $99^{100}$ vs $100^{99}$, or $3^\pi$ vs $\pi^3$, without a calculator? Take the $(ab)$-th root of both sides ($a, b \gt 0$):

$$a^b \gt b^a \iff a^{1/a} \gt b^{1/b}$$

Consider the function $f(x) = x^{1/x}$ for $x \gt 0$. Taking the logarithm $\ln f(x) = \dfrac{\ln x}{x}$, its derivative is $\dfrac{1 - \ln x}{x^2}$, which is positive for $0 \lt x \lt e$ and negative for $x \gt e$ (where $e \approx 2.71828$). Hence $f(x)$ reaches its unique global maximum at $x = e$, strictly increases on $(0, e)$, and strictly decreases on $(e, \infty)$.

Golden Exam Rule: For any two real numbers $e \le a \lt b$ (in particular, any integers $3 \le a \lt b$), we have $a^{1/a} \gt b^{1/b}$, so $a^b \gt b^a$ (the smaller base with the larger exponent always wins!). Watch the sole small-integer exceptions on the left of $e$: $2^3 = 8 \lt 3^2 = 9$, and $2^4 = 4^2 = 16$ (exact tie because $2^{1/2} = 4^{1/4}$).

A $7$-digit number $48A56B2$ is completely divisible by $72$, where $A$ and $B$ are single decimal digits ($0 \le A, B \le 9$). Find the maximum possible value of $10A + B$.

Step 1 (Coprime Factorization): Factor $72 = 8 \times 9$, noting that $\gcd(8, 9) = 1$. Therefore, $72 \mid 48A56B2$ if and only if both $8$ and $9$ divide $48A56B2$.

Step 2 (Apply Divisibility by $8$): The last three digits $6B2$ must be divisible by $8$. Write $6B2 = 600 + 10B + 2$. Since $8 \mid 600$ ($600 = 8 \times 75$), we require:

$$10B + 2 \equiv 0 \pmod 8 \implies 2B + 2 \equiv 0 \pmod 8 \implies B + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$$

For $B \in \{0, 1, \dots, 9\}$, the valid solutions are $B = 3$ ($632 = 8 \times 79$) and $B = 7$ ($672 = 8 \times 84$).

Step 3 (Apply Divisibility by $9$): The digit sum must be a multiple of $9$:

$$4 + 8 + A + 5 + 6 + B + 2 = 25 + A + B \equiv 0 \pmod 9$$

Since $25 \equiv 7 \pmod 9$, we need $A + B \equiv 2 \pmod 9$. Because $0 \le A + B \le 18$, the only possible sums are $A + B = 2$ or $A + B = 11$.

Step 4 (Evaluate Both Cases & Maximize $10A + B$):

  • Case 1 ($B = 3$): $A + 3 \in \{2, 11\}$. Since $A \ge 0$, $A = 8$ is the unique digit. This yields $10A + B = 10(8) + 3 = 83$. (Check: $4885632 = 72 \times 67856$.)
  • Case 2 ($B = 7$): $A + 7 \in \{2, 11\} \implies A = 4$. This yields $10A + B = 10(4) + 7 = 47$. (Check: $4845672 = 72 \times 67301$.)

Comparing both valid pairs, the maximum value of $10A + B$ is $\max(83, 47) = \mathbf{83}$.

What is the units digit of the positive integer $N = 17^{2026} + 23^{2027} - 14^{2028} + 19^{2029}$?

(A) $1$    (B) $3$    (C) $7$    (D) $9$

  • $19^{2029} \equiv 9^{2029} \pmod{10}$. Odd powers of $9$ always end in $9$, so $9^{2029} \equiv 9 \pmod{10}$.

Level 1 · Warmup NAT (000–999) Worked Example 3: Dual LCM Constant-Difference & HCF Equal-Remainder Problem

Let $X$ be the smallest positive integer that leaves remainders $7, 11,$ and $15$ when divided by $12, 16,$ and $20$ respectively. Let $Y$ be the largest positive integer that leaves the same remainder when dividing $152, 242,$ and $377$. Find the value of $X + Y$.

$$12 - 7 = 5, \qquad 16 - 11 = 5, \qquad 20 - 15 = 5$$

Since the difference is constant ($d = 5$), $X + 5$ is a common multiple of $12, 16,$ and $20$. Compute the LCM via prime factorization: $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$, $16 = 2^4$, $20 = 2^2 \cdot 5$:

$$\operatorname{LCM}(12, 16, 20) = 2^4 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 240$$

The smallest positive integer is $X = 240 - 5 = 235$. (Verification: $235 = 12(19)+7 = 16(14)+11 = 20(11)+15$.)

Step 2 (Find $Y$ via HCF Unknown-Same-Remainder Model): Since $152, 242,$ and $377$ leave the same remainder $r$ upon division by $Y$, subtracting any two eliminates $r$, so $Y$ divides every pairwise difference:

$$242 - 152 = 90, \qquad 377 - 242 = 135, \qquad 377 - 152 = 225$$

Thus $Y = \operatorname{HCF}(90, 135, 225)$. Since $90 = 2 \cdot 45$, $135 = 3 \cdot 45$, and $225 = 5 \cdot 45$ with $\gcd(2, 3, 5) = 1$, we get $Y = 45$. (Verification: $152 = 45(3)+17$, $242 = 45(5)+17$, $377 = 45(8)+17$.)

Step 3 (Final Answer):

$$X + Y = 235 + 45 = \mathbf{280}$$
