26.1 Versatile Nature of Carbon & Its Allotropes

Carbon ($Z = 6$, electronic configuration $2, 4$) forms over ten million known organic compounds — more than all other elements combined. This chemical monopoly rests on three atomic pillars:

  1. Tetravalency & Exclusively Covalent Bonding: Carbon has $4$ valence electrons. It cannot lose $4e^-$ to form $\text{C}^{4+}$ (the sum of its first four ionization enthalpies is prohibitively huge) and cannot gain $4e^-$ to form $\text{C}^{4-}$ (a tiny nucleus with $6$ protons cannot hold $10$ electrons against intense electron–electron repulsion). Instead, carbon achieves a noble-gas octet by sharing $4$ electron pairs via four strong covalent bonds.
  2. Catenation (Self-Linking Supremacy): Catenation is the ability of identical atoms to form long straight chains, branched chains, and closed rings. Because carbon has a remarkably small atomic radius, its nuclei grip shared electrons tightly, giving a massive $\text{C}-\text{C}$ single bond dissociation energy of $348\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$. Silicon (directly below carbon in Group 14) has a larger atomic radius and a much weaker $\text{Si}-\text{Si}$ bond ($\approx 226\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$), limiting silanes to reactive short chains of only $7\text{--}8$ atoms.
  3. Multiple Bonding ($\sigma$ vs. $\pi$ Bonds): Carbon's compact $2p$ orbitals overlap sideways efficiently with other $\text{C}, \text{O},$ and $\text{N}$ atoms to form stable double and triple bonds:
    • Single bond ($\text{C}-\text{C}$): $1\sigma$ bond (head-on overlap; free rotation; $sp^3$ carbon).
    • Double bond ($\text{C}=\text{C}$): $1\sigma + 1\pi$ bond (restricted rotation; $sp^2$ carbon).
    • Triple bond ($\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$): $1\sigma + 2\pi$ bonds (linear geometry; $sp$ carbon).

Every carbon atom in $\text{C}_{60}$ is bonded to $3$ neighbors ($sp^2$), so the number of cage edges is $E = \frac{60 \times 3}{2} = 90$. 26.2 Hydrocarbon Classification & Homologous Series

Hydrocarbons (compounds containing exclusively $\text{C}$ and $\text{H}$) split into open-chain (acyclic/aliphatic) and closed-ring (cyclic) families:

  • Saturated Hydrocarbons (Alkanes): Only $\text{C}-\text{C}$ single bonds; general formula $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$ ($n \ge 1$). Chemically unreactive ("paraffins" = little affinity).
  • Unsaturated Hydrocarbons:
    • Alkenes: At least one $\text{C}=\text{C}$ double bond; general formula $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$ ($n \ge 2$).
    • Alkynes: At least one $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$ triple bond; general formula $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n-2}$ ($n \ge 2$).
  • Alicyclic vs. Aromatic Cyclic Hydrocarbons:
    • Cycloalkanes (e.g., cyclopropane $\text{C}_3\text{H}_6$, cyclohexane $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}$) have general formula $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$ ($n \ge 3$) — identical to alkenes!
    • Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$): The parent aromatic ring consisting of $6$ planar $sp^2$ carbons with $3$ delocalized $\pi$-bonds ($12\sigma + 3\pi$ total bonds). Unlike alkenes, benzene resists simple $\text{Br}_2$-water addition to preserve its extraordinary resonance stability.
Five Laws of a Homologous Series
  1. All members share the same general molecular formula and identical functional group.
  2. Any two consecutive homologues differ by exactly $-\text{CH}_2-$ in formula and $14\text{ u}$ in molecular mass ($12 + 2 \times 1 = 14$).
  3. Chemical properties remain nearly identical because the functional group governs reactivity.
  4. Physical properties (BP, MP, density, viscosity) rise smoothly with increasing molar mass due to stronger London dispersion (van der Waals) forces, while water solubility of alcohols/acids decreases as the hydrophobic tail lengthens.
  5. Branching Trap (Olympiad Favorite): Among isomeric alkanes of identical molar mass, increasing branching lowers the boiling point because a more spherical shape reduces surface-area contact between molecules: $$\text{n-Pentane } (36^\circ\text{C}) \;\gt\; \text{Isopentane } (28^\circ\text{C}) \;\gt\; \text{Neopentane } (9.5^\circ\text{C})$$

26.3 Rigorous IUPAC Nomenclature & Priority Tie-Breakers

Every systematic IUPAC name is assembled in one strict five-slot template:

$$\underbrace{\text{Secondary Prefix}}_{\text{Substituents } (-\text{Cl}, -\text{CH}_3, \text{oxo-})} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{Primary Prefix}}_{\text{cyclo- (if ring)}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{Word Root}}_{\text{C}_1\text{--C}_{10}\text{ chain}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{Primary Suffix}}_{-ane / -ene / -yne} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{Secondary Suffix}}_{\text{Principal FG } (-oic\text{ acid}, -al, -ol)}$$

Word Roots ($\text{C}_1\text{--C}_{10}$): $\text{C}_1$ = Meth, $\text{C}_2$ = Eth, $\text{C}_3$ = Prop, $\text{C}_4$ = But, $\text{C}_5$ = Pent, $\text{C}_6$ = Hex, $\text{C}_7$ = Hept, $\text{C}_8$ = Oct, $\text{C}_9$ = Non, $\text{C}_{10}$ = Dec. (Drop the terminal '-e' of -ane/-ene/-yne whenever the secondary suffix begins with a vowel $a, e, i, o, u, y$: e.g., propane + ol $\to$ propanol, NOT propaneol.)

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — The 6-Level Tie-Breaker Hierarchy

When two candidate parent chains or numbering directions compete, apply these six rules strictly from top to bottom — stop at the first rule that breaks the tie:

  1. Principal Functional Group: Parent chain MUST contain the highest-priority functional group, and numbering gives it the lowest possible locant (note: chain-terminating $-\text{COOH}$ and $-\text{CHO}$ automatically get carbon #1!).
  2. Maximum Multiple Bonds: Include the maximum number of $\text{C}=\text{C}$ and $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$ bonds, even if this forces a shorter carbon chain!
  3. Longest Carbon Chain: Among chains equal under Rules 1–2, pick the longest continuous chain.
  4. Maximum Substituents: If two chains have equal length, pick the one bearing more side chains/substituents.
  5. Lowest Set of Locants (First Point of Difference Rule): Compare locant lists position-by-position, NOT by summing them! Example: $(2, 7, 8)$ beats $(3, 4, 9)$ even though $2+7+8=17 \gt 3+4+9=16$, because at the first position $2 \lt 3$. Also: if $\text{C}=\text{C}$ and $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$ are strictly equidistant from both ends, $\text{C}=\text{C}$ wins the lower locant!
  6. Alphabetical Order: If locant sets are identical from both ends (e.g., $2$-bromo vs. $2$-chloro), the alphabetically earlier substituent gets the lower number. Always ignore multiplicative prefixes (di-, tri-, tetra-) and hyphenated prefixes (sec-, tert-) when alphabetizing, while keeping iso- and neo- (so ethyl precedes dimethyl because 'e' precedes 'm').

26.4 Olympiad Bridge: Degree of Unsaturation & Structural Isomerism

Compounds having the same molecular formula but different spatial or structural arrangements of atoms are called isomers. Before drawing a single structure in an exam, compute the Degree of Unsaturation (DU) — also called the Index of Hydrogen Deficiency (IHD) — which tells you the exact total number of $\pi$-bonds plus rings present in the molecule:

Degree of Unsaturation (DU / IHD) Master Formula $$\text{DU} = C + 1 - \frac{H + X - N}{2}$$

where $C =$ carbons, $H =$ hydrogens, $X =$ monovalent halogens ($\text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$), and $N =$ trivalent nitrogens. Divalent atoms ($\text{O}, \text{S}$) are completely ignored!

  • $\text{DU} = 0$: Strictly saturated acyclic (alkanes, saturated alcohols, ethers, haloalkanes).
  • $\text{DU} = 1$: Either $1$ double bond ($\text{C}=\text{C}$ or $\text{C}=\text{O}$) OR $1$ ring (cycloalkane).
  • $\text{DU} = 2$: $1$ triple bond, OR $2$ double bonds, OR $1$ ring + $1$ double bond, OR $2$ rings.
  • $\text{DU} \ge 4$: Immediately suspect a benzene ring ($1\text{ ring} + 3\ \pi\text{-bonds} = 4\text{ DU}$)!

Five Types of Structural (Constitutional) Isomerism

  1. Chain Isomerism: Same functional group, different carbon skeleton branching (e.g., $n$-butane vs. 2-methylpropane).
  2. Position Isomerism: Same carbon skeleton & functional group, different locant of the functional group/multiple bond/substituent (e.g., propan-1-ol vs. propan-2-ol; but-1-ene vs. but-2-ene).
  3. Functional Group Isomerism (High-Yield Pairs): Same molecular formula, different functional groups:
    • Alcohol $\longleftrightarrow$ Ether ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}\text{O}$, $\text{DU} = 0$): e.g., Ethanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$) vs. Methoxymethane ($\text{CH}_3\text{OCH}_3$).
    • Aldehyde $\longleftrightarrow$ Ketone ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}\text{O}$, $\text{DU} = 1$): e.g., Propanal ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CHO}$) vs. Propanone ($\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3$).
    • Carboxylic Acid $\longleftrightarrow$ Ester ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}\text{O}_2$, $\text{DU} = 1$): e.g., Ethanoic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) vs. Methyl methanoate ($\text{HCOOCH}_3$).
    • Alkyne $\longleftrightarrow$ Alkadiene ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n-2}$, $\text{DU} = 2$): e.g., But-1-yne vs. Buta-1,3-diene.
  4. Metamerism: Different alkyl chain sizes flanking a polyvalent functional group ($-\text{O}-$, $-\text{CO}-$, $-\text{COO}-$): e.g., Ethoxyethane ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OC}_2\text{H}_5$) vs. 1-Methoxypropane ($\text{CH}_3\text{OC}_3\text{H}_7$).
  5. Ring–Chain Isomerism: Open-chain unsaturated compound vs. closed-ring isomer (e.g., Propene $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}=\text{CH}_2$ vs. Cyclopropane; But-1-yne vs. Cyclobutene).

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Cis–Trans (Geometrical) Isomerism in Alkenes

Because the $\pi$-bond of $\text{C}=\text{C}$ locks rotation, an alkene of general form $ab\text{C}=\text{C}cd$ exhibits Geometrical Isomerism if and only if both double-bonded carbons carry two different groups: $a \neq b$ AND $c \neq d$.

  • But-2-ene ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}=\text{CHCH}_3$): Both ends have $(\text{H} \neq \text{CH}_3) \implies$ exists as cis-but-2-ene (both $-\text{CH}_3$ on same side; polar, higher BP) and trans-but-2-ene ($-\text{CH}_3$ on opposite sides; zero dipole moment, higher MP due to tighter crystal packing).
  • But-1-ene ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CHCH}_2\text{CH}_3$) & 2-Methylpropene ($\text{CH}_2=\text{C}(\text{CH}_3)_2$): Terminal $=\text{CH}_2$ has two identical $\text{H}$ atoms ($a = b$) $\implies$ NO geometrical isomerism.

26.5 Chemical Properties, Ethanol & Ethanoic Acid Web

A. Four Core Hydrocarbon & Organic Reaction Types

  1. Combustion: All carbon compounds burn in excess $\text{O}_2$ releasing $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, heat, and light: $$\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2} + \left(\frac{3n+1}{2}\right)\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow n\text{CO}_2 + (n+1)\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{Heat \& Light}$$
    • Saturated hydrocarbons burn with a clean, non-sooty blue flame in sufficient air. (If air vents of a gas stove clog, limited $\text{O}_2$ causes incomplete combustion, turning the flame yellow/sooty and blackening cooking vessels!)
    • Unsaturated hydrocarbons (alkenes, alkynes, benzene) have a higher $\text{C}:\text{H}$ mass ratio, causing incomplete combustion with a yellow sooty flame emitting black unburnt carbon smoke.
  2. Controlled Oxidation: Alkaline $\text{KMnO}_4$ (purple → colorless) or acidified $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ (orange → green $\text{Cr}^{3+}$) oxidizes primary alcohols cleanly to carboxylic acids: $$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + 2[\text{O}] \xrightarrow{\text{Alk. KMnO}_4 + \Delta \;\text{ or }\; \text{Acid. K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7} \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
  3. Addition Reactions (Signature of Unsaturated Compounds Only):
    • Catalytic Hydrogenation: Adding $\text{H}_2$ across $\text{C}=\text{C}$ over finely divided Nickel ($\text{Ni}$) or Palladium ($\text{Pd}$) converts liquid unsaturated vegetable oils into solid saturated fats (vanaspati ghee): $$\text{R}_2\text{C}=\text{CR}_2 + \text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Ni / Pd},\; \Delta} \text{R}_2\text{CH}-\text{CHR}_2$$
    • Bromine-Water Test for Unsaturation: Shaking an alkene or alkyne with reddish-brown bromine water ($\text{Br}_2(aq)$) instantly decolorizes it by forming a colorless dibromo/tetrabromo adduct; saturated alkanes leave $\text{Br}_2$ water brown.
  4. Free-Radical Substitution (Signature of Saturated Alkanes): In diffused sunlight ($h\nu$), $\text{Cl}_2$ sequentially replaces $\text{H}$ atoms of methane: $$\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl} \xrightarrow{\text{Cl}_2/h\nu} \text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Cl}_2/h\nu} \text{CHCl}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{Cl}_2/h\nu} \text{CCl}_4$$

B. Mastery of Ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$) & Ethanoic Acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$)

  • Reaction with Sodium ($\text{Na}$): Both ethanol and ethanoic acid react with active $\text{Na}$ metal to liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas (pop sound), so $\text{Na}$ alone CANNOT distinguish an alcohol from a carboxylic acid: $$2\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + 2\text{Na} \longrightarrow \underbrace{2\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{ONa}}_{\text{Sodium ethoxide}} + \text{H}_2\uparrow, \qquad 2\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + 2\text{Na} \longrightarrow \underbrace{2\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}}_{\text{Sodium ethanoate}} + \text{H}_2\uparrow$$
  • Dehydration of Ethanol ($443\text{ K}$): Heating ethanol with excess concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ at $443\text{ K}$ ($170^\circ\text{C}$) eliminates $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ intramolecularly to form ethene ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ acts as a dehydrating agent): $$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{Hot conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4,\; 443\text{ K}} \text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$ (Olympiad Bridge: At the lower temperature of $413\text{ K}$ with excess ethanol, intermolecular dehydration yields diethyl ether, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OC}_2\text{H}_5$!)
  • Esterification (Acid + Alcohol $\rightleftharpoons$ Ester + Water): Warming a carboxylic acid with an alcohol in the presence of a few drops of conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ yields a sweet, fruity-smelling ester (used in perfumes and flavouring agents). Conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ serves both as acid catalyst and as dehydrating agent to drive the reversible equilibrium forward: $$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightleftharpoons{\text{Conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4,\; \Delta} \underbrace{\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5}_{\text{Ethyl ethanoate}} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
  • Saponification (Alkaline Hydrolysis of Esters): Heating an ester with aqueous $\text{NaOH}$ irreversibly splits it back into the alcohol and the sodium salt of the carboxylic acid (the foundation of soap-making from vegetable oil triglycerides): $$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{NaOH} \xrightarrow{\Delta} \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$
  • The Gold-Standard Distinguishing Test ($\text{NaHCO}_3 / \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ Effervescence): Ethanoic acid (glacial acetic acid, freezing point $290\text{ K}$) reacts briskly with sodium hydrogen carbonate or sodium carbonate to evolve $\text{CO}_2$ gas (turns lime water milky). Alcohols (and phenols) show ZERO reaction with $\text{NaHCO}_3$! $$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{NaHCO}_3 \longrightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$ $$2\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \longrightarrow 2\text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$

Cleansing Mechanism: Amphipathic Molecules & Micelles

A soap anion ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO}^-$) is amphipathic, possessing two antagonistic ends: a long non-polar hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail (oil-loving, water-fearing) and a short polar hydrophilic ionic head ($-\text{COO}^-$, water-loving). Because oily dirt does not dissolve in water, soap molecules spontaneously self-assemble above the Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC) into spherical clusters of $50\text{--}100$ ions called micelles:

  1. Hydrophobic tails bury inward into the oily/greasy stain, dissolving it via non-polar van der Waals interactions.
  2. Negatively charged $-\text{COO}^-$ hydrophilic heads point outward into the surrounding water.
  3. Mechanical agitation lifts the oil-loaded micelle off the cloth into water as a stable colloidal emulsion. Because every micelle carries a negatively charged exterior shell, micelles repel each other and refuse to coalesce, allowing clean water to rinse the dirt away completely. (Note: Micelles do NOT form in non-polar solvents like ethanol or hexane because the hydrocarbon tail dissolves directly in the solvent!)

Worked Example 1 — Counting $\sigma$ and $\pi$ Bonds in Hydrocarbons Level 1 Bonding & Structure
Calculate the exact number of sigma ($\sigma$) bonds and pi ($\pi$) bonds present in a single molecule of: (a) Propane ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$), (b) Propyne ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_4$), and (c) Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$).

Rule: Every single bond $= 1\sigma$; every double bond $= 1\sigma + 1\pi$; every triple bond $= 1\sigma + 2\pi$.

  • (a) Propane ($\text{CH}_3-\text{CH}_2-\text{CH}_3$): Contains $2$ $\text{C}-\text{C}$ single bonds and $8$ $\text{C}-\text{H}$ single bonds $\implies \mathbf{10\sigma}$ and $\mathbf{0\pi}$.
  • (b) Propyne ($\text{HC}\equiv\text{C}-\text{CH}_3$): Contains $1$ $\text{C}\equiv\text{C}$ ($1\sigma + 2\pi$), $1$ $\text{C}-\text{C}$ ($1\sigma$), and $4$ $\text{C}-\text{H}$ ($4\sigma$) $\implies (1 + 1 + 4) = \mathbf{6\sigma}$ and $\mathbf{2\pi}$.
  • (c) Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$, hexagonal ring with 3 alternating double bonds): Contains $6$ $\text{C}-\text{C}$ $\sigma$-bonds around the ring, $6$ $\text{C}-\text{H}$ $\sigma$-bonds, and $3$ $\pi$-bonds $\implies \mathbf{12\sigma}$ and $\mathbf{3\pi}$.

Quick Audit Formula ($\sigma = x + y - 1 + R$): Propane ($3+8-1+0=10$); Propyne ($3+4-1+0=6$); Benzene ($6+6-1+1=12$). All three match identically!

  1. 4-membered ring: Cyclobutane → $1$ isomer.
  2. 3-membered ring + 1 methyl: Methylcyclopropane → $1$ isomer.

Worked Example 7 — Exhaustive Structural Isomer Count of $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}\text{O}$ Level 3 Olympiad Counting
Determine the exact total number of structural isomers (both alcohols and ethers) having the molecular formula $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}\text{O}$. Classify them cleanly by functional group.

First-Principles Derivation:

First check $\text{DU} = 5 + 1 - \dfrac{12}{2} = 0$ (oxygen ignored). With $\text{DU} = 0$ and one $\text{O}$ atom, ONLY saturated acyclic alcohols ($\text{R}-\text{OH}$) and ethers ($\text{R}-\text{O}-\text{R}'$) are possible.

Part 1 — Alcohols ($\text{C}_5\text{H}_{11}-\text{OH}$): Take each of the $3$ carbon skeletons of $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}$ and count unique non-equivalent $\text{H}$-substitution sites:

  1. From $n$-pentane ($\overset{a}{\text{C}}-\overset{b}{\text{C}}-\overset{c}{\text{C}}-\overset{b}{\text{C}}-\overset{a}{\text{C}}$): $3$ distinct positions → pentan-1-ol, pentan-2-ol, pentan-3-ol ($3$ alcohols).
  2. From isopentane ($\overset{a}{\text{C}}-\overset{b}{\text{C}}(\overset{a}{\text{C}})-\overset{c}{\text{C}}-\overset{d}{\text{C}}$): $4$ distinct positions → 2-methylbutan-1-ol, 2-methylbutan-2-ol, 3-methylbutan-2-ol, 3-methylbutan-1-ol ($4$ alcohols).
  3. From neopentane ($\text{C}(\overset{a}{\text{C}}\text{H}_3)_4$): Central carbon has no $\text{H}$; all $4$ methyls are symmetric → 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol ($1$ alcohol).

Total Alcohols $= 3 + 4 + 1 = \mathbf{8}$.

Part 2 — Ethers ($\text{R}-\text{O}-\text{R}'$ where $C_R + C_{R'} = 5$): Partition $5$ carbons into two unordered non-empty groups:

  • Partition $(1 + 4)$ — Methyl + Butyl: Methyl ($\text{CH}_3-$) has $1$ form; Butyl ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_9-$) has $4$ forms ($n$-butyl, *sec*-butyl, *iso*-butyl, *tert*-butyl) → $1 \times 4 = \mathbf{4}$ ethers (1-methoxybutane, 2-methoxybutane, 1-methoxy-2-methylpropane, 2-methoxy-2-methylpropane).
  • Partition $(2 + 3)$ — Ethyl + Propyl: Ethyl ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5-$) has $1$ form; Propyl ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_7-$) has $2$ forms ($n$-propyl, *iso*-propyl) → $1 \times 2 = \mathbf{2}$ ethers (1-ethoxypropane, 2-ethoxypropane).

Total Ethers $= 4 + 2 = \mathbf{6}$.
Grand Total Structural Isomers of $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{12}\text{O} = 8 + 6 = \mathbf{14}$.

30-Second Exam Shortcut (Alkyl Radical Partition Law): Memorize the radical vector $R_1=1,\; R_2=1,\; R_3=2,\; R_4=4,\; R_5=8$. For any $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}\text{O}$: $$\text{Alcohols} = R_n = R_5 = \mathbf{8}; \qquad \text{Ethers} = \sum_{i+j=5} R_i R_j = R_1 R_4 + R_2 R_3 = (1)(4) + (1)(2) = \mathbf{6}$$ $$\text{Total} = 8 + 6 = \mathbf{14}\text{ in 10 seconds with zero drawings!}$$
Worked Example 8 — Combustion Formula Deduction, Acyclic Isomers & Hydrogenation Level 3 Olympiad Quantitative
Complete combustion of $0.20\text{ mol}$ of an acyclic gaseous hydrocarbon $X$ produces $35.2\text{ g}$ of $\text{CO}_2$ and $10.8\text{ g}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Determine: (a) the molecular formula and DU of $X$, (b) the exact volume of $\text{O}_2$ consumed at STP ($1\text{ mol} = 22.4\text{ L}$), (c) the number of acyclic structural isomers of $X$, and (d) the moles of $\text{H}_2$ required to fully hydrogenate $0.20\text{ mol}$ of $X$ over a $\text{Ni}$ catalyst. (Atomic masses: $\text{C} = 12, \text{H} = 1, \text{O} = 16$).

Step 1 (Deduce Molecular Formula via Atom Conservation):

$$n(\text{CO}_2) = \frac{35.2}{44} = 0.80\text{ mol C}, \qquad n(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = \frac{10.8}{18} = 0.60\text{ mol H}_2\text{O} \implies 1.20\text{ mol H}$$

Dividing by $0.20\text{ mol}$ of $X$ gives atoms per molecule:

$$x = \frac{0.80}{0.20} = 4, \qquad y = \frac{1.20}{0.20} = 6 \implies \mathbf{X = \text{C}_4\text{H}_6}$$

Degree of Unsaturation: $\text{DU} = 4 + 1 - \dfrac{6}{2} = \mathbf{2}$.

Step 2 (Oxygen Volume at STP): Balanced combustion equation:

$$\text{C}_4\text{H}_6 + \frac{11}{2}\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 4\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Moles of $\text{O}_2$ for $0.20\text{ mol }\text{C}_4\text{H}_6 = 0.20 \times 5.5 = 1.10\text{ mol} \implies V(\text{O}_2) = 1.10 \times 22.4 = \mathbf{24.64\text{ L}}$.

Step 3 (Acyclic Structural Isomers of $\text{C}_4\text{H}_6$): Since $X$ is acyclic with $\text{DU} = 2$, it must contain either $1$ triple bond (alkynes) or $2$ double bonds (alkadienes):

  • Alkynes: But-1-yne ($\text{HC}\equiv\text{CCH}_2\text{CH}_3$) and But-2-yne ($\text{CH}_3\text{C}\equiv\text{CCH}_3$) → $2$ isomers. (No branched 4-C alkyne exists without violating carbon tetravalency!)
  • Alkadienes: Buta-1,3-diene ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}-\text{CH}=\text{CH}_2$) and Buta-1,2-diene ($\text{CH}_2=\text{C}=\text{CHCH}_3$) → $2$ isomers.

Total acyclic structural isomers $= 2 + 2 = \mathbf{4}$.

Step 4 (Catalytic Hydrogenation): Complete hydrogenation over $\text{Ni}$ converts every $\pi$-bond into $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bonds ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_6 + 2\text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Ni}} \text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$). Thus $0.20\text{ mol}$ of $X$ absorbs $0.20 \times 2 = \mathbf{0.40\text{ mol H}_2}$.

30-Second Exam Shortcut (Direct $\text{O}_2$ & $\text{H}_2$ Ratios): Skip balancing completely:
• $\text{O}_2$ per mole of $\text{C}_x\text{H}_y = x + \dfrac{y}{4} = 4 + \dfrac{6}{4} = 5.5\text{ mol} \implies 0.20 \times 5.5 \times 22.4 = \mathbf{24.64\text{ L}}$.
• $\text{H}_2$ absorbed per mole of any acyclic hydrocarbon $= \text{DU} = 2\text{ mol} \implies 0.20 \times 2 = \mathbf{0.40\text{ mol}}$.
Worked Example 9 — Hard-Water Scum Stoichiometry: Soap vs. Detergent Level 3 Olympiad Applied
A $10.0\text{ L}$ bucket of hard borewell water contains $111\text{ mg L}^{-1}$ of dissolved calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$). Sodium stearate soap ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa}$, molar mass $306\text{ g mol}^{-1}$) is added until all $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ions precipitate completely as insoluble scum. Calculate: (a) the exact mass of soap wasted before lathering begins, (b) the exact mass of insoluble calcium stearate scum formed, and (c) the mass of scum that would form if a synthetic sulfonate detergent were used instead. (Atomic masses: $\text{Ca} = 40, \text{Cl} = 35.5, \text{Na} = 23, \text{C} = 12, \text{H} = 1, \text{O} = 16$).

First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 (Moles of $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ in $10.0\text{ L}$):

$$m(\text{CaCl}_2) = 10.0\text{ L} \times 111\text{ mg L}^{-1} = 1110\text{ mg} = 1.11\text{ g}$$ $$M(\text{CaCl}_2) = 40 + 2(35.5) = 111\text{ g mol}^{-1} \implies n(\text{Ca}^{2+}) = n(\text{CaCl}_2) = \frac{1.11}{111} = 0.010\text{ mol}$$

Step 2 (Balanced Scum Precipitation Equation): Each divalent $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ion binds two monovalent stearate anions:

$$2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa}(aq) + \text{CaCl}_2(aq) \longrightarrow \underbrace{(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}(s)\downarrow}_{\text{Insoluble Scum}} + 2\text{NaCl}(aq)$$
  • (a) Mass of soap wasted: $n(\text{soap}) = 2 \times n(\text{Ca}^{2+}) = 2 \times 0.010 = 0.020\text{ mol}$: $$m(\text{soap wasted}) = 0.020 \times 306 = \mathbf{6.12\text{ g}}$$
  • (b) Mass of calcium stearate scum: The stearate ion mass is $306 - 23 = 283\text{ g mol}^{-1}$, so $M\left[(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}\right] = 2(283) + 40 = 606\text{ g mol}^{-1}$: $$m(\text{scum}) = 0.010 \times 606 = \mathbf{6.06\text{ g}}$$
  • (c) Synthetic Detergent: Calcium salts of long-chain alkylbenzenesulfonates are completely water-soluble, so the mass of scum precipitated is strictly $0\text{ g}$.
30-Second Exam Shortcut (The "$-6\text{ u}$ Cation-Swap" Trick): Never re-sum $18\text{ C} + 35\text{ H} + 2\text{ O}$ to find calcium-scum molar mass! Swapping $2\text{ Na}^+$ ($2 \times 23 = 46\text{ u}$) for $1\text{ Ca}^{2+}$ ($40\text{ u}$) drops the formula mass by exactly $46 - 40 = \mathbf{6\text{ u}}$: $$M_{\text{Ca-scum}} = 2 M_{\text{soap}} - 6 = 2(306) - 6 = 606\text{ g mol}^{-1} \implies m_{\text{scum}} = 0.010 \times 606 = \mathbf{6.06\text{ g}}!$$ (For $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ hard water, the swap drops by $46 - 24 = 22\text{ u}$, giving $M_{\text{Mg-scum}} = 2 M_{\text{soap}} - 22$!)
