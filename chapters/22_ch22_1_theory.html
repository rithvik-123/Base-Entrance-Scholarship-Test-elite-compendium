Specific Charge ($e/m$) Hierarchy & Nuclear Notation ($^A_Z X^{q}$)

1. Increasing Order of Specific Charge ($|q|/m$): Comparing a neutron ($0/1 = 0$), an $\alpha$-particle ($^4_2\text{He}^{2+}$, $2/4 = 0.5\text{ units}$), a proton ($1/1 = 1\text{ unit}$), and an electron ($1/(1/1836) = 1836\text{ units}$):

$$\left(\frac{e}{m}\right)_{\text{neutron}} \lt \left(\frac{e}{m}\right)_{\alpha\text{-particle}} \lt \left(\frac{e}{m}\right)_{\text{proton}} \lt \left(\frac{e}{m}\right)_{\text{electron}}$$

2. Exact Counting Rules for $^A_Z X^{q}$:

  • Atomic Number ($Z$) $= \text{Number of protons }(p)$ — uniquely identifies the element.
  • Mass Number ($A$) $= p + n$ (total nucleons) $\implies \text{Number of neutrons }(n) = A - Z$.
  • Number of electrons ($e^-$): For a neutral atom, $e^- = Z$. For a cation $X^{c+}$, $e^- = Z - c$. For an anion $X^{a-}$, $e^- = Z + a$.

22.2 Evolution of Atomic Models: Thomson to Rutherford

J.J. Thomson's Plum Pudding Model (1904): Visualized the atom as a uniform sphere of positive charge (radius $\sim 10^{-10}\text{ m}$) with negatively charged electrons embedded inside like seeds in a watermelon. While it accounted for overall electrical neutrality, it predicted that heavy, fast-moving $\alpha$-particles should pass through thin metal foil with practically zero large-angle deflection—a prediction spectacularly disproved by Rutherford's students Geiger and Marsden in 1911.

Figure 22.1: Rutherford's nuclear scattering experiment. Because $R_{\text{atom}} / R_{\text{nucleus}} \approx 10^5$, the volume of the atom is roughly $(10^5)^3 = 10^{15}$ times larger than the nucleus!
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Nuclear Radius Scaling, Closest Approach & Maxwell's Collapse
  • Empirical Nuclear Radius Law: Nuclear density is virtually constant across all elements, so nuclear volume $\propto A$ (mass number), giving $\boxed{R = R_0 A^{1/3}}$ where $R_0 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m} = 1.3\text{ fm}$. For example, comparing $^{64}\text{Cu}$ and $^8\text{Be}$, $\frac{R_{\text{Cu}}}{R_{\text{Be}}} = \left(\frac{64}{8}\right)^{1/3} = 2$.
  • Distance of Closest Approach ($r_0$): In a head-on collision, the $\alpha$-particle ($q_1 = +2e$) stops momentarily at distance $r_0$ from the nucleus ($q_2 = +Ze$) when all initial kinetic energy converts into electrostatic potential energy: $$\text{KE}_{\alpha} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(2e)(Ze)}{r_0} \implies r_0 = \frac{2 k Z e^2}{\text{KE}_{\alpha}}$$ Exam Intuition: Doubling the $\alpha$-particle's speed quadruples its $\text{KE}$, cutting $r_0$ to $\frac{1}{4}$! Notice $r_0$ sets an upper bound on the nuclear radius.
  • Fatal Drawback (Maxwell's Electrodynamics): According to classical electromagnetism, any accelerating charged particle radiates continuous electromagnetic energy. An orbiting electron undergoes continuous centripetal acceleration, so it should lose energy, spiral into the nucleus in $\sim 10^{-8}\text{ s}$ (atom collapse!), and emit a continuous spectrum instead of the sharp line spectrum observed experimentally.

22.3 Bohr's Atomic Model & Hydrogen Spectral Series (Foundation Bridge)

Niels Bohr (1913) resolved Rutherford's stability paradox by introducing quantum restrictions for single-electron (hydrogen-like) species ($\text{H}, \text{He}^+, \text{Li}^{2+}, \text{Be}^{3+}$):

  1. Stationary Orbits: Electrons revolve around the nucleus only in certain discrete circular orbits of fixed energy called stationary states ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$) without radiating electromagnetic energy.
  2. Angular Momentum Quantization: Allowed orbits are strictly those where the electron's orbital angular momentum $L = mvr$ is an integral multiple of $\frac{h}{2\pi}$: $$\boxed{mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi} = n\hbar} \qquad (n = 1, 2, 3, \dots)$$
  3. Quantum Jumps & Line Spectra: Energy is absorbed or emitted ONLY when an electron jumps between two stationary orbits: $\Delta E = E_{\text{high}} - E_{\text{low}} = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$.
First-Principles Derivation of Bohr Scaling Laws (For Any 1-Electron Ion of Atomic Number $Z$)

Equating Coulomb attraction to centripetal force gives (i) $\frac{k Z e^2}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r} \implies m v^2 r = k Z e^2$, and quantization gives (ii) $m v r = \frac{nh}{2\pi}$. Dividing (i) by (ii) eliminates $r$ immediately to yield velocity $v_n$; substituting $v_n$ back into (ii) yields radius $r_n$:

  • Orbit Radius: $\boxed{r_n = 0.529 \frac{n^2}{Z}\text{ Å}} = a_0 \frac{n^2}{Z}$   (Proportionality: $r_n \propto \frac{n^2}{Z}$)
  • Electron Speed: $\boxed{v_n = 2.18 \times 10^6 \frac{Z}{n}\text{ m/s}} \approx \frac{c}{137}\frac{Z}{n}$   (Proportionality: $v_n \propto \frac{Z}{n}$)
  • Orbital Time Period & Frequency: $T_n = \frac{2\pi r_n}{v_n} \propto \frac{n^3}{Z^2}$,   $f_n = \frac{1}{T_n} \propto \frac{Z^2}{n^3}$
  • Total Energy ($TE$), Kinetic Energy ($KE$), and Potential Energy ($PE$): $$KE_n = \frac{1}{2}mv_n^2 = +\frac{k Z e^2}{2 r_n}, \qquad PE_n = -\frac{k Z e^2}{r_n}, \qquad TE_n = KE_n + PE_n = -\frac{k Z e^2}{2 r_n}$$ $$\boxed{E_n = TE_n = -13.6 \frac{Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV/atom}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \boxed{TE = -KE = \frac{PE}{2}}$$
  • Ionization & Separation Energy: Ionization energy (ground state $n=1 \rightarrow \infty$) $= \mathbf{+13.6 Z^2\text{ eV}}$. Separation energy from any excited state $n$ ($n \rightarrow \infty$) $= \mathbf{+13.6 \frac{Z^2}{n^2}\text{ eV}}$.
Figure 22.2: Quantized energy levels of the H-atom. Notice how energy gaps shrink rapidly as $n$ increases: the jump $2 \rightarrow 1$ ($10.2\text{ eV}$) alone exceeds $75\%$ of the entire ionization energy ($13.6\text{ eV}$)!
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Rydberg Formula & Spectral Line Counting Traps

1. Rydberg Equation for Wavenumber ($\bar{\nu} = 1/\lambda$):

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = \bar{\nu} = R_H Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right) \qquad (n_2 \gt n_1, \quad R_H \approx 1.097 \times 10^7\text{ m}^{-1})$$
  • First line (longest $\lambda$, smallest $\Delta E$) of any series: $n_2 = n_1 + 1$ (e.g., Balmer $\text{H}_\alpha$: $3 \rightarrow 2$).
  • Last line / Series Limit (shortest $\lambda$, largest $\Delta E$): $n_2 = \infty \rightarrow n_1$.

2. Emission Line Counting — Sample vs. Single Atom Trap!

  • Large Sample of H-atoms all excited to level $n$ returning to ground state ($1$): Every pair of levels produces a unique line $\implies \boxed{N_{\text{max}} = \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}}$. From $n_2$ down to $n_1$: $N = \frac{(n_2 - n_1)(n_2 - n_1 + 1)}{2}$.
  • ONE Single H-atom de-exciting from $n$ to $1$: A single electron can make at most $(n-1)$ consecutive single-step hops ($n \rightarrow n-1 \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow 1$), so $N_{\max,\text{ single atom}} = \mathbf{n - 1}$!
  • Series Breakdown from level $n$: Lines in Lyman ($n_1=1$) $= n - 1$; Lines in Balmer ($n_1=2$) $= n - 2$; Lines in Paschen ($n_1=3$) $= n - 3$ (for $n \gt n_1$).

3. Limitation of Bohr's Model: Fails completely for multi-electron atoms ($\text{He}, \text{Li}^+, \text{Be}^{2+}$) due to inter-electronic repulsions, cannot explain fine splitting of spectral lines in magnetic (Zeeman effect) or electric (Stark effect) fields, and violates Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle ($\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi}$).

22.4 Electronic Configuration & Quantum Numbers Bridge

Class 10 introduces the Bohr–Bury Shell Rules: a shell $n$ holds at most $2n^2$ electrons ($K=2, L=8, M=18, N=32$), yet the outermost shell cannot exceed $8$ electrons (explaining why Potassium $[Z=19]$ is $2, 8, 8, 1$ rather than $2, 8, 9$). Quantum mechanics explains why via four Quantum Numbers that give every electron a unique 4-part address:

Quantum Number Symbol Allowed Values Physical Meaning & Capacity
Principal $n$ $1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ ($K, L, M, N$) Determines shell size and main energy. Total orbitals $= n^2$; max electrons $= 2n^2$.
Azimuthal (Orbital Angular Momentum) $l$ $0, 1, 2, \dots, (n-1)$ Determines subshell shape: $l=0\ (s\text{, spherical})$, $l=1\ (p\text{, dumbbell})$, $l=2\ (d\text{, cloverleaf})$, $l=3\ (f)$.
Magnetic $m_l$ $-l, \dots, 0, \dots, +l$ Determines 3D spatial orientation of orbitals. Orbitals per subshell $= 2l + 1$ ($s:1, p:3, d:5, f:7$).
Spin $m_s$ $+\frac{1}{2}\ (\uparrow)$ or $-\frac{1}{2}\ (\downarrow)$ Intrinsic electron spin angular momentum. Max electrons per subshell $= 2(2l+1)$ ($s:2, p:6, d:10, f:14$).
The Three Golden Filling Rules (Aufbau, Pauli, Hund)
  1. Aufbau Principle & $(n + l)$ Rule: Orbitals fill in increasing order of energy. For multi-electron atoms, subshell energy increases with $(n + l)$. Tie-Breaker: If two subshells have the same $(n + l)$ value, the subshell with the lower $n$ has lower energy and fills first!
    Example: $4s$ has $n+l = 4+0 = 4$; $3d$ has $n+l = 3+2 = 5$. Thus $4s$ fills BEFORE $3d$! Between $3p$ ($3+1=4$) and $4s$ ($4+0=4$), $3p$ has lower $n$ so $3p$ fills first.
  2. Pauli Exclusion Principle: No two electrons in the same atom can have the exact same set of all four quantum numbers $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$. Consequently, any single orbital holds a maximum of $2$ electrons with opposite spins ($\uparrow\downarrow$).
  3. Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity: Pairing of electrons in degenerate orbitals ($p_x, p_y, p_z$ or the five $d$-orbitals) does NOT begin until each orbital in that subshell is singly occupied with parallel spins ($\uparrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow$).
Figure 22.3: Diagonal Aufbau filling chart up to $6p$. Remember the golden asymmetry: $4s$ fills before $3d$, yet when forming transition-metal cations ($\text{Fe}^{2+}, \text{Cr}^{3+}, \text{Cu}^{2+}$), electrons ALWAYS leave the outermost $4s$ orbital first!
High-Yield Configuration Reference ($Z = 19$ to $30$) & Cation Trap

Using $[\text{Ar}] = 1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6$ ($18e^-$): $\text{K}(19): [\text{Ar}]4s^1$; $\text{Ca}(20): [\text{Ar}]4s^2$; $\text{Sc}(21): [\text{Ar}]3d^1 4s^2$; $\text{Ti}(22): [\text{Ar}]3d^2 4s^2$; $\text{V}(23): [\text{Ar}]3d^3 4s^2$; $\text{Cr}(24): [\text{Ar}]3d^5 4s^1$; $\text{Mn}(25): [\text{Ar}]3d^5 4s^2$; $\text{Fe}(26): [\text{Ar}]3d^6 4s^2$; $\text{Co}(27): [\text{Ar}]3d^7 4s^2$; $\text{Ni}(28): [\text{Ar}]3d^8 4s^2$; $\text{Cu}(29): [\text{Ar}]3d^{10} 4s^1$; $\text{Zn}(30): [\text{Ar}]3d^{10} 4s^2$.

Cation Trap: Because $4s$ lies physically farther from the nucleus than $3d$, forming $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ removes the two $4s$ electrons FIRST: $\text{Fe}^{2+} = [\text{Ar}]3d^6$ ($4$ unpaired $e^-$), NOT $[\text{Ar}]3d^4 4s^2$! Similarly, $\text{Fe}^{3+} = [\text{Ar}]3d^5$ ($5$ unpaired $e^-$, extra stable half-filled) and $\text{Zn}^{2+} = [\text{Ar}]3d^{10}$ ($0$ unpaired $e^-$, diamagnetic). Olympiad Bonus: Spin-only magnetic moment $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\text{ BM}$ where $n =$ unpaired electrons.

22.5 Chemical Bonding: Octet Rule, Ionic/Covalent/Coordinate, Fajan's Rules & VSEPR

Atoms bond to lower their potential energy. According to the Kossel–Lewis Octet Rule, main-group atoms tend to gain, lose, or share valence electrons to attain a stable $ns^2 np^6$ noble-gas configuration ($8$ valence electrons, or $2$ for duplet species like $\text{H}, \text{He}, \text{Li}^+, \text{Be}^{2+}$).

Three Crucial Exceptions to the Octet Rule (Always Tested in BEST MCQs!)
  • 1. Incomplete Octet (Electron-Deficient / Hypovalent): Central atom has fewer than $8$ valence electrons — e.g., $\text{BeCl}_2$ ($4e^-$ around $\text{Be}$), $\text{BF}_3$ and $\text{AlCl}_3$ ($6e^-$ around $\text{B}/\text{Al}$). These act as strong Lewis acids (electron-pair acceptors).
  • 2. Expanded Octet (Hypervalent): Period 3 and heavier non-metals use vacant $d$-orbitals to hold more than $8$ electrons — e.g., $\text{PCl}_5$ ($10e^-$), $\text{SF}_6$ ($12e^-$), $\text{IF}_7$ ($14e^-$), $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($12e^-$ on S). (Note: Period 2 elements like $\text{C}, \text{N}, \text{O}, \text{F}$ NEVER exceed $8$ electrons because they lack $2d$ orbitals—hence $\text{NCl}_5$ and $\text{OF}_6$ do not exist!)
  • 3. Odd-Electron Molecules: Contain an odd total number of valence electrons and remain paramagnetic — e.g., $\text{NO}$ ($11$ valence $e^-$), $\text{NO}_2$ ($17$ valence $e^-$), $\text{ClO}_2$ ($19$ valence $e^-$).

Sigma ($\sigma$) vs. Pi ($\pi$) Bonds & Formal Charge Formula

1. $\sigma$ vs. $\pi$ Bonds: A $\sigma$-bond forms by head-on (axial) orbital overlap ($s\text{–}s, s\text{–}p, p\text{–}p$), is stronger, exists independently, and permits free rotation. A $\pi$-bond forms by sideways (lateral) $p\text{–}p$ overlap, is weaker, forms ONLY alongside a $\sigma$-bond, and locks rotation. Quick Count: Every single bond $= 1\sigma$; double bond $= 1\sigma + 1\pi$; triple bond $= 1\sigma + 2\pi$.

2. Formal Charge ($\text{FC}$): Bookkeeping charge assigned to an atom in a Lewis structure assuming equal sharing of bonding electrons:

$$\boxed{\text{FC} = V - L - \frac{B}{2} = (\text{Valence } e^-\text{ in free atom}) - (\text{Unshared lone-pair } e^-) - (\text{Number of covalent bonds})}$$

Sanity Check: The algebraic sum of formal charges over all atoms MUST equal the net charge of the molecule ($0$) or polyatomic ion. Preferred resonance/Lewis structures minimize $|\text{FC}|$ and place any negative formal charge on the most electronegative atom.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Fajan's Rules (Covalent Character in Ionic Bonds)

No ionic bond is $100\%$ ionic. When a cation approaches an anion, it attracts and distorts (polarizes) the anion's electron cloud toward itself, pulling electron density into the internuclear region—inducing partial covalent character. By Fajan's Rules, covalent character increases when:

  1. Smaller Cation Size: Higher surface charge density $\implies$ stronger polarizing power ($\text{LiCl} \gt \text{NaCl} \gt \text{KCl} \gt \text{RbCl}$).
  2. Higher Charge on Cation or Anion: Polarizing power $\propto q/r$ ($\text{AlCl}_3 \gt \text{MgCl}_2 \gt \text{NaCl}$; $\text{SnCl}_4$ is a volatile covalent liquid while $\text{SnCl}_2$ is an ionic solid!).
  3. Larger Anion Size: Outer valence electrons are loosely held and easily distorted ($\text{LiI} \gt \text{LiBr} \gt \text{LiCl} \gt \text{LiF}$).
  4. Pseudo-Noble-Gas Configuration ($18e^-$ outer shell): Cations with $ns^2 np^6 nd^{10}$ shells (like $\text{Cu}^+, \text{Ag}^+, \text{Zn}^{2+}, \text{Cd}^{2+}$) have poorly shielding $d$-electrons and polarize anions far more strongly than $8e^-$ noble-gas cations of similar radius ($\text{Na}^+, \text{Ca}^{2+}$). Thus $\text{CuCl}$ (MP $426^\circ\text{C}$, insoluble in water) is far more covalent than $\text{NaCl}$ (MP $801^\circ\text{C}$).

Observable Consequences: Increasing covalent character generally lowers melting point, reduces solubility in polar water, and increases solubility in non-polar organic solvents (e.g., $\text{LiCl}$ dissolves in acetone/ethanol whereas $\text{NaCl}$ does not).

VSEPR Theory, 3D Molecular Shapes & Dipole Moment (Polarity)

Under VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) Theory, electron pairs around a central atom arrange themselves in 3D space to minimize electrostatic repulsion, governed by the hierarchy:

$$\boxed{\text{lone pair–lone pair (lp–lp)} \gt \text{lone pair–bond pair (lp–bp)} \gt \text{bond pair–bond pair (bp–bp)}}$$

To predict the shape of any main-group molecule or ion in 10 seconds, calculate its Steric Number ($\text{SN} = \text{bp} + \text{lp}$):

$$\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}\left[V + M - C + A\right]$$

where $V =$ valence electrons of central atom, $M =$ number of monovalent atoms attached ($\text{H, F, Cl, Br, I}$ — do NOT count divalent $\text{O}$ or $\text{S}$ in $M$!), $C =$ positive charge on cation, and $A =$ negative charge on anion. Then $\text{lp} = \text{SN} - (\text{total atoms bonded to central atom})$.

Level 1 Nuclear & Electronic Species Worked Example 1: Classifying Isobars, Isotones, Isoelectronic Species & Isodiaphers

Consider the following set of seven atomic/ionic species: $^{31}_{15}\text{P}^{3-}$, $^{32}_{16}\text{S}^{2-}$, $^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$, $^{39}_{19}\text{K}^+$, $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}^{2+}$, $^{238}_{92}\text{U}$, and $^{234}_{90}\text{Th}$.

(a) Tabulate protons ($p$), neutrons ($n$), and electrons ($e^-$) for the first five species. (b) Identify from the full list: an isoelectronic series, a pair of isobars, two distinct pairs of isotones, and a pair of isodiaphers.

Part (a) — Subatomic Particle Breakdown:

  • $^{31}_{15}\text{P}^{3-}$: $p = 15$, $n = 31 - 15 = 16$, $e^- = 15 + 3 = 18$.
  • $^{32}_{16}\text{S}^{2-}$: $p = 16$, $n = 32 - 16 = 16$, $e^- = 16 + 2 = 18$.
  • $^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$: $p = 18$, $n = 40 - 18 = 22$, $e^- = 18$.
  • $^{39}_{19}\text{K}^+$: $p = 19$, $n = 39 - 19 = 20$, $e^- = 19 - 1 = 18$.
  • $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}^{2+}$: $p = 20$, $n = 40 - 20 = 20$, $e^- = 20 - 2 = 18$.

Part (b) — Exact Classifications:

  • Isoelectronic Series ($18e^-$ each): $\mathbf{\text{P}^{3-},\ \text{S}^{2-},\ \text{Ar},\ \text{K}^+,\ \text{Ca}^{2+}}$.
  • Isobars (same $A = 40$, different $Z$): $\mathbf{^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}\text{ and }^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}^{2+}}$.
  • Isotones (same $n = A - Z$): Pair 1 ($n = 16$): $\mathbf{^{31}_{15}\text{P}^{3-}\text{ and }^{32}_{16}\text{S}^{2-}}$; Pair 2 ($n = 20$): $\mathbf{^{39}_{19}\text{K}^+\text{ and }^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}^{2+}}$.
  • Isodiaphers (same $n - p = A - 2Z$): For $^{238}_{92}\text{U}$, $A - 2Z = 238 - 184 = 54$; for $^{234}_{90}\text{Th}$, $A - 2Z = 234 - 180 = 54$. Thus $\mathbf{^{238}_{92}\text{U}\text{ and }^{234}_{90}\text{Th}}$ are isodiaphers (linked by one $\alpha$-decay!).
Level 1 Bohr Scaling Laws Worked Example 2: Orbit Radius, Energy & Ionization Energy of $\text{He}^+$

For the single-electron helium ion $\text{He}^+$ ($Z = 2$), calculate: (a) the radius of the 2nd Bohr orbit (in $\text{Å}$), (b) the total energy of the electron in the 2nd orbit (in $\text{eV}$), and (c) the ionization energy of $\text{He}^+$ from its ground state ($n = 1$).

Because $\text{He}^+$ has exactly $1$ electron ($Z = 2$), Bohr's scaling equations apply rigorously:

Part (a) — Radius of 2nd Orbit ($n = 2, Z = 2$):

$$r_2(\text{He}^+) = 0.529 \times \frac{n^2}{Z} = 0.529 \times \frac{2^2}{2} = 0.529 \times 2 = \mathbf{1.058\text{ Å}}$$

Part (b) — Total Energy in 2nd Orbit ($n = 2, Z = 2$):

$$E_2(\text{He}^+) = -13.6 \times \frac{Z^2}{n^2} = -13.6 \times \frac{2^2}{2^2} = \mathbf{-13.6\text{ eV}}$$

(Notice the exact degeneracy: the 2nd orbit of $\text{He}^+$ has the exact same energy as the 1st orbit of Hydrogen!)

Part (c) — Ground-State Ionization Energy ($n = 1 \rightarrow \infty$):

$$E_1(\text{He}^+) = -13.6 \times \frac{2^2}{1^2} = -54.4\text{ eV}$$ $$\text{IE}(\text{He}^+) = E_\infty - E_1 = 0 - (-54.4) = \mathbf{+54.4\text{ eV}}$$
Level 1 Electronic Configuration & Cations Worked Example 3: Ground-State Exceptions ($\text{Cr}, \text{Cu}$) & Transition-Metal Cations ($\text{Fe}^{3+}, \text{Cu}^{2+}$)

Write the exact condensed noble-gas electronic configurations and find the number of unpaired electrons for: (a) $\text{Cr}\ (Z=24)$, (b) $\text{Cu}\ (Z=29)$, (c) $\text{Fe}^{3+}\ (Z=26)$, and (d) $\text{Cu}^{2+}\ (Z=29)$.

  • (a) $\text{Cr}\ (Z=24)$: Naive Aufbau predicts $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^4 4s^2$, but promoting one $4s$ electron achieves an exactly half-filled $3d^5$ subshell (high exchange energy + spherical symmetry): $$\text{Cr} = \mathbf{[\text{Ar}]\,3d^5 4s^1}$$ By Hund's rule, all five $3d$ electrons are unpaired ($\uparrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow\ \uparrow$) plus one $4s$ electron ($\uparrow$) $\implies$ $6$ unpaired electrons.
  • (b) $\text{Cu}\ (Z=29)$: Promoting one $4s$ electron achieves an exactly filled $3d^{10}$ subshell: $$\text{Cu} = \mathbf{[\text{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1}$$ All $3d$ electrons are paired ($0$ unpaired), leaving only the single $4s^1$ electron $\implies$ $1$ unpaired electron.
  • (c) $\text{Fe}^{3+}\ (Z=26)$: Neutral $\text{Fe} = [\text{Ar}]\,3d^6 4s^2$. Remove $3$ electrons starting from the outermost $4s$ shell first (two from $4s$, then one from $3d$): $$\text{Fe}^{3+} = \mathbf{[\text{Ar}]\,3d^5}$$ All five $3d$ orbitals are singly occupied $\implies$ $5$ unpaired electrons.
  • (d) $\text{Cu}^{2+}\ (Z=29)$: From $\text{Cu} = [\text{Ar}]\,3d^{10} 4s^1$, remove the $4s^1$ electron first, then one $3d$ electron: $$\text{Cu}^{2+} = \mathbf{[\text{Ar}]\,3d^9}$$ Four $3d$ orbitals are paired ($8e^-$) and one $3d$ orbital has a single electron $\implies$ $1$ unpaired electron.

Level 2 Bohr Virial Law & Line Counting Worked Example 4: Excited State of $\text{Li}^{2+}$ — Energy Breakdown & Emission Lines

In a large sample of $\text{Li}^{2+}$ ions ($Z = 3$), all electrons are excited to a stationary orbit where their total energy is $-13.6\text{ eV}$. (a) Find the principal quantum number $n$ of this orbit. (b) Determine the kinetic energy ($KE$) and electrostatic potential energy ($PE$) of each electron in this state. (c) When this sample returns to the ground state ($n = 1$), what is the maximum number of distinct emission spectral lines observed, and how many end at $n_1 = 2$?

Part (a) — Identify Orbit $n$:

$$E_n(\text{Li}^{2+}) = -13.6 \frac{Z^2}{n^2} = -13.6 \frac{3^2}{n^2} = -13.6\text{ eV}$$ $$\frac{9}{n^2} = 1 \implies n^2 = 9 \implies \mathbf{n = 3}$$

Part (b) — Kinetic & Potential Energies (Virial Relations): Using $TE = -KE = \frac{PE}{2}$:

$$KE = -TE = -(-13.6) = \mathbf{+13.6\text{ eV}}$$ $$PE = 2 \times TE = 2(-13.6) = \mathbf{-27.2\text{ eV}}$$

(Check: $KE + PE = +13.6 + (-27.2) = -13.6\text{ eV} = TE$ ✓)

Part (c) — Maximum Emission Lines (Sample of Ions):

$$N_{\max} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2} = \frac{3(3 - 1)}{2} = \mathbf{3\text{ lines}} \qquad (3 \rightarrow 2,\ 3 \rightarrow 1,\ 2 \rightarrow 1)$$

Lines terminating at $n_1 = 2$ $= n - 2 = 3 - 2 = \mathbf{1\text{ line}}$ (namely $3 \rightarrow 2$).

Level 2 $\sigma/\pi$ Bonds & Formal Charge Worked Example 5: Counting $\sigma/\pi$ Bonds in Benzene & Tetracyanoethylene + Ozone Formal Charges

(a) Count the exact number of $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonds in (i) benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$) and (ii) tetracyanoethylene, $(\text{NC})_2\text{C}=\text{C}(\text{CN})_2$.

(b) Using the Lewis structure $\text{O}_a = \text{O}_b \rightarrow \text{O}_c$ for ozone ($\text{O}_3$), calculate the formal charge on each of the three oxygen atoms.

Part (a)(i) — Benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$):

  • Six $\text{C–H}$ single bonds $= 6\sigma$.
  • Ring backbone has three $\text{C–C}$ single bonds ($3\sigma$) and three alternating $\text{C=C}$ double bonds ($3\sigma + 3\pi$).
  • Total in Benzene: $(6 + 3 + 3)\sigma = \mathbf{12\sigma}$ and $\mathbf{3\pi}$ bonds.

Part (a)(ii) — Tetracyanoethylene, $(\text{N}\equiv\text{C})_2\text{C}=\text{C}(\text{C}\equiv\text{N})_2$:

  • Central $\text{C=C}$ double bond: $1\sigma + 1\pi$.
  • Four $\text{C–C}$ single bonds connecting the central carbons to the four $-\text{CN}$ groups: $4\sigma$.
  • Four $-\text{C}\equiv\text{N}$ triple bonds (each is $1\sigma + 2\pi$): $4\sigma + 8\pi$.
  • Total in $\text{C}_2(\text{CN})_4$: $(1 + 4 + 4)\sigma = \mathbf{9\sigma}$ and $(1 + 8)\pi = \mathbf{9\pi}$ bonds!

Part (b) — Formal Charges in Ozone ($\text{O}_3$): Oxygen has $V = 6$ valence electrons; apply $\text{FC} = V - L - \frac{B}{2}$:

  • Terminal $\text{O}_a$ (double-bonded, $2$ lone pairs $= 4e^-$): $\text{FC}(\text{O}_a) = 6 - 4 - \frac{4}{2} = \mathbf{0}$.
  • Central $\text{O}_b$ ($3$ bonds $= 6$ bonding $e^-$, $1$ lone pair $= 2e^-$): $\text{FC}(\text{O}_b) = 6 - 2 - \frac{6}{2} = \mathbf{+1}$.
  • Terminal $\text{O}_c$ (single/coordinate bond $= 2$ bonding $e^-$, $3$ lone pairs $= 6e^-$): $\text{FC}(\text{O}_c) = 6 - 6 - \frac{2}{2} = \mathbf{-1}$.

Check: $0 + (+1) + (-1) = 0$ (matches neutral $\text{O}_3$ ✓).

Level 2 Fajan's Rules & Lattice Properties Worked Example 6: Predicting Covalent Character & Melting-Point Trends via Fajan's Rules

Apply Fajan's Rules to arrange each of the following sets in increasing order of covalent character, and justify which halide in pair (c) has the lower melting point:

(a) $\text{NaCl},\ \text{MgCl}_2,\ \text{AlCl}_3$   |   (b) $\text{LiF},\ \text{LiCl},\ \text{LiBr},\ \text{LiI}$   |   (c) $\text{NaCl}$ vs. $\text{CuCl}$ (given ionic radii $r(\text{Na}^+) \approx 0.95\text{ Å}$ and $r(\text{Cu}^+) \approx 0.96\text{ Å}$).

  • (a) Increasing Cation Charge & Shrinking Radius: All three share the same $\text{Cl}^-$ anion. Across Period 3, cation charge increases ($+1 \rightarrow +2 \rightarrow +3$) while ionic radius shrinks ($r_{\text{Na}^+} \gt r_{\text{Mg}^{2+}} \gt r_{\text{Al}^{3+}}$). Hence polarizing power increases sharply: $$\mathbf{\text{NaCl} \lt \text{MgCl}_2 \lt \text{AlCl}_3}$$
  • (b) Increasing Anion Size (Polarizability): All four share the tiny $\text{Li}^+$ cation. Down Group 17, anion size grows ($\text{F}^- \lt \text{Cl}^- \lt \text{Br}^- \lt \text{I}^-$), making the outer electron cloud progressively easier to distort: $$\mathbf{\text{LiF} \lt \text{LiCl} \lt \text{LiBr} \lt \text{LiI}}$$
  • (c) Pseudo-Noble-Gas Rule ($\text{NaCl}$ vs. $\text{CuCl}$): Both $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cu}^+$ have $+1$ charge and nearly identical radii ($\sim 0.95\text{ Å}$). However, $\text{Na}^+$ has a stable $8e^-$ noble-gas shell ($2s^2 2p^6$), whereas $\text{Cu}^+$ has an $18e^-$ pseudo-noble-gas shell ($3s^2 3p^6 3d^{10}$). Because $3d$ electrons shield nuclear charge very poorly, $\text{Cu}^+$ exerts a much stronger polarizing pull on $\text{Cl}^-$. Therefore, $\text{CuCl}$ is far more covalent than $\text{NaCl}$ and exhibits a much lower melting point ($\text{CuCl} \approx 426^\circ\text{C}$ vs. $\text{NaCl} \approx 801^\circ\text{C}$).

Level 3 Multi-Ion Spectral Ratio Worked Example 7: Wavelength Ratio Between Hydrogen Balmer & $\text{He}^+$ Lyman Limit

Calculate the exact ratio $\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}$, where $\lambda_1$ is the wavelength of the first line of the Balmer series of the hydrogen atom ($\text{H}, Z=1$) and $\lambda_2$ is the wavelength of the series limit (shortest wavelength) of the Lyman series of $\text{He}^+$ ($Z=2$).

Method 1 — Full Rydberg Equation Derivation:

  1. First line of Balmer series of $\text{H}$: Balmer series ends at $n_1 = 2$; its first (lowest-energy, longest-$\lambda$) line originates from $n_2 = 3$ with $Z = 1$: $$\frac{1}{\lambda_1} = R_H (1)^2 \left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2}\right) = R_H \left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{9}\right) = \frac{5 R_H}{36} \implies \lambda_1 = \frac{36}{5 R_H}$$
  2. Series limit of Lyman series of $\text{He}^+$: Lyman series ends at $n_1 = 1$; its series limit (highest-energy, shortest-$\lambda$) line originates from $n_2 = \infty$ with $Z = 2$: $$\frac{1}{\lambda_2} = R_H (2)^2 \left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{\infty^2}\right) = 4 R_H (1 - 0) = 4 R_H \implies \lambda_2 = \frac{1}{4 R_H}$$
  3. Take the Exact Ratio: $$\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = \frac{36 / (5 R_H)}{1 / (4 R_H)} = \frac{36 \times 4}{5} = \frac{144}{5} = \mathbf{28.8}$$
30-Second Olympiad Shortcut: Inverse Energy Proportionality ($\lambda \propto 1/\Delta E$)

Never touch $R_H$ or $hc$ in ratio questions! Since $\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E}$ and $\Delta E \propto Z^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$:

$$\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = \frac{\Delta E_2}{\Delta E_1} = \frac{2^2 \left(1 - 0\right)}{1^2 \left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{9}\right)} = \frac{4}{5/36} = \frac{144}{5} = \mathbf{28.8}$$
Level 3 Quantum Numbers Counting Worked Example 8: Exact Quantum-Number Electron Inventory in Ground-State Chromium ($Z = 24$)

In a neutral ground-state chromium atom ($\text{Cr}, Z = 24$), determine the exact number of electrons satisfying each condition: (a) $l = 1$, (b) $m_l = 0$, (c) $n + l = 4$, and (d) the maximum number of electrons simultaneously having $l = 2$ and $m_s = +\frac{1}{2}$.

Method 1 — Full Subshell-by-Subshell Quantum Audit:
First write the true anomalous ground-state configuration of $\text{Cr}\ (Z=24)$:

$$\text{Cr}\ (Z=24): \quad 1s^2\ 2s^2\ 2p^6\ 3s^2\ 3p^6\ 3d^5\ 4s^1$$
  • (a) Electrons with $l = 1$ ($p$-subshells): Only $2p^6$ and $3p^6$ have $l = 1$. Total $= 6 + 6 = \mathbf{12\text{ electrons}}$.
  • (b) Electrons with $m_l = 0$: Every $s$-subshell has $1$ orbital ($m_l = 0$); every $p$-subshell has $1$ orbital ($m_l = 0$) out of $\{-1, 0, +1\}$; every $d$-subshell has $1$ orbital ($m_l = 0$) out of $\{-2, -1, 0, +1, +2\}$.
    • Filled subshells ($1s^2, 2s^2, 2p^6, 3s^2, 3p^6$): $5$ subshells $\times 2e^-$ each $= 10e^-$.
    • Half-filled $3d^5$: By Hund's rule, each of the $5$ $d$-orbitals holds exactly $1$ electron, so the $m_l = 0$ orbital holds $1e^-$.
    • Half-filled $4s^1$: Holds $1e^-$ with $m_l = 0$.
    Total electrons with $m_l = 0$ $= 10 + 1 + 1 = \mathbf{12\text{ electrons}}$.
  • (c) Electrons with $n + l = 4$: Check $(n+l)$ for every occupied subshell: $1s(1), 2s(2), 2p(3), 3s(3), \mathbf{3p(3+1=4)}, 3d(5), \mathbf{4s(4+0=4)}$.
    Only $3p^6$ and $4s^1$ qualify! Total $= 6 + 1 = \mathbf{7\text{ electrons}}$. (Trap: Using the naive $3d^4 4s^2$ configuration wrongly gives $6 + 2 = 8$!)
  • (d) Maximum electrons with $l = 2$ AND $m_s = +\frac{1}{2}$: $l = 2$ corresponds exclusively to $3d^5$. By Hund's rule, all $5$ unpaired electrons in $3d^5$ have parallel spins—either all five are $+\frac{1}{2}$ or all five are $-\frac{1}{2}$. Thus the maximum count is $\mathbf{5\text{ electrons}}$.
30-Second Olympiad Shortcut: The "$m_l = 0$ Subshell Rule"

Every completely filled subshell contributes $2$ electrons with $m_l = 0$, and every exact half-filled subshell ($s^1, p^3, d^5, f^7$) contributes $1$ electron with $m_l = 0$! In $\text{Cr}$: $5$ filled subshells ($1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p$) + $2$ half-filled subshells ($3d^5, 4s^1$) $\implies 5(2) + 2(1) = \mathbf{12}$ in 5 seconds!

Level 3 VSEPR Angles & Dipole Vectors Worked Example 9: Bond-Angle Ordering & The $\text{NH}_3$ vs. $\text{NF}_3$ Dipole Paradox

(a) Use VSEPR Steric Numbers to determine the molecular shape and arrange $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{NH}_4^+$, $\text{NH}_3$, and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ in decreasing order of $\text{H–X–H}$ bond angle.

(b) Fluorine ($\text{EN} = 4.0$) is far more electronegative than hydrogen ($\text{EN} = 2.1$), yet $\text{NH}_3$ ($\mu = 1.47\text{ D}$) has a dipole moment more than $6\times$ larger than $\text{NF}_3$ ($\mu = 0.23\text{ D}$). Explain this vectorially, and predict whether $\text{SF}_6$ and $\text{SO}_2$ are polar or non-polar.

Part (a) — Steric Number & Bond-Angle Compression:

  • $\text{CH}_4$: $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}(4 + 4) = 4$ ($4\text{ bp}, 0\text{ lp}$) → Regular Tetrahedral, $\mathbf{109.5^\circ}$.
  • $\text{NH}_4^+$: $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}(5 + 4 - 1) = 4$ ($4\text{ bp}, 0\text{ lp}$) → Regular Tetrahedral, $\mathbf{109.5^\circ}$.
  • $\text{NH}_3$: $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}(5 + 3) = 4$ ($3\text{ bp}, 1\text{ lp}$) → Trigonal Pyramidal; $1$ lone pair exerts extra $\text{lp–bp}$ repulsion, compressing angle to $\mathbf{107^\circ}$.
  • $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}(6 + 2) = 4$ ($2\text{ bp}, 2\text{ lp}$) → Bent / V-shaped; $2$ lone pairs exert $\text{lp–lp} \gt \text{lp–bp}$ repulsion, compressing angle further to $\mathbf{104.5^\circ}$.

Decreasing Bond-Angle Order: $\mathbf{\text{CH}_4 = \text{NH}_4^+\ (109.5^\circ) \gt \text{NH}_3\ (107^\circ) \gt \text{H}_2\text{O}\ (104.5^\circ)}$.

Part (b) — Vector Resolution of $\text{NH}_3$ vs. $\text{NF}_3$ & Polarity of $\text{SF}_6, \text{SO}_2$:

  • Both $\text{NH}_3$ and $\text{NF}_3$ are trigonal pyramidal with one lone pair on $\text{N}$ pointing upward (giving an upward orbital dipole vector).
  • In $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{EN}(\text{N}) = 3.0 \gt \text{EN}(\text{H}) = 2.1$, so all three $\text{N–H}$ bond dipoles point upward toward $\text{N}$—reinforcing the upward lone-pair dipole (constructive addition $\implies \mu = 1.47\text{ D}$).
  • In $\text{NF}_3$, $\text{EN}(\text{F}) = 4.0 \gt \text{EN}(\text{N}) = 3.0$, so all three $\text{N–F}$ bond dipoles point downward toward $\text{F}$—opposing the upward lone-pair dipole (destructive cancellation $\implies \mu = 0.23\text{ D}$).
  • $\text{SF}_6$: $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}(6 + 6) = 6$ ($6\text{ bp}, 0\text{ lp}$) → Symmetric Octahedral $\implies \mathbf{\mu = 0\text{ (Non-Polar)}}$.
  • $\text{SO}_2$: $\text{SN} = \frac{1}{2}(6 + 0) = 3$ ($2\text{ bp}, 1\text{ lp}$; remember divalent $\text{O}$ contributes $0$ to $M$!) → Bent ($\sim 119^\circ$) $\implies \mathbf{\mu \neq 0\text{ (Polar)}}$.
30-Second Olympiad Shortcut: The "Zero-Lone-Pair Symmetry Test"

Whenever a central atom has ZERO lone pairs ($\text{lp} = 0$) AND all attached atoms are identical (e.g., $\text{CO}_2, \text{BF}_3, \text{CH}_4, \text{CCl}_4, \text{PCl}_5, \text{SF}_6$), the 3D geometry is perfectly symmetric and $\mathbf{\mu = 0}$ (Non-Polar) instantaneously—no vector math required! Conversely, $1$ lone pair ($\text{SO}_2, \text{NH}_3, \text{NF}_3$) ALWAYS breaks symmetry and gives $\mu \neq 0$ (Polar).
