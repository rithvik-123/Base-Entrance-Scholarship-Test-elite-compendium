1. Percentages, Multiplying Factors & Successive Percentage Changes

A percentage is not a standalone number—it is a relative operator acting on a specific base. Virtually every percentage trap in BEST Paper 1 exploits a silent shift of the base between two consecutive steps.

First-Principles Core: The Multiplying Factor (MF) Engine
  • Percentage Change as Scaling: Changing a base value $V_0$ by $+r\%$ multiplies it by the factor $f = 1 + \dfrac{r}{100}$. Decreasing by $r\%$ multiplies it by $f = 1 - \dfrac{r}{100}$. $$V_{\text{new}} = V_0 \left(1 \pm \frac{r}{100}\right)$$
  • Percentage Change vs. Percentage Points (CRITICAL DISTINCTION): If an acid concentration rises from $20\%$ to $25\%$, it increased by $5$ percentage points, NOT by $5\%$. The actual percentage increase is: $$\frac{25 - 20}{20} \times 100\% = 25\%$$
  • Asymmetric Reversal (The $A$-to-$B$ vs. $B$-to-$A$ Identity): If $A$ is $r\%$ more than $B$, then $A = B\left(1 + \dfrac{r}{100}\right)$. How much percent is $B$ less than $A$? Expressing $r\%$ as a fraction $\dfrac{p}{q}$: $$\text{Increase by } \frac{p}{q} \iff \text{Multiply by } \frac{q + p}{q} \iff \text{Reverse by decreasing by } \frac{p}{q + p}$$ $$\text{Decrease by } \frac{p}{q} \iff \text{Multiply by } \frac{q - p}{q} \iff \text{Reverse by increasing by } \frac{p}{q - p}$$ Example: $+25\% = +\dfrac{1}{4}$ is exactly undone by $-\dfrac{1}{4+1} = -\dfrac{1}{5} = -20\%$.

1.1 Successive Percentage Changes & The Product-Invariance Principle

Suppose a quantity $V_0$ undergoes successive changes of $a\%$ and then $b\%$ (signed: positive for increase, negative for decrease). Multiplying the two scaling factors directly gives:

$$V_{\text{final}} = V_0 \left(1 + \frac{a}{100}\right)\left(1 + \frac{b}{100}\right) = V_0 \left(1 + \frac{a + b + \frac{ab}{100}}{100}\right)$$

Hence the net single equivalent percentage change is:

$$r_{\text{eff}} = a + b + \frac{ab}{100}$$

Crucial Commutativity Law: Because multiplication is commutative, $(1 + a/100)(1 + b/100) = (1 + b/100)(1 + a/100)$. The order of successive percentage changes never affects the final value! Moreover, for $n$ successive changes $r_1\%, r_2\%, \dots, r_n\%$, never iterate the $a + b + ab/100$ formula repeatedly—simply multiply the fractional multiplying factors:

$$\text{Net MF} = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \left(1 + \frac{r_i}{100}\right)$$

Product-Invariance Rule ($E = P \times Q$): Whenever a quantity $E$ is a product of two variables (Expenditure $=$ Price $\times$ Consumption; Area $=$ Length $\times$ Breadth; Revenue $=$ Ticket Price $\times$ Attendance), their multiplying factors multiply directly:

$$\text{MF}_E = \text{MF}_P \times \text{MF}_Q \implies \text{MF}_Q = \frac{\text{MF}_E}{\text{MF}_P}$$

2. Profit, Loss & Discount Architecture (The CP–SP–MP Invariant)

Every commercial transaction revolves around three prices: Cost Price ($\text{CP}$), Marked/List Price ($\text{MP}$), and Selling Price ($\text{SP}$). The golden rule of bases is non-negotiable:

  • Profit $\%$ ($P\%$) and Loss $\%$ ($L\%$) are always calculated on $\text{CP}$ (unless a problem explicitly says “profit on SP”): $\text{SP} = \text{CP}\left(1 + \dfrac{P}{100}\right)$ or $\text{CP}\left(1 - \dfrac{L}{100}\right)$.
  • Markup $\%$ ($M\%$) is calculated on $\text{CP}$: $\text{MP} = \text{CP}\left(1 + \dfrac{M}{100}\right)$.
  • Discount $\%$ ($D\%$) is always calculated on $\text{MP}$: $\text{SP} = \text{MP}\left(1 - \dfrac{D}{100}\right)$.
Figure 2.1: Equating the two paths from $\text{CP}$ to $\text{SP}$ eliminates $\text{SP}$ immediately, yielding the Master Ratio Bridge between $\text{MP}$ and $\text{CP}$.
High-Frequency BEST Profit/Loss/Discount Traps (Derived from First Principles)
  1. The Master Bridge Identity: Since $\text{SP} = \text{CP}\left(\dfrac{100 + P}{100}\right) = \text{MP}\left(\dfrac{100 - D}{100}\right)$, dividing directly gives: $$\frac{\text{MP}}{\text{CP}} = \frac{100 + P}{100 - D} \qquad (\text{replace } +P \text{ with } -L \text{ for a loss})$$ This single ratio solves 80% of markup-discount problems in under 15 seconds without introducing intermediate variables.
  2. Same SP, Equal Profit% and Loss% ($r\%$) Trap: Suppose two items are sold at the exact same selling price $S$, one at $+r\%$ profit and the other at $-r\%$ loss. Their cost prices are NOT equal: $$\text{CP}_1 = \frac{S}{1 + r/100}, \qquad \text{CP}_2 = \frac{S}{1 - r/100}$$ $$\text{Total CP} = S \left(\frac{1}{1 + r/100} + \frac{1}{1 - r/100}\right) = \frac{2S}{1 - (r/100)^2}$$ Since $\text{Total SP} = 2S$, we get $\dfrac{\text{Total SP}}{\text{Total CP}} = 1 - \left(\dfrac{r}{100}\right)^2$. Therefore, the transaction ALWAYS results in a net loss of: $$\text{Net Loss } \% = \frac{r^2}{100}\%, \qquad \text{Exact Loss in Rupees} = \frac{2S}{\left(\frac{100}{r}\right)^2 - 1}$$ Contrast: If the two items have the same CP (one at $+r\%$, one at $-r\%$), the gains and losses cancel symmetrically: $0\%$ net profit/loss.
  3. “Buy $x$, Get $y$ Free” Scheme: The customer walks out with $x + y$ items (total marked value $(x+y)\cdot\text{MP}$) while paying for only $x$ items (value $x\cdot\text{MP}$). Thus $y$ items are given as discount out of $x + y$ total items: $$\text{Effective Discount } \% = \frac{y}{x + y} \times 100\%, \qquad \text{Discount MF} = \frac{x}{x + y}$$ If an additional cash discount of $D\%$ is offered on the billed amount, multiply the factors: $\text{Total Discount MF} = \left(\dfrac{x}{x+y}\right)\left(1 - \dfrac{D}{100}\right)$.
  4. Profit Calculated on SP vs. CP Conversion: If profit is stated as $p_s = \dfrac{\text{SP} - \text{CP}}{\text{SP}}$ (fraction of SP), then $\text{CP} = (1 - p_s)\text{SP}$, so the true profit fraction on CP is: $$p_c = \frac{p_s}{1 - p_s} \qquad \left(\text{Example: } 20\% = \frac{1}{5} \text{ on SP} \iff \frac{1}{5-1} = \frac{1}{4} = 25\% \text{ on CP}\right)$$

3. Dishonest Dealer & False Weight Traps

Why do students constantly miscalculate dishonest-dealer problems? Because they divide the stolen weight by the claimed weight ($1000\text{ g}$) instead of the actually delivered weight! Always anchor to one unbreakable physical principle:

The Cash-In / Goods-Out Universal Principle

Profit percentage ALWAYS equals $\dfrac{\text{Money Received} - \text{True Cost of Goods Delivered}}{\text{True Cost of Goods Delivered}} \times 100\%$. Equivalently, track the ratio:

$$\frac{\text{SP}_{\text{effective}}}{\text{CP}_{\text{effective}}} = \frac{\text{Money Collected per Nominal Unit}}{\text{True Cost of Physical Mass Actually Handed Over}}$$

Single-Step Selling False Weight: If a dealer promises to sell at Cost Price but uses a false weight of $W_{\text{true}}$ grams instead of $W_{\text{claimed}}$ grams ($W_{\text{true}} \lt W_{\text{claimed}}$):

$$\frac{\text{SP}}{\text{CP}} = \frac{W_{\text{claimed}}}{W_{\text{true}}} \implies \text{Profit } \% = \frac{W_{\text{claimed}} - W_{\text{true}}}{W_{\text{true}}} \times 100\%$$

Classic Trap Check: Giving $800\text{ g}$ instead of $1000\text{ g}$ is a profit of $\dfrac{200}{800} = \mathbf{25\%}$, NOT $20\%$!

3.1 Multi-Layer Cheating: The Multiplying-Factor Chain

In BEST Level 2/3, a dishonest merchant combines up to four simultaneous manipulations: (1) cheating the wholesaler while buying, (2) marking up the price, (3) giving a discount, and (4) using a false weight while selling. Every single manipulation is simply a multiplicative factor on $\dfrac{\text{SP}}{\text{CP}}$:

$$\frac{\text{SP}_{\text{net}}}{\text{CP}_{\text{net}}} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{W_{\text{received from wholesaler}}}{W_{\text{paid to wholesaler}}}\right)}_{\text{Buying Weight Fraud}} \times \underbrace{\left(1 + \frac{M}{100}\right)}_{\text{Markup}} \times \underbrace{\left(1 - \frac{D}{100}\right)}_{\text{Discount}} \times \underbrace{\left(\frac{W_{\text{billed to customer}}}{W_{\text{delivered to customer}}}\right)}_{\text{Selling Weight Fraud}}$$
Warning: “Cheats by $x\%$ While Buying and $x\%$ While Selling” Ambiguity

In Indian competitive exams, read the exact phrasing of buying fraud carefully!

  • Case A (Quantity fraud — standard): Takes $(100 + x)\%$ quantity for the price of $100\%$ while buying, and gives $(100 - x)\%$ quantity for the price of $100\%$ while selling: $$\frac{\text{SP}}{\text{CP}} = \frac{100 + x}{100} \times \frac{100}{100 - x} = \frac{100 + x}{100 - x} \implies \text{Profit } \% = \frac{2x}{100 - x} \times 100\%$$ For $x = 10\%$, $\text{Profit } \% = \dfrac{20}{90} \times 100\% = 22.\overline{2}\%$ (NOT $21\%$!).
  • Case B (Pure price fraud): Underpays by $x\%$ (pays $100-x$ for $100$ worth) and overcharges by $x\%$ (collects $100+x$ for $100$ worth) — this yields the same ratio $\dfrac{100+x}{100-x}$!
  • Case C (Successive markup interpretation): Only if a problem explicitly states “earns $x\%$ profit while buying and $x\%$ profit while selling” does the factor equal $(1 + x/100)^2 = 1 + \frac{2x + x^2/100}{100}$ ($21\%$ for $x = 10\%$). Always verify whether $x\%$ acts on weight or profit margin.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Faulty Beam Balance (Unequal Arms & Asymmetric Pans)

Suppose a traditional two-pan balance has unequal lever arms $L_1 \neq L_2$ (with massless pans). A body of true mass $W$ balances standard weight $W_1$ when placed on pan $1$ (arm $L_1$), and balances standard weight $W_2$ when placed on pan $2$ (arm $L_2$). By rotational torque equilibrium ($\tau_{\text{left}} = \tau_{\text{right}}$):

$$W \cdot L_1 = W_1 \cdot L_2 \quad \text{and} \quad W_2 \cdot L_1 = W \cdot L_2$$

Multiplying the two equations eliminates $L_1 L_2$: $W^2 = W_1 W_2$, giving the Geometric Mean Law:

$$W = \sqrt{W_1 W_2}, \qquad \text{and Arm Ratio } \frac{L_1}{L_2} = \sqrt{\frac{W_1}{W_2}}$$

Contrast — Equal Arms, Unequal Pan Masses ($m_1 \neq m_2$): If $L_1 = L_2$ but pan $1$ is heavier by $\Delta m = m_1 - m_2$, then $W + \Delta m = W_1$ and $W - \Delta m = W_2$, yielding the Arithmetic Mean Law: $W = \dfrac{W_1 + W_2}{2}$.

4. Ratios, Proportions, Partnerships & Joint Variation

A ratio $a : b$ compares two quantities of the same dimension. Never equate two ratios directly unless their shared connecting variable is scaled to the same numerical unit.

Algebraic Laws of Proportion & Olympiad Ratio Theorems
  • Merging Ratios ($A:B$ and $B:C$): Scale both ratios so the common middle term $B$ equals $\operatorname{LCM}(b_1, b_2)$. Example: $A:B = 2:3$ and $B:C = 4:5 \implies B$ scaled to $12 \implies A:B:C = 8:12:15$.
  • Componendo & Dividendo: If $\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}$ ($a \neq b, c \neq d$), then: $$\frac{a + b}{a - b} = \frac{c + d}{c - d}$$ Applying C&D twice restores $\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}$. This eliminates messy cross-multiplication in radical and rational equations.
  • Theorem of Equal Ratios (Addendo): If $\dfrac{a_1}{b_1} = \dfrac{a_2}{b_2} = \dots = \dfrac{a_n}{b_n} = k$, then for any constants $p_1, \dots, p_n$ with $\sum p_i b_i \neq 0$: $$k = \frac{p_1 a_1 + p_2 a_2 + \dots + p_n a_n}{p_1 b_1 + p_2 b_2 + \dots + p_n b_n}$$ Olympiad Caution: Always check the boundary case where the denominator sum $\sum p_i b_i = 0$! For example, if $\dfrac{x}{y+z} = \dfrac{y}{z+x} = \dfrac{z}{x+y} = k$, adding numerators and denominators gives $k = \dfrac{x+y+z}{2(x+y+z)} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ ONLY if $x + y + z \neq 0$; if $x + y + z = 0$, then $y + z = -x \implies k = -1$!
  • Constant-Difference & Cross-Product Shift: If two numbers in ratio $a:b$ both increase by the same constant $+k$ to reach ratio $c:d$, their difference remains invariant. Scale the ratios so $|a - b| = |c - d|$; then $1\text{ ratio unit} = \dfrac{k}{c - a}$.
  • Partnerships (Time-Weighted Capital): Profit is shared strictly in the ratio of Capital $\times$ Time: $$\text{Profit}_i \propto \sum_{j} C_{i,j} \cdot t_{i,j}$$ If a working partner receives a fixed salary or $s\%$ management share first, deduct that from Total Profit before splitting the remaining $(100 - s)\%$ in the capital-time ratio.

5. Weighted Averages & The Alligation Cross Method

The “Alligation Cross” is not magic—it is a geometric rearrangement of the Weighted Average Equation (and physically identical to finding the center of mass on a balance beam).

Suppose quantity $w_1$ with unit value (concentration, price, profit$\%$, or speed) $C_1$ is mixed with quantity $w_2$ having unit value $C_2$ ($C_1 \lt C_2$) to produce a mixture of mean value $C_m$ ($C_1 \lt C_m \lt C_2$). Conservation of total value gives:

$$w_1 C_1 + w_2 C_2 = (w_1 + w_2) C_m$$

Rearranging terms so $w_1$ sits on one side and $w_2$ on the other:

$$w_1 (C_m - C_1) = w_2 (C_2 - C_m) \implies \frac{w_1}{w_2} = \frac{C_2 - C_m}{C_m - C_1}$$

Notice the inverse lever law: the heavier quantity pulls the mean $C_m$ closer to itself, so the ratio of weights $w_1 : w_2$ equals the reverse ratio of distances from the mean!

Critical Alligation Trap: What Exactly Is the Bottom Ratio $w_1 : w_2$?

The bottom ratio of an Alligation Cross ALWAYS gives the ratio of the denominators (bases) of the rates you placed on top!

  • Top = Cost per kg (₹/kg) $\implies$ Bottom = ratio of weights (kg).
  • Top = Profit $\%$ (Profit / CP) $\implies$ Bottom = ratio of Cost Prices (CP), NOT Selling Prices!
  • Top = Speed (km/hr) $\implies$ Bottom = ratio of Times (hr), NOT Distances!
  • Top = Concentration (Solute / Total Volume) $\implies$ Bottom = ratio of Total Mixture Volumes.
Never mix an SP on the middle node with CPs on the top nodes—convert the mixture’s SP to its true CP first using $\text{CP}_m = \dfrac{\text{SP}_m}{1 + P/100}$.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Three-Component Alligation & Deviation Balance

When mixing three ingredients with values $C_1 \lt C_2 \lt C_3$ to reach target mean $C_m$, the solution is no longer a single unique ratio—it is an infinite family of ratios! Use the Zero-Deviation Balance:

$$(C_1 - C_m)w_1 + (C_2 - C_m)w_2 + (C_3 - C_m)w_3 = 0$$

To construct one valid ratio quickly: pair every ingredient below $C_m$ with an ingredient above $C_m$ via two separate 2-component alligations, then add any positive linear combination of those two solution vectors. When integer constraints ($w_1, w_2, w_3 \in \mathbb{Z}^+$) and a fixed total weight $w_1 + w_2 + w_3 = W$ are imposed (classic IOQM/BEST Level 3!), solve the resulting 2-equation Diophantine system to find exact min/max bounds (see Worked Example 8).

6. Mixtures & The Repeated Replacement Formula

Consider a vessel of total volume $V$ initially containing a solution of concentration $C_0$ of a tracked solute $A$ (for pure solute $A$, $C_0 = 1$ or $100\%$). In each operation, we withdraw $x$ litres of the well-mixed solution ($0 \lt x \lt V$) and replace it with $x$ litres of pure diluent (containing $0\%$ of solute $A$).

6.1 First-Principles Derivation of $C_{\text{final}} = C_0\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{V}\right)^n$

Track the amount (or concentration) of solute $A$ remaining inside the vessel across each cycle:

  • Start (Step $0$): Solute volume $= V C_0$; concentration $= C_0$.
  • After Step $1$: Removing $x$ litres of uniform mixture removes a fraction $\dfrac{x}{V}$ of the solute, leaving a fraction $\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{V}\right)$ untouched. Replacing with $x$ litres of pure diluent restores the total volume to $V$ without adding any solute $A$. Thus: $$C_1 = C_0 \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)$$
  • After Step $2$: Removing $x$ litres again scales the remaining solute by the exact same retention factor $\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{V}\right)$: $$C_2 = C_1 \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right) = C_0 \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)^2$$
  • After $n$ Identical Operations (by Induction): $$C_{\text{final}} = C_0 \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)^n \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad Q_{\text{final}} = Q_0 \left(1 - \frac{x}{V}\right)^n$$
Figure 2.3: Replacing $25\%$ of a tank with pure water $3$ times leaves $(3/4)^3 = 27/64 \approx 42.2\%$ solute, never $100\% - 75\% = 25\%$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Three Generalized Replacement Laws
  1. Variable Draw Volumes (Constant Tank Volume $V$): If you replace $x_1$, then $x_2$, then $\dots$, $x_n$ litres with pure diluent: $$C_{\text{final}} = C_0 \left(1 - \frac{x_1}{V}\right)\left(1 - \frac{x_2}{V}\right)\cdots\left(1 - \frac{x_n}{V}\right)$$
  2. Unequal Withdrawal & Refill Volumes ($x_i$ out, $y_i$ diluent in): When $x_i$ is removed from current volume $V_{i-1}$, the solute concentration does not change; only the subsequent addition of $y_i$ pure diluent dilutes the remaining volume $(V_{i-1} - x_i)$ up to $V_i = V_{i-1} - x_i + y_i$. Therefore, at each step: $$C_i = C_{i-1} \left(\frac{V_{i-1} - x_i}{V_{i-1} - x_i + y_i}\right)$$ Notice that when $y_i = x_i$, this collapses smoothly to $C_i = C_{i-1}\left(1 - \dfrac{x_i}{V}\right)$!
  3. The Shifted Excess-Concentration Invariant (Replacing with Non-Zero Concentration $C_{\text{rep}}$): What if you draw out $x_i$ litres and replace it NOT with pure water ($0\%$), but with a solution of concentration $C_{\text{rep}}$? Subtract $C_{\text{rep}}$ from the concentration! The excess concentration $\Delta C = C - C_{\text{rep}}$ decays to zero by the exact same geometric formula: $$C_{\text{final}} - C_{\text{rep}} = (C_0 - C_{\text{rep}}) \prod_{i=1}^{n} \left(1 - \frac{x_i}{V}\right)$$ Proof: After removing $x$ and adding $x$ of $C_{\text{rep}}$, $C_1 = C_0\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{V}\right) + C_{\text{rep}}\left(\dfrac{x}{V}\right)$. Subtracting $C_{\text{rep}}$ from both sides yields $C_1 - C_{\text{rep}} = (C_0 - C_{\text{rep}})\left(1 - \dfrac{x}{V}\right)$. By induction, the formula holds for all $n$!

Level 1 · Warmup NAT (000–999) Worked Example 1: Successive Price Hikes & Expenditure-Capped Consumption

The price of high-grade coffee beans increases by $25\%$ in Quarter 1, and then increases by a further $20\%$ in Quarter 2. A café wants to restrict its total expenditure on coffee beans to at most a $20\%$ increase over its original budget. By what exact percentage must the café reduce its consumption of coffee beans?

Step 1 (Compute the Combined Price Multiplying Factor):

$$\text{MF}_P = \left(1 + \frac{25}{100}\right)\left(1 + \frac{20}{100}\right) = \frac{5}{4} \times \frac{6}{5} = \frac{3}{2} = 1.50$$

(Equivalently, the effective price increase is $25 + 20 + \dfrac{25 \times 20}{100} = 50\%$.)

Step 2 (Apply the Product-Invariance Equation $\text{MF}_E = \text{MF}_P \times \text{MF}_Q$): The maximum allowed expenditure factor is $\text{MF}_E = 1 + \dfrac{20}{100} = 1.20$. Solving for the consumption factor $\text{MF}_Q$:

$$\text{MF}_Q = \frac{\text{MF}_E}{\text{MF}_P} = \frac{1.20}{1.50} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.80$$

Step 3 (Convert to Percentage Reduction): A multiplying factor of $0.80$ corresponds to a reduction of $(1 - 0.80) \times 100\% = \mathbf{20}\%$.

Verification: With original price ₹$100$ and consumption $100\text{ kg}$ (budget ₹$10{,}000$), new price is ₹$150$ and new consumption is $80\text{ kg}$, giving expenditure $150 \times 80 = \text{₹}12{,}000$ (exact $+20\%$).

Level 1 · Warmup Single-Correct MCQ Worked Example 2: Marked Price, Discount & Net Profit%

A merchant marks his merchandise at $40\%$ above cost price and allows a discount of $15\%$ on the marked price. What is his exact profit percentage?

(A) $19\%$    (B) $21\%$    (C) $25\%$    (D) $26\%$

Step 1 (Direct Multiplying Factor Path $\text{CP} \to \text{MP} \to \text{SP}$): Let $\text{CP} = 100$. Marking up by $40\%$ gives $\text{MP} = 140$. Offering a $15\%$ discount on $\text{MP}$ gives:

$$\text{SP} = 140 \times \left(1 - \frac{15}{100}\right) = 140 \times 0.85 = 119$$

Step 2 (Compute Profit%): Profit $= 119 - 100 = 19$ on $\text{CP} = 100$, so Profit $\% = 19\%$.

Formula Check: $P = M - D - \dfrac{MD}{100} = 40 - 15 - \dfrac{40 \times 15}{100} = 25 - 6 = 19\%$. Correct Option: (A).

Level 1 · Warmup NAT (000–999) Worked Example 3: Mixing Two Solutions via Alligation Cross

Two vessels $A$ and $B$ contain milk-water mixtures with milk concentrations of $40\%$ and $75\%$ respectively. What volume (in litres) must be drawn from vessel $A$ to mix with $28$ litres from vessel $B$ so that the resulting mixture contains exactly $60\%$ milk?

Step 1 (Apply the Alligation Cross on Milk Concentration): Place $C_A = 40\%$ and $C_B = 75\%$ on the top nodes and $C_m = 60\%$ at the center:

$$\frac{V_A}{V_B} = \frac{C_B - C_m}{C_m - C_A} = \frac{75 - 60}{60 - 40} = \frac{15}{20} = \frac{3}{4}$$

Step 2 (Scale Using $V_B = 28\text{ L}$):

$$V_A = \frac{3}{4} \times 28 = \mathbf{21}\text{ L}$$

Verification: Pure milk from $A = 0.40(21) = 8.4\text{ L}$; pure milk from $B = 0.75(28) = 21\text{ L}$. Total milk $= 29.4\text{ L}$ in $21 + 28 = 49\text{ L}$ total mixture $\implies \dfrac{29.4}{49} = 0.60 = 60\%$.

Level 2 · Exam-Real NAT (000–999) Worked Example 4: Four-Layer Dishonest Grain Trader

A dishonest grain trader cheats his wholesaler by taking $1200\text{ g}$ while paying the nominal price of only $1000\text{ g}$. When selling to customers, he marks up the nominal price per kg by $20\%$, announces a $10\%$ “festival discount,” and uses a rigged weight that delivers only $900\text{ g}$ instead of $1000\text{ g}$. Find his exact overall profit percentage.

Step 1 (First-Principles Cash-In / Goods-Out Method): Let the nominal wholesale price of $1000\text{ g}$ be ₹$1000$ (₹$1$ per nominal gram).

  • True Cost per Gram: Because he pays ₹$1000$ and receives $1200\text{ g}$, his true cost per physical gram is $\dfrac{1000}{1200} = \text{₹}\dfrac{5}{6}\text{/g}$.
  • Money Received per Billed kg: Marking up ₹$1000$ by $20\%$ yields ₹$1200$; applying a $10\%$ discount gives $\text{SP}_{\text{collected}} = 1200 \times 0.90 = \text{₹}1080$.
  • True Cost of Goods Actually Delivered: He hands over only $900\text{ g}$, whose true cost to him is $900 \times \dfrac{5}{6} = \text{₹}750$.

Step 2 (Calculate Overall Profit%):

$$\text{Profit } \% = \frac{1080 - 750}{750} \times 100\% = \frac{330}{750} \times 100\% = \frac{11}{25} \times 100\% = \mathbf{44}\%$$

Multiplying-Factor Chain Verification: $\dfrac{\text{SP}}{\text{CP}} = \left(\dfrac{1200}{1000}\right)\left(\dfrac{6}{5}\right)\left(\dfrac{9}{10}\right)\left(\dfrac{1000}{900}\right) = \dfrac{6}{5} \times \dfrac{6}{5} \times \dfrac{9}{10} \times \dfrac{10}{9} = \dfrac{36}{25} = 1.44 \implies \mathbf{44}\%$.

Level 2 · Exam-Real Single-Correct MCQ Worked Example 5: Same-SP Rupee Loss Trap & Buy-$x$-Get-$y$-Free Margin

A shopkeeper sells two electronics items at the exact same selling price of ₹$9900$ each, earning a $10\%$ profit on the first and suffering a $10\%$ loss on the second. Independently, on a promotional bundle, he offers “Buy $5$, Get $3$ Free” plus an additional $20\%$ cash discount on the billed amount, yet still realizes a net profit of $25\%$ on the bundle. What is the ratio $\text{MP} : \text{CP}$ of a promotional item, and what is the net rupee loss on the first two items combined?

(A) $5:2$; ₹$200$ loss   (B) $5:2$; ₹$198$ loss   (C) $2:1$; ₹$200$ loss   (D) $8:3$; ₹$198$ loss

Step 1 (Net Rupee Loss on the First Two Items): Compute each cost price explicitly:

$$\text{CP}_1 = \frac{9900}{1.10} = \text{₹}9000, \qquad \text{CP}_2 = \frac{9900}{0.90} = \text{₹}11000$$ $$\text{Total CP} = 9000 + 11000 = \text{₹}20000, \qquad \text{Total SP} = 9900 + 9900 = \text{₹}19800$$ $$\text{Net Loss} = 20000 - 19800 = \text{₹}200$$

Trap Watch: The net loss percentage is $\dfrac{10^2}{100} = 1\%$ of CP (not $1\%$ of SP $= \text{₹}198$!), so $\text{SP}_{\text{total}}$ represents $99\%$ of $\text{CP}_{\text{total}}$, giving $\text{Loss} = \dfrac{1}{99} \times 19800 = \text{₹}200$.

Step 2 ($\text{MP} : \text{CP}$ Ratio for the Promotional Bundle): Under “Buy $5$, Get $3$ Free,” the customer receives $8$ items (costing the shopkeeper $8\cdot\text{CP}$) and pays for $5$ items with an extra $20\%$ discount:

$$\text{SP}_{\text{bundle}} = 5 \cdot \text{MP} \times 0.80 = 4 \cdot \text{MP}$$

Since net profit is $25\%$ on the $8$ delivered items:

$$\text{SP}_{\text{bundle}} = 1.25 \times (8 \cdot \text{CP}) = 10 \cdot \text{CP}$$

Equating both expressions gives $4 \cdot \text{MP} = 10 \cdot \text{CP} \implies \dfrac{\text{MP}}{\text{CP}} = \dfrac{10}{4} = \dfrac{5}{2}$ (i.e., $5:2$).

Correct Option: (A).

Level 2 · Exam-Real NAT (000–999) Worked Example 6: Inverting Repeated Replacement to Find Vessel Capacity

A tank is completely filled with pure ethanol. $15$ litres of ethanol are drawn out and replaced with $15$ litres of pure water. This exact replacement process is performed two more times. After the final operation, the ratio of ethanol to water in the tank is $27 : 37$. Find the total capacity of the tank (in litres).

Step 1 (Count Total Operations Carefully): One initial operation plus “two more times” equals $n = 1 + 2 = 3$ total operations. (Using $n = 2$ is a notorious BEST trap!)

Step 2 (Convert Ratio to Final Ethanol Concentration): With $\text{Ethanol} : \text{Water} = 27 : 37$, the fraction of ethanol remaining out of the total tank volume is:

$$\frac{C_{\text{final}}}{C_0} = \frac{27}{27 + 37} = \frac{27}{64}$$

Step 3 (Solve the Repeated Replacement Equation): Let the tank capacity be $V$ litres:

$$\left(1 - \frac{15}{V}\right)^3 = \frac{27}{64} = \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^3$$

Taking the real cube root of both sides:

$$1 - \frac{15}{V} = \frac{3}{4} \implies \frac{15}{V} = \frac{1}{4} \implies V = \mathbf{60}\text{ L}$$

Level 3 · Rank-Booster NAT (000–999) Worked Example 7: Variable Replacement with Non-Zero Concentration + Pure Dilution

A laboratory vat contains $100$ litres of an $80\%$ acid solution (rest water). In Step 1, $20$ litres of the solution are removed and replaced with $20$ litres of a $30\%$ acid solution. In Step 2, $25$ litres of the resulting mixture are removed and replaced with $25$ litres of the same $30\%$ acid solution. Finally, in Step 3, $20$ litres of pure water ($0\%$ acid) are poured into the vat without removing any liquid. Find the exact final percentage concentration of acid in the vat.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Step-by-Step Solute Tracking):

  • Initial State: Total volume $= 100\text{ L}$; pure acid $= 80\% \text{ of } 100 = 80\text{ L}$.
  • Step 1 (Remove $20\text{ L}$, Add $20\text{ L}$ of $30\%$ acid): Removing $20\text{ L}$ removes $\dfrac{20}{100} = \dfrac{1}{5}$ of the acid, leaving $80 \times \dfrac{4}{5} = 64\text{ L}$ of acid. Adding $20\text{ L}$ of $30\%$ acid contributes $20 \times 0.30 = 6\text{ L}$ of acid. $$\text{Acid after Step 1} = 64 + 6 = 70\text{ L} \quad (\text{in } 100\text{ L} \implies 70\%)$$
  • Step 2 (Remove $25\text{ L}$, Add $25\text{ L}$ of $30\%$ acid): Removing $25\text{ L}$ removes $\dfrac{25}{100} = \dfrac{1}{4}$ of the acid, leaving $70 \times \dfrac{3}{4} = 52.5\text{ L}$ of acid. Adding $25\text{ L}$ of $30\%$ acid contributes $25 \times 0.30 = 7.5\text{ L}$ of acid. $$\text{Acid after Step 2} = 52.5 + 7.5 = 60\text{ L} \quad (\text{in } 100\text{ L} \implies 60\%)$$
  • Step 3 (Add $20\text{ L}$ Pure Water): Pure acid remains $60\text{ L}$, while total volume grows to $100 + 20 = 120\text{ L}$. $$\text{Final Concentration} = \frac{60}{120} \times 100\% = \mathbf{50}\%$$
30-Second Exam Shortcut (Shifted Excess-Concentration Invariant)

1. Track excess concentration above the replacement solution ($C_{\text{rep}} = 30\%$): initially $\Delta C_0 = 80\% - 30\% = 50\%$.
2. After replacing $20/100 = 1/5$ and then $25/100 = 1/4$: $\Delta C_2 = 50\% \times \dfrac{4}{5} \times \dfrac{3}{4} = 50\% \times \dfrac{3}{5} = 30\% \implies C_2 = 30\% + 30\% = 60\%$.
3. Diluting $100\text{ L} \to 120\text{ L}$ with pure water scales concentration by $\dfrac{100}{120} = \dfrac{5}{6} \implies C_{\text{final}} = 60\% \times \dfrac{5}{6} = \mathbf{50}\%$!

Level 3 · Rank-Booster NAT (000–999) Worked Example 8: Three-Component Integer Alligation & Diophantine Bounds

A tea blender mixes three varieties of tea costing ₹$120/\text{kg}$, ₹$150/\text{kg}$, and ₹$210/\text{kg}$ in positive integer kilogram quantities $x, y,$ and $z$ respectively, such that the total weight of the blend is $x + y + z = 24\text{ kg}$ and the exact average cost of the blend is ₹$170/\text{kg}$. Let $x_{\max}$ be the maximum possible value of $x$ and $z_{\min}$ be the minimum possible value of $z$. Find the exact value of $10 x_{\max} + z_{\min}$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 (Write the Weight & Cost Conservation Equations):

$$x + y + z = 24 \qquad \text{--- (1)}$$ $$120x + 150y + 210z = 170 \times 24 = 4080 \qquad \text{--- (2)}$$

Divide Equation (2) by $10$: $12x + 15y + 21z = 408$, and divide by $3$: $4x + 5y + 7z = 136$.

Step 2 (Eliminate $y$ to Parameterize in Terms of $x$): Multiply Equation (1) by $5$ ($5x + 5y + 5z = 120$) and subtract from $4x + 5y + 7z = 136$:

$$-x + 2z = 16 \implies z = \frac{x + 16}{2}$$

Substitute $z = \dfrac{x + 16}{2}$ back into Equation (1) to express $y$ in terms of $x$:

$$y = 24 - x - \frac{x + 16}{2} = 16 - \frac{3x}{2}$$

Step 3 (Enforce Positive Integer Constraints): For $y$ and $z$ to be integers, $x$ must be even; let $x = 2t$ ($t \in \mathbb{Z}^+$). Then $z = t + 8$ and $y = 16 - 3t$. Applying $x \ge 1, y \ge 1, z \ge 1$:

$$t \ge 1 \quad \text{and} \quad 16 - 3t \ge 1 \implies 3t \le 15 \implies t \le 5$$

Hence $t \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ gives all $5$ valid integer blends:

  • Maximum $x$ occurs at $t = 5$: $x_{\max} = 2(5) = 10$ (blend $(10, 1, 13)$, cost $1200 + 150 + 2730 = 4080$).
  • Minimum $z$ occurs at $t = 1$: $z_{\min} = 1 + 8 = 9$ (blend $(2, 13, 9)$, cost $240 + 1950 + 1890 = 4080$).

Step 4 (Final Calculation): $10 x_{\max} + z_{\min} = 10(10) + 9 = \mathbf{109}$.

30-Second Exam Shortcut (Deviation Torque Balance)

1. Subtract the target mean $170$ from each cost: deviations are $-50, -20, +40$. Cancel $10$: $-5x - 2y + 4z = 0 \implies 5x + 2y = 4z$.
2. Plug $y = 24 - x - z$ straight in: $3x + 48 = 6z \implies z = 8 + x/2$ and $y = 16 - 3x/2$.
3. Immediately read off even $x \in [2, 10] \implies x_{\max} = 10$ and $z_{\min} = 8 + 2/2 = 9 \implies 10(10) + 9 = \mathbf{109}$.

Level 3 · Rank-Booster NAT (000–999) Worked Example 9: Unequal-Arm Beam Balance + Markup-Discount Fraud

A grocer owns an unequal-arm beam balance with arm lengths $L_1 \neq L_2$. When a sealed metal block of true mass $W$ grams is placed on the left pan (arm $L_1$), it balances $640\text{ g}$ of standard weights on the right pan; when placed on the right pan (arm $L_2$), it balances $1000\text{ g}$ of standard weights on the left pan. Later, while selling sugar, the grocer marks up the price per kg by $20\%$ above cost price, gives a $10\%$ discount on the marked price, and always places the customer’s sugar on the longer-arm pan to maximize profit. If his overall profit percentage on selling sugar is $P\%$, find the exact value of $W + P$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 (Find True Mass $W$ and Arm Ratio via Torque Equilibrium): Balancing torques ($\text{Mass} \times \text{Arm Length}$) for both weighings of the metal block:

$$W \cdot L_1 = 640 \cdot L_2 \quad \text{and} \quad 1000 \cdot L_1 = W \cdot L_2$$

From the first equation $\dfrac{L_1}{L_2} = \dfrac{640}{W}$; from the second $\dfrac{L_1}{L_2} = \dfrac{W}{1000}$. Equating both ratios:

$$W^2 = 640 \times 1000 = 640000 \implies W = 800\text{ g}$$

Substituting $W = 800$ gives the arm ratio $\dfrac{L_1}{L_2} = \dfrac{640}{800} = \dfrac{4}{5}$, so the right arm $L_2$ is longer ($L_2 = \dfrac{5}{4} L_1$).

Step 2 (Determine Physical Sugar Mass Delivered per Billed kg): When a customer asks for $1000\text{ g}$ of sugar, the grocer places the $1000\text{ g}$ standard weight on the shorter arm $L_1$ and sugar of true mass $M$ on the longer arm $L_2$:

$$1000 \cdot L_1 = M \cdot L_2 \implies M = 1000 \cdot \frac{L_1}{L_2} = 1000 \times \frac{4}{5} = 800\text{ g}$$

Thus for every $1000\text{ g}$ billed, the grocer parts with only $800\text{ g}$ of sugar.

Step 3 (Compute Overall Profit Percentage $P\%$): Let true cost of $1000\text{ g}$ of sugar be ₹$1000$.

  • Money collected per billed kg $= 1000 \times 1.20 \times 0.90 = \text{₹}1080$.
  • True cost of the $800\text{ g}$ actually delivered $= \text{₹}800$.
$$P = \frac{1080 - 800}{800} \times 100 = \frac{280}{800} \times 100 = 35\%$$

Step 4 (Final Answer): $W + P = 800 + 35 = \mathbf{835}$.

30-Second Exam Shortcut (Geometric Mean + MF Chain)

1. Instantly read $W = \sqrt{640 \times 1000} = \mathbf{800\text{ g}}$ and arm ratio $\sqrt{1000/640} = \sqrt{25/16} = \dfrac{5}{4}$.
2. Placing goods on the longer arm gives a weight-cheat multiplying factor equal to the arm ratio $\dfrac{5}{4}$ directly!
3. Chain all three factors: $\dfrac{\text{SP}}{\text{CP}} = \dfrac{5}{4} \times \dfrac{6}{5} \times \dfrac{9}{10} = \dfrac{27}{20} = 1.35 \implies P = \mathbf{35} \implies W + P = \mathbf{835}$.
