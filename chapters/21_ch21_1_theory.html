21.1 Laws of Chemical Combination & Vapour Density

Before the mole existed, quantitative chemistry rested on five empirical laws governing how matter combines by mass and by gaseous volume. BEST Paper 2 tests both the exact statements and the subtle boundary conditions / exceptions to each law.

Law & Scientist Exact Quantitative Statement Illustrative Verification Olympiad Nuance / Exceptions
Law of Conservation of Mass
(Lavoisier, 1789)		 In a closed system undergoing a chemical or physical change, total mass of reactants equals total mass of products: $\sum m_{\text{reactants}} = \sum m_{\text{products}}$. $\text{AgNO}_3(aq) + \text{NaCl}(aq) \rightarrow \text{AgCl}(s) + \text{NaNO}_3(aq)$ in a sealed Landolt tube shows zero mass change. Strictly holds for chemical reactions where energy change $\Delta E$ corresponds to negligible $\Delta m = \Delta E/c^2$. Violated in nuclear reactions (mass defect converts to energy).
Law of Definite (Constant) Proportions
(Proust, 1799)		 A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in the exact same fixed ratio by mass, irrespective of its source or method of preparation. Pure $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (rain, ocean, or synthesized from $\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$) always contains $\text{H}:\text{O} = 2:16 = 1:8$ by mass. Fails for (1) Isotopic variations (e.g., $\text{D}_2\text{O}$ has $\text{D}:\text{O} = 4:16 = 1:4$) and (2) Non-stoichiometric (Berthollide) compounds like wüstite ($\text{Fe}_{0.95}\text{O}$).
Law of Multiple Proportions
(Dalton, 1803)		 When two elements A and B form more than one compound, the masses of B that combine with a fixed mass of A bear a simple whole-number ratio. In $\text{N}_2\text{O}, \text{NO}, \text{N}_2\text{O}_3, \text{NO}_2, \text{N}_2\text{O}_5$, masses of $\text{O}$ combining with $28\text{ g}$ of $\text{N}$ are $16, 32, 48, 64, 80\text{ g}$ → ratio $1:2:3:4:5$. Applies strictly to the same two elements with identical isotopic composition. Does not apply to non-stoichiometric solids.
Law of Reciprocal Proportions
(Richter, 1792)		 The ratio of masses of two elements A and B which separately combine with a fixed mass of a third element C is either the same as, or a simple whole-number multiple of, the mass ratio in which A and B combine directly with each other. With $12\text{ g C}$: $\text{CH}_4$ uses $4\text{ g H}$; $\text{CO}_2$ uses $32\text{ g O}$. Ratio $\text{H}:\text{O} = 4:32 = 1:8$. Directly in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{H}:\text{O} = 2:16 = 1:8$ (identical!). Historical foundation for the Law of Chemical Equivalence: elements always combine in the ratio of their equivalent weights.
Gay-Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes
(Gay-Lussac, 1808)		 At constant temperature and pressure, gases react and are produced in volumes that bear simple whole-number ratios to one another. $1\text{ vol } \text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{ vol } \text{H}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{ vol } \text{NH}_3(g)$. Ratio $= 1:3:2$. Applies ONLY to gases, never to solids or liquids. Volume is NOT conserved ($1 + 3 = 4\text{ vol} \rightarrow 2\text{ vol}$).
Avogadro's Law & First-Principles Derivation of Vapour Density ($M = 2 \times \text{VD}$)

Avogadro's Law (1811): Equal volumes of all ideal gases measured at the same temperature $T$ and pressure $P$ contain an equal number of molecules ($V \propto n$ at constant $T, P$). This directly links Gay-Lussac's volume ratio to the stoichiometric mole ratio.

Vapour Density ($\text{VD}$) of a gas is defined as the ratio of the mass of a certain volume $V$ of the gas to the mass of an equal volume $V$ of hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$) measured under identical $T$ and $P$:

$$\text{VD} = \frac{\text{Mass of volume } V \text{ of gas}}{\text{Mass of volume } V \text{ of } \text{H}_2\text{ at same } T, P}$$

By Avogadro's Law, equal volumes at identical $T, P$ contain equal numbers of molecules (say $N$ molecules). Therefore:

$$\text{VD} = \frac{\text{Mass of } N \text{ molecules of gas}}{\text{Mass of } N \text{ molecules of } \text{H}_2} = \frac{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of gas}}{\text{Mass of } 1 \text{ molecule of } \text{H}_2} = \frac{M_{\text{gas}}}{2} \implies \boxed{M_{\text{gas}} = 2 \times \text{VD}}$$

Relative Density (General Form): If density of gas A is given relative to gas B (instead of $\text{H}_2$), then $d_{A/B} = \frac{M_A}{M_B}$. For a non-reacting gaseous mixture, the Average Molar Mass is $M_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\sum n_i M_i}{\sum n_i} = \sum \chi_i M_i$, and $\text{VD}_{\text{mix}} = \frac{M_{\text{avg}}}{2}$.

21.2 Atomic Mass, Isotopic Abundance & The Master Mole Concept

A single carbon-12 atom weighs $1.9926 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}$—too tiny for any laboratory balance. Chemists bridge the microscopic (atoms/molecules) and macroscopic (grams/litres) worlds using two tightly linked definitions:

  • Unified Atomic Mass Unit ($1\text{ u}$ or $1\text{ amu}$): Exactly $\frac{1}{12}\text{th}$ the mass of one neutral $^{12}\text{C}$ atom. Numerically, $1\text{ u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g} = \frac{1}{N_A}\text{ g}$.
  • Average Atomic Mass (Isotopic Abundance): Most elements occur naturally as mixtures of isotopes. If an element has isotopes with masses $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_k$ and fractional (or percentage) abundances $f_1, f_2, \dots, f_k$, then: $$A_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\sum (\%\text{ abundance}_i \times A_i)}{100} = \sum f_i A_i$$ Example: Chlorine consists of $75\%$ $^{35}\text{Cl}$ and $25\%$ $^{37}\text{Cl}$, giving $A_{\text{avg}} = \frac{75(35) + 25(37)}{100} = 35.5\text{ u}$. Note that no single chlorine atom weighs $35.5\text{ u}$—it is a weighted statistical average!
  • The Mole ($1\text{ mol}$): The SI unit of amount of substance containing exactly $N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, or formula units).
Critical Terminology Trap: Atomic Mass vs. Gram-Atomic Mass (GAM)

Examiners deliberately exploit sloppy phrasing in MCQ statements:

  • Mass of $1$ atom of Oxygen $= 16\text{ u} = 16 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g} = 2.66 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}$.
  • Gram-Atomic Mass (GAM) of Oxygen $= \text{mass of } 1\text{ mole of O atoms} = 16\text{ g}$.
  • "$1\text{ gram-atom}$ of Oxygen" means $1\text{ mole}$ of O atoms ($= 16\text{ g}$), NOT $1\text{ g}$ of oxygen!
  • "$1\text{ gram-molecule}$ of Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$)" means $1\text{ mole}$ of $\text{O}_2$ molecules ($= 32\text{ g} = 2\text{ gram-atoms}$ of O).
  • Hydrated Salts Trap: $1\text{ mol}$ of washing soda ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $M = 286\text{ g/mol}$) contains $2\text{ mol Na}$, $1\text{ mol C}$, $13\text{ mol O}$ ($3$ from carbonate $+ 10$ from water of crystallization!), $20\text{ mol H}$, and $36\text{ mol}$ total atoms. Forgetting the water's oxygen atoms is the #1 error on BEST hydrated-salt questions.

21.3 Percentage Composition, Empirical Formula & Minimum Molecular Mass

The mass percentage of any element $X$ in a compound is strictly determined by its stoichiometry:

$$\% \text{ of } X = \frac{(\text{Number of atoms of } X \text{ per formula unit}) \times (\text{Atomic mass of } X)}{\text{Molar mass of compound}} \times 100$$
Algorithm for Empirical & Molecular Formula Deduction
  1. Assume $100\text{ g}$ sample: Percentage values directly become masses in grams ($w_i$).
  2. Compute relative moles: Divide each element's mass by its atomic mass: $n_i = w_i / A_i$.
  3. Find simplest molar ratio: Divide every $n_i$ by the smallest $n_{\min}$ in the set.
  4. Clear fractions carefully: Never blindly round $1.5$ or $1.33$! Multiply ALL ratios by an integer $k$:
    • Decimal ends in $.50 \implies \times 2$
    • Decimal ends in $.33$ or $.67 \implies \times 3$
    • Decimal ends in $.25$ or $.75 \implies \times 4$
    • Decimal ends in $.20, .40, .60, .80 \implies \times 5$
  5. Scale to Molecular Formula: Calculate integer multiplier $n = \frac{\text{Molecular Mass}}{\text{Empirical Formula Mass}}$, where Molecular Mass may be given directly or via $M = 2 \times \text{VD}$. Then $\text{Molecular Formula} = (\text{Empirical Formula})_n$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Minimum Molecular Mass of Biomolecules

In NSEJS and BEST Paper 2, you are often told a giant biomolecule (haemoglobin, insulin, chlorophyll, peroxidase) contains a tiny mass percentage $p\%$ of a trace element $X$ (atomic mass $A_X$) and asked for its minimum molecular mass.

Derivation: A valid molecule cannot contain a fraction of an atom; it must contain at least $k \ge 1$ atom of element $X$. Thus:

$$p = \frac{k \cdot A_X}{M} \times 100 \implies M = \frac{k \cdot A_X \times 100}{p}$$

Setting $k = 1$ gives the minimum possible molecular mass: $\boxed{M_{\min} = \frac{A_X \times 100}{\% \text{ of } X}}$. Note that the actual molecular mass is an integer multiple $k \cdot M_{\min}$ (for instance, haemoglobin has $0.335\%\text{ Fe} \implies M_{\min} \approx 16{,}700\text{ u}$, while actual haemoglobin has $k = 4\text{ Fe}$ atoms giving $M \approx 66{,}800\text{ u}$).

21.4 Stoichiometry, Limiting Reagent, Purity/Yield & POAC

Consider a general balanced chemical equation:

$$a\text{A} + b\text{B} \rightarrow c\text{C} + d\text{D}$$

The fundamental law of stoichiometric equivalence states that the moles reacted or formed divided by their respective stoichiometric coefficients are all equal:

$$\frac{n_{\text{A, reacted}}}{a} = \frac{n_{\text{B, reacted}}}{b} = \frac{n_{\text{C, formed}}}{c} = \frac{n_{\text{D, formed}}}{d} = \xi \quad (\text{extent of reaction})$$
Exact Limiting Reagent (LR) Test — Never Eyeball by Mass or Raw Moles!

When quantities of two or more reactants are given simultaneously, one reactant runs out first and halts the reaction. This reactant is the Limiting Reagent (LR); all product yields MUST be calculated strictly from the LR.

  1. Convert every given reactant quantity into moles ($n_{\text{A}}, n_{\text{B}}$).
  2. Compute the Stoichiometric Ratio for each reactant: $R_i = \frac{n_{i,\text{ available}}}{\nu_i}$ (where $\nu_i$ is its balanced coefficient).
  3. The reactant with the smallest value of $R_i$ is the Limiting Reagent! That minimum value $R_{\min}$ equals the extent of reaction $\xi$.
  4. Instant Quantities:
    • Moles of any product $P$ formed $= \nu_P \times R_{\min}$.
    • Moles of any excess reactant $E$ consumed $= \nu_E \times R_{\min}$.
    • Moles of excess reactant $E$ left unreacted $= n_{E,\text{ available}} - \nu_E R_{\min}$.
Figure 21.2: Normalizing each reactant by its stoichiometric coefficient ($R = n/\nu$) visually reveals why $\text{H}_2$ runs out first even though it starts with more moles ($4.5 > 2.0$) than $\text{N}_2$.

Percentage Purity, Percentage Yield & Sequential/Parallel Reactions

  • Percentage Purity (applies to impure reactants BEFORE stoichiometry): Only the pure fraction reacts. $$\%\text{ Purity} = \frac{\text{Mass of pure substance}}{\text{Total mass of impure sample}} \times 100$$
  • Percentage Yield (applies to products AFTER stoichiometry): Side reactions or incomplete conversion reduce real output. $$\%\text{ Yield} = \frac{\text{Actual yield obtained}}{\text{Theoretical yield from LR}} \times 100$$
  • Sequential (Consecutive) Reactions: If $\text{A} \xrightarrow{y_1\%} \text{B} \xrightarrow{y_2\%} \text{C} \xrightarrow{y_3\%} \text{D}$, the intermediate product of Step 1 becomes the reactant of Step 2. Overall fractional yield multiplies sequentially: $Y_{\text{overall}} = \left(\frac{y_1}{100}\right)\left(\frac{y_2}{100}\right)\left(\frac{y_3}{100}\right)$.
  • Parallel (Competing) Reactions: A single reactant splits along two pathways simultaneously (e.g., $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$ and $2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}$). Solve by letting $x\text{ mol}$ react via Path 1 and $(n_{\text{total}} - x)\text{ mol}$ react via Path 2—or directly via POAC!
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Principle of Atom Conservation (POAC)

Core Theorem: In any chemical reaction (or sequence of reactions), atoms of each element are conserved. Therefore, moles of atoms of any element $X$ entering from reactants MUST equal moles of atoms of $X$ leaving in products—without ever needing to balance the chemical equation!

First-Principles Derivation: Consider thermal decomposition $\text{KClO}_3(s) \rightarrow \text{KCl}(s) + \text{O}_2(g)$ (deliberately left UNBALANCED). Every oxygen atom in $\text{KClO}_3$ ends up exclusively in $\text{O}_2$. Since $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{KClO}_3$ contains $3\text{ mol}$ of O atoms and $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{O}_2$ contains $2\text{ mol}$ of O atoms:

$$\text{Moles of O atoms in } \text{KClO}_3 = \text{Moles of O atoms in } \text{O}_2 \implies \boxed{3 \times n(\text{KClO}_3) = 2 \times n(\text{O}_2)}$$

Notice that this gives $n(\text{O}_2) = \frac{3}{2} n(\text{KClO}_3)$ in 5 seconds, identical to the balanced equation $2\text{KClO}_3 \rightarrow 2\text{KCl} + 3\text{O}_2$! Even better, for a 4-step industrial chain $\text{FeS}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_3 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, if all sulfur transfers to $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ at $100\%$ yield, POAC on S gives $2 \times n(\text{FeS}_2) = 1 \times n(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4)$ instantaneously.

Golden Condition for POAC: Always verify that the conserved atom has only one source on the left and one destination on the right (or explicitly sum ALL species containing that atom on each side, e.g., $n_{\text{C}}(\text{CH}_4) = n_{\text{C}}(\text{CO}_2) + n_{\text{C}}(\text{CO})$ in incomplete combustion).

21.5 Out-of-Portion Bridge: Eudiometry (Gas-Volume Stoichiometry)

Eudiometry is the experimental analysis of gaseous mixtures by sparking them (usually with excess $\text{O}_2$) inside a graduated glass tube (eudiometer) over mercury, cooling back to the initial room temperature and pressure, and sequentially absorbing specific product gases.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: The Four Rules of Eudiometry
  1. Volume $\equiv$ Moles (for gases): Because all measurements occur at identical $T$ and $P$, Avogadro's Law allows you to apply stoichiometric coefficients directly to gas volumes ($\text{mL}$ or $\text{L}$).
  2. Water Condenses to Zero Volume: At room temperature (below $100^\circ\text{C}$), $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ formed during combustion condenses into liquid water whose volume is negligible ($\sim \frac{1}{1200}$ of gas volume) and is treated as $0\text{ mL}$. (Trap: If the problem states gases are measured above $100^\circ\text{C}$, e.g. $120^\circ\text{C}$, water remains steam $\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$ and MUST be counted in total volume!)
  3. General Hydrocarbon Combustion Equation: $$\text{C}_x\text{H}_y(g) + \left(x + \frac{y}{4}\right)\text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow x\text{CO}_2(g) + \frac{y}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$ (For an oxygenated organic vapour $\text{C}_x\text{H}_y\text{O}_z(g)$, the $\text{O}_2$ coefficient becomes $x + \frac{y}{4} - \frac{z}{2}$.)
  4. Two-Stage Contraction Formulas (for $V\text{ mL}$ of $\text{C}_x\text{H}_y$ exploded with excess $\text{O}_2$):
    • First Contraction ($\Delta V_1$, on cooling to room temperature): $$\Delta V_1 = V_{\text{initial}} - V_{\text{after cooling}} = \left[V + \left(x + \frac{y}{4}\right)V\right] - xV = \boxed{V\left(1 + \frac{y}{4}\right)}$$ Notice how $x$ cancels out completely! $\Delta V_1$ immediately gives $y$ (hydrogen atoms).
    • Second Contraction ($\Delta V_2$, on shaking with aqueous $\text{KOH}$): $$\Delta V_2 = V(\text{CO}_2\text{ absorbed}) = \boxed{xV}$$ $\Delta V_2$ immediately gives $x$ (carbon atoms).

21.6 Out-of-Portion Bridge: Concentration Terms, Molarity/Molality & Equivalent Concept

A solution consists of a solute (minor component, mass $w_B$, molar mass $M_B$, moles $n_B$) dissolved in a solvent (major component, mass $w_A$, molar mass $M_A$, moles $n_A$), giving total solution mass $W_{\text{sol}} = w_A + w_B$, density $d$ (in $\text{g/mL}$), and volume $V_{\text{sol}} = \frac{W_{\text{sol}}}{d}$ (in $\text{mL}$).

Concentration Term Exact Formula Units Temp. Dependent? Exam Trap / Note
Mass Percent ($\%w/w$) $\frac{w_B}{w_A + w_B} \times 100$ Unitless ($\%$) No Denominator is solution mass ($w_A + w_B$), NOT solvent mass $w_A$.
Mass by Volume ($\%w/v$) $\frac{w_B\text{ (g)}}{V_{\text{sol}}\text{ (mL)}} \times 100$ $\text{g per } 100\text{ mL}$ Yes Related to $\%w/w$ via density: $(\%w/v) = (\%w/w) \times d$.
Volume Percent ($\%v/v$) $\frac{V_B}{V_{\text{sol}}} \times 100$ Unitless ($\%$) Yes Used for liquid–liquid solutions (e.g., ethanol in water).
Parts Per Million ($\text{ppm}$) $\frac{w_B}{W_{\text{sol}}} \times 10^6$ $\text{mg/kg}$ or $\text{mg/L}$ (dilute aq.) No ($w/w$ basis) Used for water hardness ($\text{CaCO}_3$ equiv.) & atmospheric pollutants.
Mole Fraction ($\chi_B$) $\frac{n_B}{n_A + n_B}$ Unitless No Always obeys $\chi_A + \chi_B = 1$ (for binary solution).
Molarity ($M$) $\frac{n_B}{V_{\text{sol}}\text{ (in L)}} = \frac{w_B \times 1000}{M_B \times V_{\text{sol}}\text{ (mL)}}$ $\text{mol L}^{-1}$ ($\text{M}$) Yes (decreases on heating) Volume expands with $T$, so $M$ changes with temperature!
Molality ($m$) $\frac{n_B}{w_A\text{ (in kg)}} = \frac{w_B \times 1000}{M_B \times w_A\text{ (g)}}$ $\text{mol kg}^{-1}$ ($\text{m}$) No (mass-only!) Temperature-independent! Denominator is solvent mass, NOT solution mass.
Normality ($N$) $\frac{\text{Gram-equivalents of solute}}{V_{\text{sol}}\text{ (in L)}} = M \times n\text{-factor}$ $\text{eq L}^{-1}$ ($\text{N}$) Yes Always reaction-specific because $n$-factor depends on the reaction.
First-Principles Derivations of the Two Most-Tested Interconversion Shortcuts

1. Density–Molarity Shortcut: Consider $100\text{ g}$ of a solution labeled $x\%\text{ w/w}$ with density $d\text{ (g/mL)}$.

  • Mass of solute $= x\text{ g} \implies n_B = \frac{x}{M_B}$.
  • Volume of $100\text{ g}$ solution $= \frac{100}{d}\text{ mL} = \frac{100}{1000 d}\text{ L} = \frac{1}{10 d}\text{ L}$.
  • Therefore: $M = \frac{n_B}{V_{\text{L}}} = \frac{x / M_B}{1 / (10 d)} \implies \boxed{M = \frac{10 \times (\%w/w) \times d}{M_B}}$.

2. Molarity ($M$) $\leftrightarrow$ Molality ($m$) Exact Formula: Consider exactly $1\text{ L}$ ($1000\text{ mL}$) of a solution of Molarity $M$ and density $d\text{ (g/mL)}$.

  • Moles of solute in $1\text{ L} = M\text{ mol} \implies$ Mass of solute $= M \cdot M_B\text{ g}$.
  • Total mass of $1000\text{ mL}$ solution $= 1000 d\text{ g}$.
  • Mass of solvent $= (1000 d - M \cdot M_B)\text{ g} = \frac{1000 d - M \cdot M_B}{1000}\text{ kg}$.
  • Therefore: $\boxed{m = \frac{1000 M}{1000 d - M \cdot M_B}}$.

3. Dilution & Mixing Laws (Conservation of Solute Moles): Adding pure solvent never changes solute moles, so $M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2$. When mixing two non-reacting solutions of the same solute, total moles add: $M_1 V_1 + M_2 V_2 = M_f (V_1 + V_2)$ (assuming additive volumes, or $V_{\text{final}}$ if diluted to a mark).

Equivalent Weight ($E$), $n$-Factor & The Law of Chemical Equivalence

The Equivalent Weight ($E$) of a substance is its molar mass divided by its valence factor ($n$-factor):

$$E = \frac{M}{n\text{-factor}}, \qquad \text{Gram-Equivalents (eq)} = \frac{w}{E} = n \times (n\text{-factor}), \qquad \text{Milliequivalents (meq)} = N \times V_{\text{mL}}$$
Species Category Definition of $n$-Factor Standard Examples & Equivalent Weights High-Yield Olympiad Traps
Acids Basicity: number of replaceable $\text{H}^+$ ions furnished per molecule in the given reaction. $\text{HCl}$ ($n=1, E=36.5$); $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($n=2, E=49$); $\text{H}_2\text{C}_2\text{O}_4 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ ($n=2, E=63$). Oxyacids of Phosphorus! Only $\text{O-H}$ hydrogens are acidic: $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$ ($n=3$), $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_3$ ($n=2$, NOT 3!), $\text{H}_3\text{PO}_2$ ($n=1$, NOT 3!).
Bases Acidity: number of replaceable $\text{OH}^-$ ions furnished per formula unit. $\text{NaOH}$ ($n=1, E=40$); $\text{Ca}(\text{OH})_2$ ($n=2, E=37$); $\text{Al}(\text{OH})_3$ ($n=3, E=26$). In partial neutralization (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{NaHSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$), $n$-factor of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is $1$, not $2$!
Salts (Non-Redox) Total magnitude of positive (or negative) charge on cations (or anions) per formula unit. $\text{NaCl}$ ($n=1$); $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ ($n=2, E=53$); $\text{CaCO}_3$ ($n=2, E=50$); $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$ ($n=6$). For $\text{NaHCO}_3 + \text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$, $\text{NaHCO}_3$ accepts $1\text{ H}^+$, so $n = 1$.
Redox Agents (Preview) Total electrons lost or gained per molecule/formula unit. $\text{KMnO}_4$ in acidic medium ($\text{Mn}^{+7} \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+}$): $n=5, E=M/5 = 31.6$. In neutral/faintly alkaline medium ($\text{MnO}_2$), $n=3$; in strongly alkaline medium ($\text{MnO}_4^{2-}$), $n=1$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge: Law of Chemical Equivalence & Back-Titration

Law of Chemical Equivalence: Regardless of stoichiometric coefficients in a balanced equation, 1 equivalent of any reactant always reacts with exactly 1 equivalent of another reactant to form exactly 1 equivalent of each product:

$$\text{eq}(\text{A}) = \text{eq}(\text{B}) \implies \boxed{N_1 V_1 = N_2 V_2} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{w_A}{E_A} = \frac{w_B}{E_B}$$

Back-Titration Shortcut: When an insoluble/impure base (like $\text{CaCO}_3$ or $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$) is dissolved in a known excess of acid ($N_a V_a$) and the unreacted leftover acid is neutralized by a standard base ($N_b V_b$):

$$\text{meq of sample} = \text{meq of total acid added} - \text{meq of base used in back-titration} = N_a V_a - N_b V_b$$

Then mass of pure sample $= \frac{(N_a V_a - N_b V_b)}{1000} \times E_{\text{sample}}$. No balanced equations required!

Level 1 Hydrated Salt Atom Counting Worked Example 1: Washing Soda Crystals ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$)

Calculate (a) the number of oxygen atoms, (b) the total number of atoms present in $57.2\text{ g}$ of washing soda crystals ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$), and (c) the mass of anhydrous $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ left behind when this sample is strongly heated to constant mass. (Use $\text{Na}=23, \text{C}=12, \text{O}=16, \text{H}=1, N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$.)

Step 1 — Molar Mass of Hydrated Salt:

$$M(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3) = 2(23) + 12 + 3(16) = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106\text{ g/mol}$$ $$M(10\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 10 \times [2(1) + 16] = 180\text{ g/mol}$$ $$M(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 106 + 180 = 286\text{ g/mol}$$

Step 2 — Moles of Formula Units:

$$n = \frac{w}{M} = \frac{57.2\text{ g}}{286\text{ g/mol}} = 0.2\text{ mol}$$

Part (a) — Oxygen Atoms (Watch the Water of Crystallization!):
Each formula unit of $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$ contains $3$ oxygen atoms from $\text{CO}_3^{2-}$ PLUS $10$ oxygen atoms from $10\text{H}_2\text{O}$, giving $3 + 10 = 13$ oxygen atoms per unit.

$$n(\text{O atoms}) = 0.2 \times 13 = 2.6\text{ mol}$$ $$N(\text{O atoms}) = 2.6 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 15.6572 \times 10^{23} \approx \mathbf{1.566 \times 10^{24}\text{ O atoms}}$$

Part (b) — Total Atoms:
Atomicity per formula unit $= 2(\text{Na}) + 1(\text{C}) + 3(\text{O}) + 10 \times (2\text{ H} + 1\text{ O}) = 6 + 30 = 36\text{ atoms}$.

$$N(\text{total atoms}) = 0.2 \times 36 \times N_A = 7.2 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 43.3584 \times 10^{23} \approx \mathbf{4.336 \times 10^{24}\text{ atoms}}$$

Part (c) — Mass of Anhydrous $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$:
Heating drives off all $10\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules while conserving the $0.2\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$:

$$w(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3) = 0.2\text{ mol} \times 106\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{21.2\text{ g}}$$
Level 1 Isotopic Abundance & Trace Element Worked Example 2: Average Atomic Mass of Boron & Minimum Molar Mass of Insulin

(a) Naturally occurring boron consists of two isotopes, $^{10}\text{B}$ (mass $10.0\text{ u}$) and $^{11}\text{B}$ (mass $11.0\text{ u}$). If the average atomic mass of boron is $10.8\text{ u}$, calculate the percentage abundance of each isotope.

(b) A sample of insulin contains $3.2\%$ sulfur by mass. Calculate the minimum possible molecular mass of insulin (in $\text{g/mol}$). (Atomic mass of $\text{S} = 32\text{ u}$.)

Part (a) — Isotopic Abundance:
Let the fractional abundance of $^{10}\text{B}$ be $x$. Since there are only two isotopes, the fractional abundance of $^{11}\text{B}$ is $(1 - x)$.

$$A_{\text{avg}} = 10.0(x) + 11.0(1 - x) = 10.8$$ $$11.0 - x = 10.8 \implies x = 0.20$$

Therefore, natural boron contains $20\%$ $^{10}\text{B}$ and $80\%$ $^{11}\text{B}$.

Part (b) — Minimum Molecular Mass:
For the molecular mass to be minimum, each molecule of insulin must contain the smallest whole number of sulfur atoms, i.e., $k = 1\text{ atom}$ of S (mass $= 32\text{ g/mol}$).

$$\% \text{ of S} = \frac{1 \times 32}{M_{\min}} \times 100 = 3.2$$ $$M_{\min} = \frac{32 \times 100}{3.2} = \mathbf{1000\text{ g/mol}}$$
Level 1 Empirical Formula + Vapour Density Worked Example 3: Deducing Molecular Formula from Composition & VD

An organic compound contains $40.0\%$ carbon, $6.67\%$ hydrogen, and the remainder oxygen by mass. Its vapour density is $30$. Determine its empirical formula and molecular formula.

Step 1 — Find Mass Percentage of Oxygen:

$$\%\text{ O} = 100 - (40.0 + 6.67) = 53.33\%$$

Step 2 — Relative Moles & Simplest Ratio (Assuming $100\text{ g}$ sample):

  • $\text{C}: \frac{40.0}{12} = 3.333\text{ mol} \implies \text{Ratio} = \frac{3.333}{3.333} = 1$
  • $\text{H}: \frac{6.67}{1} = 6.67\text{ mol} \implies \text{Ratio} = \frac{6.67}{3.333} = 2$
  • $\text{O}: \frac{53.33}{16} = 3.333\text{ mol} \implies \text{Ratio} = \frac{3.333}{3.333} = 1$

Hence, the Empirical Formula $= \text{CH}_2\text{O}$, and Empirical Formula Mass $(\text{EFM}) = 12 + 2(1) + 16 = 30\text{ g/mol}$.

Step 3 — Molecular Formula via Vapour Density:

$$M = 2 \times \text{VD} = 2 \times 30 = 60\text{ g/mol}$$ $$n = \frac{M}{\text{EFM}} = \frac{60}{30} = 2 \implies \text{Molecular Formula} = (\text{CH}_2\text{O})_2 = \mathbf{\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}_2}$$

Level 2 Limiting Reagent + % Yield Worked Example 4: Haber Process Limiting Reagent, Excess Leftover & Real Yield

If $56\text{ g}$ of $\text{N}_2$ is reacted with $9\text{ g}$ of $\text{H}_2$: (a) identify the limiting reagent and find the mass of excess reagent left unreacted, (b) calculate the theoretical volume of $\text{NH}_3$ produced at STP, and (c) find the actual mass of $\text{NH}_3$ obtained if the percentage yield of the process is $80\%$.

Step 1 — Convert Reactants to Moles:

Part (a) — Limiting Reagent Test ($R = n / \nu$):

$$n(\text{N}_2\text{ consumed}) = 1 \times 1.5 = 1.5\text{ mol} \implies n(\text{N}_2\text{ leftover}) = 2.0 - 1.5 = 0.5\text{ mol}$$ $$\text{Mass of excess }\text{N}_2\text{ left} = 0.5 \times 28 = \mathbf{14\text{ g}}$$

$$n(\text{NH}_3\text{ actual}) = 3.0 \times \frac{80}{100} = 2.4\text{ mol}$$ $$w(\text{NH}_3\text{ actual}) = 2.4 \times 17 = \mathbf{40.8\text{ g}}$$

Level 3 Eudiometry Contraction Analysis Worked Example 8: Identifying an Unknown Hydrocarbon by Two-Stage Eudiometry

$20\text{ mL}$ of a gaseous hydrocarbon $\text{C}_x\text{H}_y$ is exploded with $120\text{ mL}$ of excess $\text{O}_2$ in a eudiometer. On cooling to room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$), the volume of the residual gases is $90\text{ mL}$. When shaken with aqueous $\text{KOH}$, the volume shrinks further to $30\text{ mL}$, and this final $30\text{ mL}$ is completely absorbed by alkaline pyrogallol. Deduce the molecular formula of the hydrocarbon (all volumes at identical $T, P$).

Method 1 — Full First-Principles Volume Accounting:

$$\text{C}_x\text{H}_y(g) + \left(x + \frac{y}{4}\right)\text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow x\text{CO}_2(g) + \frac{y}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
  1. Volume of $\text{CO}_2$ formed (absorbed by $\text{KOH}$): $$V(\text{CO}_2) = 90 - 30 = 60\text{ mL}$$ Since $20\text{ mL}$ of $\text{C}_x\text{H}_y$ produces $20x\text{ mL}$ of $\text{CO}_2$: $$20x = 60 \implies \mathbf{x = 3}$$
  2. Volume of $\text{O}_2$ actually consumed:
    Alkaline pyrogallol absorbs the final $30\text{ mL}$, confirming that $V(\text{O}_2\text{ unused}) = 30\text{ mL}$. Therefore: $$V(\text{O}_2\text{ consumed}) = V(\text{O}_2\text{ taken}) - V(\text{O}_2\text{ unused}) = 120 - 30 = 90\text{ mL}$$
  3. Solve for $y$:
    From the combustion stoichiometry, $V(\text{O}_2\text{ consumed}) = 20\left(x + \frac{y}{4}\right)$: $$20\left(3 + \frac{y}{4}\right) = 90 \implies 3 + \frac{y}{4} = 4.5 \implies \frac{y}{4} = 1.5 \implies \mathbf{y = 6}$$

Hence, the molecular formula of the hydrocarbon is $\text{C}_3\text{H}_6$ (propene / cyclopropane).

Read $\Delta V_1$ and $\Delta V_2$ straight from the prompt without tracking $\text{O}_2$:

  • $\Delta V_1 = V_{\text{init}} - V_{\text{cooled}} = (20 + 120) - 90 = 50\text{ mL} = 20\left(1 + \frac{y}{4}\right) \implies 1 + \frac{y}{4} = 2.5 \implies \mathbf{y = 6}$.
  • $\Delta V_2 = 90 - 30 = 60\text{ mL} = 20x \implies \mathbf{x = 3}$.

Formula $= \mathbf{\text{C}_3\text{H}_6}$ in two mental lines!

Level 3 Back-Titration & Equivalence Worked Example 9: Purity of Impure Soda Ash ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$) via Acid–Base Back-Titration

$2.65\text{ g}$ of an impure sample of anhydrous sodium carbonate ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$, $M = 106\text{ g/mol}$; impurities are neutral and inert) is dissolved in $50\text{ mL}$ of $0.5\text{ M}$ $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ (excess acid). After complete reaction and boiling off $\text{CO}_2$, the remaining excess acid requires $20\text{ mL}$ of $0.5\text{ M}$ $\text{NaOH}$ for exact neutralization. Calculate the percentage purity of the $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ sample.

Method 1 — Full Mole Stoichiometry (Two Balanced Equations):

(1) $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$
(2) $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$

  1. Total moles of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ initially taken $= 0.5\text{ M} \times 0.050\text{ L} = 0.025\text{ mol}$.
  2. Moles of $\text{NaOH}$ used in back-titration $= 0.5\text{ M} \times 0.020\text{ L} = 0.010\text{ mol}$.
  3. From Eq. (2), $1\text{ mol } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ reacts with $2\text{ mol } \text{NaOH}$, so excess $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 = \frac{0.010}{2} = 0.005\text{ mol}$.
  4. Moles of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ consumed by $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 = 0.025 - 0.005 = 0.020\text{ mol}$.
  5. From Eq. (1) ($1:1$ ratio), $n(\text{pure Na}_2\text{CO}_3) = 0.020\text{ mol} \implies w = 0.020 \times 106 = 2.12\text{ g}$.
  6. Percentage purity $= \frac{2.12}{2.65} \times 100 = \mathbf{80\%}$.
30-Second Olympiad Shortcut: Law of Chemical Equivalence (Zero Equations!)

Convert Molarities directly to Normalities: $N(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4) = 0.5 \times 2 = 1.0\text{ N}$; $N(\text{NaOH}) = 0.5 \times 1 = 0.5\text{ N}$; Equivalent weight of $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 = \frac{106}{2} = 53\text{ g/eq}$.

$$\text{meq}(\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3) = \text{meq}(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{ total}) - \text{meq}(\text{NaOH}) = (1.0 \times 50) - (0.5 \times 20) = 50 - 10 = 40\text{ meq} = 0.04\text{ eq}$$ $$w(\text{pure Na}_2\text{CO}_3) = 0.04\text{ eq} \times 53\text{ g/eq} = 2.12\text{ g} \implies \%\text{ Purity} = \frac{2.12}{2.65} \times 100 = \mathbf{80\%}$$
