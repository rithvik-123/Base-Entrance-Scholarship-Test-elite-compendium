1. Graphical Representation, Consistency & The Zero-Coefficient Trap

A pair of linear equations in two variables $x$ and $y$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is written in standard form as:

$$a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1 = 0 \qquad (a_1^2 + b_1^2 \neq 0)$$ $$a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2 = 0 \qquad (a_2^2 + b_2^2 \neq 0)$$

The non-degeneracy condition $a_i^2 + b_i^2 \neq 0$ simply guarantees that $a_i$ and $b_i$ are not simultaneously zero, so each equation represents a genuine straight line in the Cartesian plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. A pair $(x_0, y_0)$ is a solution of the system if and only if the point $(x_0, y_0)$ lies on both lines simultaneously.

Figure 8.1: The three exhaustive geometric configurations of two straight lines in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Both parallel and coincident systems share the vanishing determinant $\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = 0$.

1.1 First-Principles Derivation of Consistency Conditions

Eliminate $y$ by multiplying the first equation by $b_2$ and the second by $b_1$, then subtracting:

$$(a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)x = b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1$$

Similarly, eliminate $x$ by multiplying the first by $a_2$ and the second by $a_1$, then subtracting:

$$(a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)y = c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1$$

Define the three fundamental $2 \times 2$ determinants (keeping $c_1, c_2$ on the left-hand side as $a_i x + b_i y + c_i = 0$):

$$\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1, \qquad D_x = b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1, \qquad D_y = c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1$$

This gives the exact linear identities $\Delta \cdot x = D_x$ and $\Delta \cdot y = D_y$. Every consistency theorem follows rigorously from these two equations—without ever dividing by a potentially zero coefficient:

Geometric State Consistency Label Exact Determinant Criterion (Always Valid) Ratio Form (Valid ONLY if $a_2, b_2, c_2 \neq 0$) Number of Solutions
Intersecting Lines Consistent & Independent $\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \neq 0$ $\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} \neq \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}$ Exactly $1$
Parallel Distinct Lines Inconsistent $\Delta = 0$ AND at least one of $D_x \neq 0$ or $D_y \neq 0$ $\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2} \neq \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}$ $0$ (No solution)
Coincident Lines Consistent & Dependent $\Delta = 0$, $D_x = 0$, AND $D_y = 0$ (assuming $a_i^2+b_i^2 \neq 0$) $\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2} = \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}$ Infinitely many
Critical Warning — The Deadly Zero-Coefficient Trap

In NCERT Level 1 questions, coefficients are nonzero numbers, so students memorize $\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} \neq \frac{c_1}{c_2}$ mechanically. In BEST, NTSE Stage 2, and PRMO-lite exams, examiners deliberately inject parameters that vanish at critical roots. Naive ratio division fails in three distinct ways:

  1. Silent Root Loss by Cancellation: Writing $\frac{k-1}{k^2-1} = \frac{3}{2k+1}$ and cancelling $(k-1)$ implicitly assumes $k \neq 1$, silently deleting $k=1$—which is frequently the exact value producing parallel horizontal/vertical lines!
  2. Division by Zero ($0/0$ Ambiguity): If $a_1 = a_2 = 0$ (both lines horizontal: $b_1 y + c_1 = 0$ and $b_2 y + c_2 = 0$), the fraction $\frac{a_1}{a_2}$ is undefined, yet the lines are legitimately parallel or coincident.
  3. The Constant-Zero Trap ($c_1 = c_2 = 0$): A homogeneous system $a_1 x + b_1 y = 0,\ a_2 x + b_2 y = 0$ is ALWAYS consistent because $(0, 0)$ is always a solution. It can NEVER have “no solution”! When $\Delta = 0$, it automatically has infinitely many solutions; when $\Delta \neq 0$, its only solution is the trivial solution $(0, 0)$.

Golden Exam Protocol: To find parameter values for “no solution” or “infinitely many solutions,” NEVER start with fractions. Always:

  1. Solve the polynomial equation $\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = 0$ to collect all candidate parameter values (never cancel a common parameter factor!).
  2. Substitute each candidate value explicitly back into the original two equations and inspect by sight whether the resulting lines are parallel (contradiction) or identical (coincident).
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Degenerate Line Check ($0x + 0y = c$)

Watch out for the ultimate Olympiad edge case: what if a parameter value $k = k_0$ makes both $a_1 = 0$ AND $b_1 = 0$ in one equation? Then $\Delta = 0$ automatically, AND $D_x, D_y$ might vanish too if $c_1 = 0$!

  • If Equation 1 collapses to $0x + 0y = 0$, it holds for every point $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, so the solution set is the entire second line (infinitely many solutions).
  • If Equation 1 collapses to $0x + 0y = 5$, it holds for no point, so the system has no solution—even if someone only checked $b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1 = 0$ when $b_1 = b_2 = 0$!

Direct substitution of every root of $\Delta = 0$ into the original equations makes you 100% immune to this trap.

2. Algebraic Solution Methods & 2×2 Cramer’s Rule Shortcut

Choosing the right algebraic solver cuts solution time from 2 minutes to 20 seconds and eliminates sign errors.

  • Substitution Method: Optimal when one variable already has coefficient $\pm 1$ (e.g., $y = 3x - 7$), or when solving a line-curve system later in Quadratics/Coordinate Geometry.
  • Elimination (Linear Combination) Method: Multiply equations by minimal integer factors to match the LCM of coefficients of $x$ or $y$, then add or subtract. Optimal for standard integer-coefficient systems.
  • Cross-Multiplication / $2 \times 2$ Cramer’s Rule: Optimal when coefficients are algebraic parameters (like $a, b, m, k$) or prime/coprime integers where finding LCMs is messy.

2.1 Derivation of Cross-Multiplication & Cramer’s Rule

Consider the system written in RHS-constant form (preferred in JEE/Olympiad work to prevent sign mistakes):

$$a_1 x + b_1 y = d_1$$ $$a_2 x + b_2 y = d_2$$

Eliminating $y$ via $b_2 \cdot (\text{Eq } 1) - b_1 \cdot (\text{Eq } 2)$ yields $(a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)x = d_1 b_2 - d_2 b_1$. Eliminating $x$ via $a_1 \cdot (\text{Eq } 2) - a_2 \cdot (\text{Eq } 1)$ yields $(a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)y = a_1 d_2 - a_2 d_1$. Using $2 \times 2$ determinant notation $\begin{vmatrix} p & q \\ r & s \end{vmatrix} = ps - qr$, we obtain Cramer’s Rule:

$$\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 \end{vmatrix} = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1, \qquad \Delta_x = \begin{vmatrix} d_1 & b_1 \\ d_2 & b_2 \end{vmatrix} = d_1 b_2 - d_2 b_1, \qquad \Delta_y = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & d_1 \\ a_2 & d_2 \end{vmatrix} = a_1 d_2 - a_2 d_1$$

Whenever $\Delta \neq 0$, the unique solution is instantly:

$$x = \frac{\Delta_x}{\Delta}, \qquad y = \frac{\Delta_y}{\Delta}$$
Sign-Error Firewall — RHS vs. LHS Convention

Notice the subtle difference between the NCERT Cross-Multiplication form (constants on the LHS: $a_i x + b_i y + c_i = 0$) and Cramer’s Rule (constants on the RHS: $a_i x + b_i y = d_i$, so $d_i = -c_i$):

  • LHS Form ($a_i x + b_i y + c_i = 0$): $\dfrac{x}{b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1} = \dfrac{y}{c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1} = \dfrac{1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}$.
  • RHS Form ($a_i x + b_i y = d_i$): $\Delta_x$ replaces the $x$-column with the RHS column; $\Delta_y$ replaces the $y$-column with the RHS column.

Pick Cramer’s Rule (RHS form) as your default: replacing column $1$ for $x$ and column $2$ for $y$ generalizes directly to $3 \times 3$ matrices in Class 11/12 JEE Mathematics and virtually eliminates cyclic-order sign slips.

3. Special Symmetric Systems & Equations Reducible to Linear Form

3.1 The Interchanged-Coefficient (Symmetric) System

Whenever a system has large coefficients swapped between $x$ and $y$:

$$ax + by = c_1 \quad \text{(1)}$$ $$bx + ay = c_2 \quad \text{(2)}$$

where $|a| \neq |b|$, never multiply by $a$ and $b$ (that creates 5-digit numbers and arithmetic errors). Instead, exploit symmetry by adding and subtracting:

  1. Add (1) + (2): $(a + b)(x + y) = c_1 + c_2 \implies x + y = \dfrac{c_1 + c_2}{a + b}$.
  2. Subtract (1) − (2): $(a - b)(x - y) = c_1 - c_2 \implies x - y = \dfrac{c_1 - c_2}{a - b}$.

This collapses huge coefficients into a trivial sum-and-difference pair $x + y = S$ and $x - y = D$, giving $x = \frac{S+D}{2}$ and $y = \frac{S-D}{2}$ in under 20 seconds.

Note on Anti-Symmetric Variant: If $ax - by = c_1$ and $bx - ay = c_2$, adding gives $(a+b)(x-y) = c_1+c_2$ and subtracting gives $(a-b)(x+y) = c_1-c_2$. The exact same add-and-subtract engine works!

3.2 Equations Reducible to Linear Form & Domain Traps

Many non-linear systems become strictly linear after substituting auxiliary variables $u$ and $v$:

Non-Linear Pattern Linearizing Substitution Mandatory Domain Restriction
$\dfrac{p_1}{x} + \dfrac{q_1}{y} = r_1,\; \dfrac{p_2}{x} + \dfrac{q_2}{y} = r_2$ $u = \dfrac{1}{x},\; v = \dfrac{1}{y}$ $x \neq 0,\; y \neq 0$ (hence $u \neq 0,\; v \neq 0$)
$\dfrac{p_1}{ax+by} + \dfrac{q_1}{cx+dy} = r_1$ $u = \dfrac{1}{ax+by},\; v = \dfrac{1}{cx+dy}$ $ax+by \neq 0,\; cx+dy \neq 0$
$\dfrac{p_1}{\sqrt{x}} + \dfrac{q_1}{\sqrt{y}} = r_1$ $u = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}},\; v = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{y}}$ $x > 0,\; y > 0$ AND $u > 0,\; v > 0$!
$a_1 x + b_1 y = c_1 xy,\; a_2 x + b_2 y = c_2 xy$ Check $(0,0)$ first; then divide by $xy$ and set $u = \frac{1}{y}, v = \frac{1}{x}$ Never lose $(0,0)$ when dividing by $xy$!
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Two Extraneous-Solution Killers

Trap A (The Square-Root Sign Trap): If you substitute $u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ and solve the linear system to get $u = -\frac{1}{2}$, you must REJECT this solution! By definition of the principal square root over $\mathbb{R}$, $\sqrt{x} > 0$, so $u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ cannot be negative. Squaring $u = -\frac{1}{2}$ to write $x = 4$ is a classic Olympiad blunder.

Trap B (The Lost-Origin Trap): In systems like $6x + 3y = 6xy$ and $2x + 4y = 5xy$, dividing both sides by $xy$ yields a linear system in $\frac{1}{y}$ and $\frac{1}{x}$ giving $(x, y) = (1, 2)$. However, $(0, 0)$ ALSO satisfies both original equations! Always test $x = 0$ and $y = 0$ separately before dividing by $xy$.

4. Areas of Triangles & Quadrilaterals Bounded by Linear Graphs

BEST Paper 1 frequently fuses algebra with coordinate geometry by asking for the area enclosed by two intersecting lines $L_1, L_2$ and one or both coordinate axes.

Figure 8.2: Geometric decomposition of $\triangle PAB$ (bounded by $L_1, L_2$, and the $x$-axis) and the first-quadrant quadrilateral $OAPE$ (bounded by $L_1, L_2$, and both positive axes).

4.1 Direct Axis-Triangle Formulas

Suppose $L_1$ and $L_2$ intersect at $P(x_P, y_P)$, cut the $x$-axis at $A(x_1, 0)$ and $B(x_2, 0)$, and cut the $y$-axis at $D(0, y_1)$ and $E(0, y_2)$.

  • Triangle with the $x$-axis ($\triangle PAB$): The base lies strictly along $y = 0$, so its length is $|x_1 - x_2|$, and the perpendicular altitude from $P$ to the $x$-axis is $|y_P|$: $$\text{Area}(\triangle PAB) = \frac{1}{2} \, |x_1 - x_2| \cdot |y_P|$$
  • Triangle with the $y$-axis ($\triangle PDE$): The base lies strictly along $x = 0$, so its length is $|y_1 - y_2|$, and the perpendicular altitude from $P$ to the $y$-axis is $|x_P|$: $$\text{Area}(\triangle PDE) = \frac{1}{2} \, |y_1 - y_2| \cdot |x_P|$$
  • Quadrilateral in Quadrant I (or Trapezium between Parallel Lines): Always decompose a quadrilateral bounded by two lines and the axes as the difference of two right/axis triangles! As Figure 8.2 illustrates, $\text{Area}(OAPE) = \text{Area}(\triangle PAB) - \text{Area}(\triangle OBE) = \text{Area}(\triangle OAD) - \text{Area}(\triangle PDE)$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Shoelace Formula & Single-Line Axis Triangle

1. Single Line with Both Axes: A line $ax + by = c$ ($a, b, c \neq 0$) intercepts the axes at $\left(\frac{c}{a}, 0\right)$ and $\left(0, \frac{c}{b}\right)$, forming a right triangle with the origin of area:

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\left|\frac{c}{a}\right|\left|\frac{c}{b}\right| = \frac{c^2}{2|ab|}$$

2. Arbitrary 3-Line Triangle (Shoelace Formula): If three non-concurrent lines meet pairwise at $(x_1, y_1)$, $(x_2, y_2)$, and $(x_3, y_3)$, compute the area directly via the determinant shoelace formula (no distance formula or Heron’s formula needed):

$$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl|x_1(y_2 - y_3) + x_2(y_3 - y_1) + x_3(y_1 - y_2)\Bigr|$$

Exam Trap: Always draw a quick 5-second quadrant sketch before subtracting areas! Never assume both $x$-intercepts are positive—if $x_1 = 4$ and $x_2 = -1$, the base is $|4 - (-1)| = 5$, NOT $4 - 1 = 3$.

5. Linear Diophantine Equations (Olympiad Bridge)

A single linear equation $ax + by = c$ has infinitely many real solutions $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. However, when BEST Paper 1 or IOQM restricts $(x, y)$ to integers ($\mathbb{Z}$), non-negative integers ($\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$), or positive integers ($\mathbb{Z}^+$)—often disguised inside coin, stamp, or ticket word problems—it becomes a Linear Diophantine Equation.

Fundamental Theorem of Linear Diophantine Equations

Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $a, b$ not both zero, and let $d = \gcd(a, b)$.

  1. Existence (Bézout’s Criterion): The equation $ax + by = c$ has integer solutions $(x, y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ if and only if $d \mid c$ (i.e., $\gcd(a, b)$ divides $c$). If $d \nmid c$, there are zero integer solutions.
  2. Complete Parametric Solution: If $d \mid c$ and $(x_0, y_0)$ is any one particular integer solution, then every integer solution is generated uniquely by an integer parameter $t \in \mathbb{Z}$: $$x = x_0 + \frac{b}{d}t, \qquad y = y_0 - \frac{a}{d}t \qquad (t \in \mathbb{Z})$$

5.1 First-Principles Proof of the Parametric Generator

First divide $ax + by = c$ by $d = \gcd(a, b)$ to obtain the reduced equation $a' x + b' y = c'$, where $a' = \frac{a}{d}$, $b' = \frac{b}{d}$, $c' = \frac{c}{d}$, and crucially $\gcd(a', b') = 1$. Since $(x_0, y_0)$ is also a solution, $a' x_0 + b' y_0 = c'$. Subtracting the two equations eliminates $c'$:

$$a'(x - x_0) + b'(y - y_0) = 0 \implies a'(x - x_0) = -b'(y - y_0)$$

Since $b'$ divides the right-hand side, $b'$ must divide $a'(x - x_0)$. Because $\gcd(a', b') = 1$, Euclid’s Lemma forces $b' \mid (x - x_0)$. Therefore, there exists a unique integer $t \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that:

$$x - x_0 = b' t = \frac{b}{d}t$$

Substituting back into $a'(b' t) = -b'(y - y_0)$ and dividing by $b' \neq 0$ yields $y - y_0 = -a' t = -\frac{a}{d}t$. Conversely, substituting these into $ax + by$ gives $a\left(x_0 + \frac{b}{d}t\right) + b\left(y_0 - \frac{a}{d}t\right) = ax_0 + by_0 + \frac{ab - ba}{d}t = c$. Hence the formula generates all and only the integer solutions.

Figure 8.3: Integer lattice geometry of $3x + 5y = 47$ in Quadrant I. Every valid lattice point is separated from the next by the constant vector $(\Delta x, \Delta y) = (+b/d, -a/d) = (+5, -3)$.

5.2 Step-by-Step Algorithm for Counting Feasible Integer Pairs

  1. Reduce: Compute $d = \gcd(a, b)$. If $d \nmid c$, answer is $0$ immediately. Otherwise divide through by $d$ so $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.
  2. Find One Particular Solution $(x_0, y_0)$: Take the equation modulo the smaller coefficient (say $a$): $by \equiv c \pmod{a}$. Solve for the smallest valid $y_0$ (e.g., $y_0 \ge 1$ for positive integers, or $y_0 \ge 0$ for non-negative integers), then compute $x_0 = \frac{c - by_0}{a}$.
  3. Parametrize: Write $x = x_0 - bt$ and $y = y_0 + at$ (or $x = x_0 + bt,\ y = y_0 - at$).
  4. Bound $t$: Impose the exact domain inequalities carefully:
    • For positive integers (natural numbers): require $x \ge 1$ AND $y \ge 1$.
    • For non-negative integers (whole numbers): require $x \ge 0$ AND $y \ge 0$.
    Solve both inequalities for $t$ to get $t_{\min} \le t \le t_{\max}$ (rounding $t_{\min}$ up with $\lceil \cdot \rceil$ and $t_{\max}$ down with $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$). The exact count is $t_{\max} - t_{\min} + 1$ (or $0$ if $t_{\max} < t_{\min}$).
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Exact Quotient Formula & Chicken McNugget Theorem

1. The Quotient-Remainder Counting Shortcut ($\gcd(a,b) = 1$, $a, b > 0$): Divide $c$ by $ab$: write $c = q(ab) + r$ with $0 \le r < ab$, where $q=\left\lfloor \frac{c}{ab} \right\rfloor$.

  • Positive integer solutions ($x, y \ge 1$):
    • If $r = 0$ (i.e., $ab \mid c$): exact count $= q - 1$.
    • If $r > 0$: exact count $= q + 1$ if $ax + by = r$ has a positive integer solution, and $q$ otherwise!
  • Non-negative integer solutions ($x, y \ge 0$): Since $x' = x+1 \ge 1$ and $y' = y+1 \ge 1$ transforms $ax + by = c$ into $ax' + by' = c + a + b$, the count is always $\left\lfloor \frac{c}{ab} \right\rfloor$ or $\left\lfloor \frac{c}{ab} \right\rfloor + 1$ (specifically, $q + 1$ if $ax + by = r$ has a non-negative solution, else $q$).

2. Frobenius Coin (Chicken McNugget) Theorem: For coprime positive integers $a, b$, the largest integer $c$ that CANNOT be expressed as $ax + by$ in non-negative integers ($x, y \ge 0$) is:

$$g(a, b) = ab - a - b$$

Every integer $c > ab - a - b$ has at least one non-negative integer representation! Furthermore, the total number of unrepresentable positive integers is exactly $\dfrac{(a-1)(b-1)}{2}$.

6. High-Trap Situational Word Problems & 3-Variable Homogeneous Systems

Even strong algebra students lose marks on Paper 1 word problems due to phrasing traps. Lock in these five canonical modeling blueprints:

Sum $= 11(x+y)$; Difference $= 9(x-y)$.		 Never confuse the digits $(x,y)$ with the number $10x+y$. Enforce $1 \le x \le 9$ and $0 \le y \le 9$. When “difference between digits is $d$,” test BOTH $x-y=d$ and $y-x=d$!
Two-Way Fractions Fraction $= \dfrac{N}{D}$ ($D \neq 0$). Translate numerator and denominator changes into two separate equations. Check whether the question asks for the original fraction or its simplified form, and ensure $D \neq 0$.
Ages (Past / Future Shifts) Let present ages be $F$ and $S$. In $k$ years: $F+k, S+k$. $k$ years ago: $F-k, S-k$. Phrasing trap: “When I was as old as you are now” shifts time back by $(F - S)$ years, making the younger person’s age $S - (F - S) = 2S - F$!

3-Variable Homogeneous Ratio Formula (Cross-Product Rule)

Given two independent homogeneous equations $a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1 z = 0$ and $a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2 z = 0$, divide through by $z$ (assuming $z \neq 0$) and apply $2 \times 2$ cross-multiplication to $(x/z, y/z)$:

$$\frac{x}{b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1} = \frac{y}{c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1} = \frac{z}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1} = k \qquad (k \neq 0)$$

Thus $x = (b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1)k$, $y = (c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1)k$, and $z = (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)k$. In any homogeneous ratio like $\frac{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}{xy + yz + zx}$, the scalar $k^2$ cancels identically—so you can simply set $k = 1$!

No solution $\iff \Delta = 0$ and $(D_x, D_y) \neq (0, 0)$ (verify by direct substitution!). Infinite solutions $\iff$ identical equations.
  • 2×2 Cramer’s Rule ($a_i x + b_i y = d_i$): $x = \dfrac{d_1 b_2 - d_2 b_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}$, $\quad y = \dfrac{a_1 d_2 - a_2 d_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}$ (for $\Delta \neq 0$).
  • Symmetric System ($ax + by = c_1,\ bx + ay = c_2$): $x + y = \dfrac{c_1 + c_2}{a + b}$ and $x - y = \dfrac{c_1 - c_2}{a - b}$ ($|a| \neq |b|$).
  • Axis Triangle Areas: With $x$-axis: $\frac{1}{2}|x_1 - x_2|\cdot |y_P|$. With $y$-axis: $\frac{1}{2}|y_1 - y_2|\cdot |x_P|$. Single line $ax + by = c$ with both axes: $\dfrac{c^2}{2|ab|}$.
  • Shoelace Area of $\triangle P_1 P_2 P_3$: $\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl|x_1(y_2 - y_3) + x_2(y_3 - y_1) + x_3(y_1 - y_2)\bigr|$.
  • Linear Diophantine ($ax + by = c$, $d = \gcd(a,b)$): Solvable in $\mathbb{Z} \iff d \mid c$. General solution: $x = x_0 + \dfrac{b}{d}t,\; y = y_0 - \dfrac{a}{d}t$ ($t \in \mathbb{Z}$).
  • Diophantine Positive Count ($\gcd(a,b)=1,\ c = qab + r,\ 0 \le r < ab$): Count $= q - 1$ if $r = 0$; else $q + 1$ if $ax + by = r$ has a positive integer solution, otherwise $q$.
  • Frobenius Bound ($\gcd(a,b)=1$): Largest integer NOT expressible as $ax + by$ ($x, y \ge 0$) is $ab - a - b$.
  • 3-Variable Homogeneous Ratio: $x : y : z = (b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1) : (c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1) : (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)$.

7. Graded Worked Examples (Level 1 → Level 2 → Level 3)

LEVEL 1: WARMUP — Core Mechanics & Foundation Precision
Level 1 Consistency Parameters Worked Example 1: Distinguishing No Solution vs. Infinite Solutions
Find all real values of $k$ for which the system of linear equations $$kx + 3y = k - 3$$ $$12x + ky = k$$ has (a) no solution, and (b) infinitely many solutions.

Step 1 — Apply the Determinant Condition (Avoid Premature Division):

Here $a_1 = k,\ b_1 = 3$ and $a_2 = 12,\ b_2 = k$. For the system to have either no solution or infinitely many solutions, the coefficient determinant $\Delta$ must vanish:

$$\Delta = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = k(k) - 12(3) = k^2 - 36 = 0 \implies k = 6 \quad \text{or} \quad k = -6$$

Notice that for $k = 0$, $\Delta = -36 \neq 0$, so $k = 0$ yields a unique solution $(0, -1)$ even though the naive ratio $\frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{3}{0}$ is undefined.

Step 2 — Substitute Each Candidate Back into the Original System:

  • Case $k = 6$: $$\text{Eq (1): } 6x + 3y = 3 \iff 2x + y = 1$$ $$\text{Eq (2): } 12x + 6y = 6 \iff 2x + y = 1$$ Both equations represent the exact same line $2x + y = 1$. Hence $k = 6$ gives infinitely many solutions.
  • Case $k = -6$: $$\text{Eq (1): } -6x + 3y = -9 \iff 2x - y = 3$$ $$\text{Eq (2): } 12x - 6y = -6 \iff 2x - y = -1$$ Here the left-hand sides are identical while $3 \neq -1$, representing two parallel distinct lines. Hence $k = -6$ gives no solution.

Final Answer: (a) No solution for $k = -6$; (b) Infinitely many solutions for $k = 6$.

Level 1 Symmetric System Worked Example 2: High-Coefficient Interchanged System
Solve the following system of equations efficiently: $$152x - 378y = -74$$ $$-378x + 152y = -604$$

Step 1 — Recognize Coefficient Symmetry:

The coefficients $152$ and $-378$ are interchanged between the two equations. Instead of multiplying by $152$ and $378$, add and subtract the equations directly.

Step 2 — Add Eq (1) and Eq (2):

$$(152 - 378)x + (-378 + 152)y = -74 + (-604)$$ $$-226x - 226y = -678$$

Dividing both sides by $-226$ (since $226 \times 3 = 678$):

$$x + y = 3 \qquad \text{(3)}$$

Step 3 — Subtract Eq (2) from Eq (1):

$$\bigl(152 - (-378)\bigr)x + (-378 - 152)y = -74 - (-604)$$ $$530x - 530y = 530$$

Dividing both sides by $530$:

$$x - y = 1 \qquad \text{(4)}$$

Step 4 — Solve the Reduced Pair (3) & (4):

Adding (3) and (4): $2x = 4 \implies x = 2$. Substituting into (3): $2 + y = 3 \implies y = 1$.

Verification: $152(2) - 378(1) = 304 - 378 = -74$ and $-378(2) + 152(1) = -756 + 152 = -604$. Both match.

Final Answer: $(x, y) = (2, 1)$.

Level 1 Reducible + Cramer’s Rule Worked Example 3: Linearizing Reciprocal Binomial Denominators
Solve for real $(x, y)$: $$\frac{10}{x + y} + \frac{2}{x - y} = 4, \qquad \frac{15}{x + y} - \frac{5}{x - y} = -2$$

Step 1 — State Domain Restrictions & Substitute:

Denominators cannot vanish, so $x + y \neq 0$ and $x - y \neq 0$. Set $u = \dfrac{1}{x+y}$ and $v = \dfrac{1}{x-y}$ (with $u, v \neq 0$). The system linearizes to:

$$10u + 2v = 4 \iff 5u + v = 2 \qquad \text{(1)}$$ $$15u - 5v = -2 \qquad \text{(2)}$$

Step 2 — Solve for $(u, v)$ via $2 \times 2$ Cramer’s Rule:

$$\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 15 & -5 \end{vmatrix} = 5(-5) - 15(1) = -25 - 15 = -40$$ $$\Delta_u = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -2 & -5 \end{vmatrix} = 2(-5) - (-2)(1) = -10 + 2 = -8 \implies u = \frac{-8}{-40} = \frac{1}{5}$$ $$\Delta_v = \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 15 & -2 \end{vmatrix} = 5(-2) - 15(2) = -10 - 30 = -40 \implies v = \frac{-40}{-40} = 1$$

Step 3 — Back-Substitute & Verify Domain:

$$\frac{1}{x + y} = \frac{1}{5} \implies x + y = 5, \qquad \frac{1}{x - y} = 1 \implies x - y = 1$$

Adding gives $2x = 6 \implies x = 3$; subtracting gives $2y = 4 \implies y = 2$. Since $x+y = 5 \neq 0$ and $x-y = 1 \neq 0$, the domain conditions are satisfied.

Final Answer: $(x, y) = (3, 2)$.

LEVEL 2: EXAM-REAL — BEST / NTSE Stage 2 Traps & Multi-Concept Modeling
Level 2 Zero-Coefficient Trap Worked Example 4: Catching the Hidden Root Lost by Naive Ratio Cancellation
Find all real values of $m$ for which the following system of linear equations has (a) no solution, and (b) infinitely many solutions: $$(m - 1)x + 3y = m + 2$$ $$(m^2 - 1)x + (2m + 1)y = 2m + 4$$

Why Naive Ratio Division Fails Here:

A student who writes $\dfrac{m - 1}{m^2 - 1} = \dfrac{3}{2m + 1}$ and cancels $(m - 1)$ immediately gets $\frac{1}{m+1} = \frac{3}{2m+1} \implies m = -2$, completely losing the critical value $m = 1$ where both $x$-coefficients become $0$!

Step 1 — Factor the Determinant $\Delta = 0$ Without Cancelling:

$$\Delta = (m - 1)(2m + 1) - 3(m^2 - 1) = 0$$

Write $m^2 - 1 = (m - 1)(m + 1)$ and factor out $(m - 1)$ instead of dividing by it:

$$(m - 1)\bigl[(2m + 1) - 3(m + 1)\bigr] = 0$$ $$(m - 1)(-m - 2) = 0 \implies -(m - 1)(m + 2) = 0$$

Thus $\Delta = 0$ if and only if $m = 1$ or $m = -2$. (For all other real $m$, $\Delta \neq 0$ and the system has a unique solution.)

Step 2 — Test Both Candidates Directly in the Original Equations:

  • Test $m = 1$: $$\text{Eq (1): } 0 \cdot x + 3y = 3 \implies y = 1$$ $$\text{Eq (2): } 0 \cdot x + 3y = 6 \implies y = 2$$ These are two distinct parallel horizontal lines ($y = 1$ and $y = 2$). They never intersect, so $m = 1$ gives no solution.
  • Test $m = -2$: $$\text{Eq (1): } -3x + 3y = 0 \implies x - y = 0$$ $$\text{Eq (2): } 3x - 3y = 0 \implies x - y = 0$$ Both equations collapse to the exact same line $y = x$, so $m = -2$ gives infinitely many solutions.

Final Answer: (a) No solution solely at $m = 1$; (b) Infinitely many solutions solely at $m = -2$.

Level 2 Coordinate Geometry Area Worked Example 5: Triangle & First-Quadrant Quadrilateral Bounded by Two Lines
Two straight lines are defined by $L_1: 2x + y = 8$ and $L_2: x - 2y + 1 = 0$.
(a) Find the area of the triangle bounded by $L_1$, $L_2$, and the $x$-axis.
(b) Find the area of the quadrilateral bounded by $L_1$, $L_2$, and the positive $x$- and $y$-axes in Quadrant I.

Step 1 — Find the Intersection Point $P$ and All Axis Intercepts (see Figure 8.2):

  • Intersection $P$: From $L_2$, $x = 2y - 1$. Substituting into $L_1$: $$2(2y - 1) + y = 8 \implies 5y - 2 = 8 \implies y = 2, \quad x = 2(2) - 1 = 3$$ Hence $P = (3, 2)$, which lies strictly in Quadrant I.
  • $x$-intercepts (set $y = 0$): $L_1$ meets the $x$-axis at $A(4, 0)$; $L_2$ meets the $x$-axis at $B(-1, 0)$.
  • $y$-intercepts (set $x = 0$): $L_1$ meets the $y$-axis at $D(0, 8)$; $L_2$ meets the $y$-axis at $E\left(0, \frac{1}{2}\right)$.

Step 2 — Part (a): Area of $\triangle PAB$ with the $x$-Axis:

The base $AB$ runs along the $x$-axis from $x = -1$ to $x = 4$, so its length is $AB = |4 - (-1)| = 5$. The perpendicular height from $P(3, 2)$ to the $x$-axis is $h = |y_P| = 2$:

$$\text{Area}(\triangle PAB) = \frac{1}{2} \times AB \times |y_P| = \frac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 2 = 5 \text{ sq. units}$$

Step 3 — Part (b): Area of Quadrilateral $OAPE$ in Quadrant I:

Notice that the $y$-axis cuts $\triangle PAB$ along segment $OE$, removing the small right triangle $\triangle OBE$ lying in Quadrant II (with vertices $O(0,0)$, $B(-1,0)$, $E\left(0, \frac{1}{2}\right)$). Therefore:

$$\text{Area}(\triangle OBE) = \frac{1}{2} \times OB \times OE = \frac{1}{2} \times 1 \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4} \text{ sq. units}$$ $$\text{Area}(OAPE) = \text{Area}(\triangle PAB) - \text{Area}(\triangle OBE) = 5 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{19}{4} = 4.75 \text{ sq. units}$$

Independent Cross-Check via the $y$-Axis: $\text{Area}(OAPE) = \text{Area}(\triangle OAD) - \text{Area}(\triangle PDE) = \frac{1}{2}(4)(8) - \frac{1}{2}\left(8 - \frac{1}{2}\right)(3) = 16 - \frac{45}{4} = \frac{19}{4}$. Exact match!

Final Answer: (a) $5$ sq. units; (b) $\dfrac{19}{4}$ (or $4.75$) sq. units.

Level 2 Situational Modeling Worked Example 6: Boat-and-Stream Reducible Time System
A motorboat travels $30\text{ km}$ upstream and $44\text{ km}$ downstream in $10\text{ hours}$. In $13\text{ hours}$, it can travel $40\text{ km}$ upstream and $55\text{ km}$ downstream. Find the speed of the boat in still water and the speed of the stream.

Step 1 — Model Upstream & Downstream Speeds:

Let the speed of the boat in still water be $b\text{ km/h}$ and the speed of the stream be $s\text{ km/h}$ ($b > s > 0$). Then upstream speed $= b - s$ and downstream speed $= b + s$. Using $\text{Time} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Speed}}$:

$$\frac{30}{b - s} + \frac{44}{b + s} = 10 \qquad \text{(1)}$$ $$\frac{40}{b - s} + \frac{55}{b + s} = 13 \qquad \text{(2)}$$

Step 2 — Linearize & Exploit GCD Structure:

Set $u = \dfrac{1}{b - s}$ and $v = \dfrac{1}{b + s}$ ($u, v > 0$). Divide Eq (1) by $2$ first to shrink coefficients:

$$15u + 22v = 5 \qquad \text{(1')}$$ $$40u + 55v = 13 \qquad \text{(2')}$$

Notice that $\text{LCM}(22, 55) = 110$ (since $22 = 2 \times 11$ and $55 = 5 \times 11$). Multiply (1') by $5$ and (2') by $2$:

$$75u + 110v = 25$$ $$80u + 110v = 26$$

Subtracting the first from the second gives $5u = 1 \implies u = \dfrac{1}{5}$. Substituting $u = \frac{1}{5}$ into (1'):

$$15\left(\frac{1}{5}\right) + 22v = 5 \implies 3 + 22v = 5 \implies 22v = 2 \implies v = \frac{1}{11}$$

Step 3 — Recover $b$ and $s$:

$$b - s = \frac{1}{u} = 5, \qquad b + s = \frac{1}{v} = 11$$

Adding: $2b = 16 \implies b = 8\text{ km/h}$. Subtracting: $2s = 6 \implies s = 3\text{ km/h}$.

Verification: $\frac{30}{5} + \frac{44}{11} = 6 + 4 = 10\text{ h}$; $\frac{40}{5} + \frac{55}{11} = 8 + 5 = 13\text{ h}$.

Final Answer: Speed of boat in still water $= 8\text{ km/h}$; speed of stream $= 3\text{ km/h}$.

Level 3 Linear Diophantine Worked Example 7: Exact Counting of Positive Integer Solutions
Find the exact number of ordered pairs of positive integers $(x, y)$ that satisfy the equation $$7x + 11y = 2027.$$

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 — Check Bézout’s Condition: $\gcd(7, 11) = 1$, which divides $2027$, so integer solutions exist.

Step 2 — Find a Particular Solution $(x_0, y_0)$ Modulo the Smaller Coefficient ($7$):

Reduce $7x + 11y = 2027$ modulo $7$. Since $2027 = 7 \times 289 + 4$ and $11 \equiv 4 \pmod{7}$:

$$11y \equiv 2027 \pmod{7} \implies 4y \equiv 4 \pmod{7}$$

Since $\gcd(4, 7) = 1$, we can cancel $4$ to obtain $y \equiv 1 \pmod{7}$. Choose the smallest positive value $y_0 = 1$:

$$7x_0 = 2027 - 11(1) = 2016 \implies x_0 = \frac{2016}{7} = 288$$

Verify: $7(288) + 11(1) = 2016 + 11 = 2027$.

Step 3 — Write the Complete Parametric Family & Bound $t$:

$$x = 288 - 11t, \qquad y = 1 + 7t \qquad (t \in \mathbb{Z})$$

Enforce the positive-integer conditions $x \ge 1$ and $y \ge 1$:

  • $y \ge 1 \implies 1 + 7t \ge 1 \implies 7t \ge 0 \implies t \ge 0$.
  • $x \ge 1 \implies 288 - 11t \ge 1 \implies 11t \le 287 \implies t \le \dfrac{287}{11} = 26.09\ldots \implies t \le 26$.

Therefore, $t$ takes every integer value in $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots, 26\}$, giving $26 - 0 + 1 = 27$ ordered pairs (ranging from $(288, 1)$ at $t = 0$ down to $(2, 183)$ at $t = 26$).

30-Second Exam Trick (Quotient-Remainder Test):

Since $\gcd(7, 11) = 1$, divide $c = 2027$ by $ab = 7 \times 11 = 77$:

$$2027 = 77 \times 26 + 25 \implies q = 26,\quad r = 25$$

By the Quotient-Remainder Theorem, the positive integer count is $q + 1$ if $7x + 11y = r$ has a positive integer solution, and $q$ otherwise. Test $7x + 11y = 25$ mentally: $y = 1 \implies 7x = 14 \implies x = 2$ works! Hence the exact count is immediately $26 + 1 = 27$.

Final Answer: $27$ ordered pairs.

Level 3 Homogeneous 3-Variable Ratio Worked Example 8: Evaluating a Degree-2 Symmetric Ratio from Two Equations
If $x, y, z$ are non-zero real numbers satisfying $$2x - 3y + 4z = 0$$ $$3x + 2y - 5z = 0$$ find the exact simplified value of $\dfrac{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}{xy + yz + zx}$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Since $z \neq 0$, divide both equations by $z$ and set $p = \dfrac{x}{z}$ and $q = \dfrac{y}{z}$:

$$2p - 3q = -4 \qquad \text{(1)}$$ $$3p + 2q = 5 \qquad \text{(2)}$$

Eliminate $q$ by taking $2 \times \text{(1)} + 3 \times \text{(2)}$:

$$(4p - 6q) + (9p + 6q) = -8 + 15 \implies 13p = 7 \implies p = \frac{7}{13}$$

Substitute $p = \frac{7}{13}$ into Eq (2):

$$2q = 5 - 3\left(\frac{7}{13}\right) = \frac{65 - 21}{13} = \frac{44}{13} \implies q = \frac{22}{13}$$

Now divide numerator and denominator of the target expression by $z^2$:

$$\frac{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}{xy + yz + zx} = \frac{p^2 + q^2 + 1}{pq + q + p} = \frac{\left(\frac{7}{13}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{22}{13}\right)^2 + 1}{\left(\frac{7}{13}\right)\left(\frac{22}{13}\right) + \frac{22}{13} + \frac{7}{13}}$$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $13^2 = 169$:

$$= \frac{7^2 + 22^2 + 13^2}{7(22) + 22(13) + 13(7)} = \frac{49 + 484 + 169}{154 + 286 + 91} = \frac{702}{531}$$

Notice both $702$ and $531$ have digit sum $9$, so divide top and bottom by $9$:

$$\frac{702 \div 9}{531 \div 9} = \frac{78}{59}$$

Since $59$ is prime and $59 \nmid 78$, this fraction is in lowest terms.

30-Second Exam Trick (Cross-Product Ratio + Scale Invariance):

Skip $p, q$ fractions completely! Apply the Cross-Product Ratio Rule directly to the coefficient rows $[2,\ -3,\ 4]$ and $[3,\ 2,\ -5]$:

$$x : y : z = \bigl((-3)(-5) - (2)(4)\bigr) : \bigl((4)(3) - (-5)(2)\bigr) : \bigl((2)(2) - (3)(-3)\bigr) = 7 : 22 : 13$$

Because $\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{xy+yz+zx}$ is homogeneous of degree $2$ in both numerator and denominator, plug in $(x, y, z) = (7, 22, 13)$ directly to get $\frac{49+484+169}{154+286+91} = \frac{702}{531} = \frac{78}{59}$ in 25 seconds!

Final Answer: $\dfrac{78}{59}$.

Level 3 Parametric Area Synthesis Worked Example 9: Area-Partitioning Ray Through the Origin
The line $L_1: 3x + 4y = 24$ intersects the $x$-axis at $A$ and the $y$-axis at $B$. A line $L_2: y = kx$ (with $k > 0$) passes through the origin $O(0,0)$ and intersects segment $AB$ at point $P$. If $\text{Area}(\triangle OAP) = \dfrac{1}{3}\,\text{Area}(\triangle OAB)$, find the value of $k$ and the coordinates of $P$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 — Determine Intercepts & Total Triangle Area:

  • Setting $y = 0$ in $3x + 4y = 24$ gives $x = 8 \implies A(8, 0)$.
  • Setting $x = 0$ in $3x + 4y = 24$ gives $y = 6 \implies B(0, 6)$.
$$\text{Area}(\triangle OAB) = \frac{1}{2} \times OA \times OB = \frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 6 = 24 \text{ sq. units}$$

Hence the required area is $\text{Area}(\triangle OAP) = \dfrac{1}{3}(24) = 8$ sq. units.

Step 2 — Solve the Parametric System for $P(x_P, y_P)$:

Substitute $y = kx$ into $3x + 4y = 24$:

$$(3 + 4k)x_P = 24 \implies x_P = \frac{24}{3 + 4k}, \qquad y_P = kx_P = \frac{24k}{3 + 4k}$$

Step 3 — Equate $\text{Area}(\triangle OAP) = 8$ and Solve for $k$:

Since $O(0,0)$ and $A(8,0)$ lie on the $x$-axis and $k > 0 \implies y_P > 0$, $\triangle OAP$ has base $OA = 8$ and altitude $y_P$:

$$\frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times y_P = 8 \implies 4y_P = 8 \implies y_P = 2$$

Equating the two expressions for $y_P$:

$$\frac{24k}{3 + 4k} = 2 \implies 24k = 6 + 8k \implies 16k = 6 \implies k = \frac{3}{8}$$

Substituting $y_P = 2$ back into $3x_P + 4y_P = 24$ gives $3x_P + 8 = 24 \implies x_P = \dfrac{16}{3}$. Thus $P = \left(\dfrac{16}{3}, 2\right)$.

30-Second Exam Trick (Shared-Base Altitude Ratio / Section Formula):

$\triangle OAP$ and $\triangle OAB$ share the exact same base $OA$ along the $x$-axis! Therefore, the ratio of their areas equals the ratio of their $y$-coordinates directly:

$$\frac{y_P}{y_B} = \frac{\text{Area}(\triangle OAP)}{\text{Area}(\triangle OAB)} = \frac{1}{3} \implies y_P = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$

Plug $y_P = 2$ straight into $3x + 4y = 24 \implies x_P = \frac{16}{3}$, and read off the slope $k = \dfrac{y_P}{x_P} = \dfrac{2}{16/3} = \dfrac{3}{8}$. Zero parametric solving required!

Final Answer: $k = \dfrac{3}{8}$ and $P = \left(\dfrac{16}{3},\ 2\right)$.
