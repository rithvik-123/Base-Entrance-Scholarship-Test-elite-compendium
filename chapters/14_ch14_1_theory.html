14.1 Trigonometric Ratios of Acute Angles: First Principles & Standard Values

First-Principles Proof • Well-Definedness via AA Similarity

Why does $\sin 30^\circ$ equal $\frac{1}{2}$ in every right-angled triangle containing a $30^\circ$ angle, regardless of whether the hypotenuse is $2\text{ cm}$ or $200\text{ km}$? Trigonometry exists as a consistent function of an angle $\theta \in (0^\circ, 90^\circ)$ strictly because of AA Similarity.

Proof: Let $\angle XOY = \theta$ be any acute angle. Pick any three distinct points $B, D, F$ on the terminal ray $OY$, and drop perpendiculars $BA \perp OX$, $DC \perp OX$, and $FE \perp OX$. Consider $\triangle OAB$, $\triangle OCD$, and $\triangle OEF$:

  1. $\angle AOB = \angle COD = \angle EOF = \theta$ (common acute angle).
  2. $\angle OAB = \angle OCD = \angle OEF = 90^\circ$ (by construction).

By the AA Similarity Criterion, $\triangle OAB \sim \triangle OCD \sim \triangle OEF$. Therefore, the ratios of corresponding sides are invariant across all triangle sizes:

$$ \frac{BA}{OB} = \frac{DC}{OD} = \frac{FE}{OF} = \sin\theta, \qquad \frac{OA}{OB} = \frac{OC}{OD} = \frac{OE}{OF} = \cos\theta, \qquad \frac{BA}{OA} = \frac{DC}{OC} = \frac{FE}{OE} = \tan\theta $$

Thus, every trigonometric ratio depends solely on the angle $\theta$ and is completely independent of the scale of the right triangle.

Because the hypotenuse is strictly the longest side ($h \gt p$ and $h \gt b$), for any real acute angle $\theta$: $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ can never exceed $1$, while $\sec\theta$ and $\csc\theta$ can never lie strictly between $0$ and $1$. Also note: $\sin\theta$ is a function of $\theta$, not a product $\sin \times \theta$, and $\sin^2\theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^2$, whereas $\sin\theta^2$ means $\sin(\theta^2)$.

Complementary Angle Identities & Converting Between Ratios

In right $\triangle ABC$ with $\angle B = 90^\circ$ and $\angle A = \theta$, the remaining acute angle is $\angle C = 90^\circ - \theta$. The side opposite to $\theta$ ($BC$) is automatically the side adjacent to $90^\circ - \theta$, and vice versa. This directly yields the three co-function pairs:

$$ \sin(90^\circ - \theta) = \cos\theta, \qquad \cos(90^\circ - \theta) = \sin\theta $$ $$ \tan(90^\circ - \theta) = \cot\theta, \qquad \cot(90^\circ - \theta) = \tan\theta $$ $$ \sec(90^\circ - \theta) = \csc\theta, \qquad \csc(90^\circ - \theta) = \sec\theta $$
Core Technique • Expressing Any Ratio in Terms of Another

Standard Problem Pattern: Given $\cos\theta = \dfrac{a}{b}$ (where $0 \lt a \lt b$ and $\theta$ is acute), find $\csc\theta + \cot\theta$.

Method (Scale-Factor Right Triangle): Set base $B = ak$ and hypotenuse $H = bk$ ($k \gt 0$). By Pythagoras, the perpendicular is $P = \sqrt{b^2k^2 - a^2k^2} = k\sqrt{b^2 - a^2}$. Then:

$$ \csc\theta + \cot\theta = \frac{H}{P} + \frac{B}{P} = \frac{bk + ak}{k\sqrt{b^2 - a^2}} = \frac{b + a}{\sqrt{(b - a)(b + a)}} = \sqrt{\frac{b + a}{b - a}} $$

Always simplify radicals by factoring differences of squares ($b^2 - a^2 = (b-a)(b+a)$) and verify $b - a \gt 0$ so the denominator is strictly positive.

14.2 Fundamental Identities & Algebraic Power Weapons

The Three Pythagorean Identities & Exact Domain Restrictions

In any right triangle with sides $p, b, h$, Pythagoras gives $p^2 + b^2 = h^2$. Dividing sequentially by $h^2$, $b^2$, and $p^2$ yields:

  1. Divide by $h^2$: $\left(\frac{p}{h}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{b}{h}\right)^2 = 1 \implies \mathbf{\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1}$   (valid for all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$).
  2. Divide by $b^2$ ($\cos\theta \neq 0$): $\left(\frac{p}{b}\right)^2 + 1 = \left(\frac{h}{b}\right)^2 \implies \mathbf{1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta \iff \sec^2\theta - \tan^2\theta = 1}$   (valid for $\theta \neq (2n+1)90^\circ$).
  3. Divide by $p^2$ ($\sin\theta \neq 0$): $1 + \left(\frac{b}{p}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{h}{p}\right)^2 \implies \mathbf{1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta \iff \csc^2\theta - \cot^2\theta = 1}$   (valid for $\theta \neq n \cdot 180^\circ$).

Immediate Higher-Order Power Weapons (derived via $x^2+y^2 = (x+y)^2 - 2xy$ and $x^3+y^3 = (x+y)^3 - 3xy(x+y)$ with $x = \sin^2\theta, y = \cos^2\theta$):

$$ \sin^4\theta + \cos^4\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta, \qquad \sin^6\theta + \cos^6\theta = 1 - 3\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta $$ $$ \sec^4\theta - \tan^4\theta = (\sec^2\theta - \tan^2\theta)(\sec^2\theta + \tan^2\theta) = \sec^2\theta + \tan^2\theta = 1 + 2\tan^2\theta $$
Resonance Weapon 1 • The Reciprocal Conjugate Pair Shortcut

Because $\sec^2\theta - \tan^2\theta = 1$ factors as $(\sec\theta + \tan\theta)(\sec\theta - \tan\theta) = 1$, the two quantities $\sec\theta + \tan\theta$ and $\sec\theta - \tan\theta$ are exact multiplicative reciprocals (whenever $\cos\theta \neq 0$):

$$ \sec\theta + \tan\theta = p \iff \sec\theta - \tan\theta = \frac{1}{p} \qquad (p \neq 0) $$

Adding and subtracting these two linear equations solves for every trigonometric ratio in two lines:

$$ \sec\theta = \frac{p^2 + 1}{2p}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{p^2 - 1}{2p}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{2p}{p^2 + 1}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{\tan\theta}{\sec\theta} = \frac{p^2 - 1}{p^2 + 1} $$

Twin Identity for Cosecant–Cotangent (whenever $\sin\theta \neq 0$): Since $(\csc\theta + \cot\theta)(\csc\theta - \cot\theta) = 1$,

$$ \csc\theta + \cot\theta = q \iff \csc\theta - \cot\theta = \frac{1}{q} \implies \csc\theta = \frac{q^2 + 1}{2q}, \quad \cot\theta = \frac{q^2 - 1}{2q}, \quad \cos\theta = \frac{q^2 - 1}{q^2 + 1} $$

Olympiad Sightline: Whenever an exam problem shows $p^2 - 1$, $p^2 + 1$, and $2p$, recognize the Pythagorean triple $(p^2 - 1,\; 2p,\; p^2 + 1)$ immediately!

Resonance Weapon 2 • The Orthogonal Pair Identity

Theorem: If $a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta = c$, then swapping coefficients and flipping the interior sign produces:

$$ a\cos\theta - b\sin\theta = \pm\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 - c^2} $$

First-Principles Proof: Let $k = a\cos\theta - b\sin\theta$. Square both equations and add:

$$ (a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta)^2 + (a\cos\theta - b\sin\theta)^2 = c^2 + k^2 $$ $$ \left(a^2\sin^2\theta + b^2\cos^2\theta + 2ab\sin\theta\cos\theta\right) + \left(a^2\cos^2\theta + b^2\sin^2\theta - 2ab\sin\theta\cos\theta\right) = c^2 + k^2 $$

The cross-terms $\pm 2ab\sin\theta\cos\theta$ annihilate identically, leaving:

$$ a^2(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta) + b^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = c^2 + k^2 \implies a^2 + b^2 = c^2 + k^2 \implies k = \pm\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 - c^2} $$

Hidden Bonus: Because $k^2 = a^2 + b^2 - c^2 \ge 0$ for every real $\theta$, this single proof simultaneously proves $c^2 \le a^2 + b^2$, i.e., $|a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta| \le \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$!

Weapons 3 & 4 • Eliminating $\theta$ (Eliminants) & MCQ Value-Substitution Protocol

Weapon 3 — Eliminating $\theta$ from Two Equations: To produce an algebraic relation free of $\theta$, isolate two ratios linked by a Pythagorean identity and substitute:

  • Template A ($\sin/\cos$): $x = h + a\cos\theta,\; y = k + b\sin\theta \implies \left(\dfrac{x-h}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{y-k}{b}\right)^2 = 1$.
  • Template B ($\sec/\tan$): $x = a\sec\theta + b\tan\theta,\; y = a\tan\theta + b\sec\theta \implies x^2 - y^2 = a^2 - b^2$.
  • Template C ($\cot\pm\cos$ classic NTSE/IOQM): If $\cot\theta + \cos\theta = m$ and $\cot\theta - \cos\theta = n$, then $m^2 - n^2 = (m+n)(m-n) = (2\cot\theta)(2\cos\theta) = 4\cot\theta\cos\theta$, while $mn = \cot^2\theta - \cos^2\theta = \cos^2\theta\left(\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta} - 1\right) = \cos^2\theta\cot^2\theta$. Therefore $\mathbf{(m^2 - n^2)^2 = 16mn}$.

14.3 Out-of-Portion Bridge: Radian Measure, Unit Circle & Allied Angles

Resonance Bridge • Radian Measure & Unit Circle Coordinates

Radian Definition: One radian ($1\text{ rad}$) is the angle subtended at the center of a circle of radius $r$ by an arc of length $\ell = r$. In general, $\theta\text{ (in radians)} = \dfrac{\ell}{r}$. Since a full circle has circumference $2\pi r$, one complete revolution is $2\pi\text{ rad} = 360^\circ$, giving the master conversion bridge:

$$ \pi\text{ rad} = 180^\circ \implies 1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180}\text{ rad}, \qquad 1\text{ rad} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.2958^\circ $$

Unit Circle Extension Beyond $90^\circ$: Place a circle of radius $r = 1$ centered at the origin $O(0,0)$. Rotate the positive $x$-axis counterclockwise by any real angle $\theta$ (clockwise if $\theta \lt 0$) to locate a unique terminal point $P(x, y)$ on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. We define:

$$ \cos\theta = x, \qquad \sin\theta = y, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} \; (x \neq 0) $$

This seamlessly extends trigonometry to all real angles $\theta \in (-\infty, +\infty)$, preserves $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = x^2 + y^2 = 1$ everywhere, and establishes the ASTC Quadrant Sign Rule from the signs of $(x, y)$ in each quadrant.

14.4 Out-of-Portion Bridge: Compound Angles, Multiple Angles & Telescopic Cascades

First-Principles Derivation • The Master $\cos(A - B)$ Identity

Proof via Unit-Circle Chord Invariance: Place points $P(\cos A, \sin A)$ and $Q(\cos B, \sin B)$ on the unit circle. The central angle between radii $OP$ and $OQ$ is $|A - B|$. Rotating the entire chord $PQ$ rigidly by angle $-B$ around the origin maps $Q$ to $S(1, 0)$ and $P$ to $R(\cos(A - B), \sin(A - B))$. Since rotation preserves distance, $PQ^2 = RS^2$:

$$ (\cos A - \cos B)^2 + (\sin A - \sin B)^2 = (\cos(A - B) - 1)^2 + (\sin(A - B) - 0)^2 $$

Expand both sides and apply $\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi = 1$:

$$ (1 + 1 - 2\cos A\cos B - 2\sin A\sin B) = (1 + 1 - 2\cos(A - B)) $$

Canceling $2$ and dividing by $-2$ gives the Master Identity:

$$ \cos(A - B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B $$

Every other compound-angle formula follows deductively in one line:

  • Replace $B$ by $-B$:   $\mathbf{\cos(A + B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B}$.
  • Replace $A$ by $90^\circ - A$ in $\cos(A - B)$:   $\cos((90^\circ - A) - B) = \sin(A + B) = \mathbf{\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B}$.
  • Replace $B$ by $-B$:   $\mathbf{\sin(A - B) = \sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B}$.
  • Divide $\sin(A \pm B)$ by $\cos(A \pm B)$ and divide numerator & denominator by $\cos A\cos B$:
$$ \tan(A + B) = \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B}, \qquad \tan(A - B) = \frac{\tan A - \tan B}{1 + \tan A\tan B} $$

Domain Note: $\tan(A\pm B)$ requires $A, B, A\pm B \neq (2n+1)90^\circ$.

Exact Surd Values of $15^\circ$ and $75^\circ$

Writing $15^\circ = 45^\circ - 30^\circ$ and $75^\circ = 45^\circ + 30^\circ$ directly yields four high-frequency Olympiad constants:

Olympiad Signature Identity • The $A + B = 45^\circ$ Theorem

Theorem: Whenever $A + B = 45^\circ$ (or $225^\circ$, where $\tan(A+B) = 1$, with $A, B \neq 90^\circ$):

$$ \mathbf{(1 + \tan A)(1 + \tan B) = 2} \qquad \text{and equivalently} \qquad \mathbf{(\cot A - 1)(\cot B - 1) = 2} $$

Proof:

$$ \tan(A + B) = \tan 45^\circ = 1 \implies \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B} = 1 \implies \tan A + \tan B + \tan A\tan B = 1 $$

Add $1$ to both sides and factor by grouping:

$$ 1 + \tan A + \tan B + \tan A\tan B = 2 \implies (1 + \tan A)(1 + \tan B) = 2 $$

Dividing both sides by $\tan A\tan B$ yields $(\cot A + 1)(\cot B + 1) = 2\cot A\cot B \iff \cot A\cot B - \cot A - \cot B + 1 = 2 \iff (\cot A - 1)(\cot B - 1) = 2$.
Companion Difference Identity: If $A - B = 45^\circ$, then $\mathbf{(1 + \tan A)(1 - \tan B) = 2}$.

Double-Angle & Power-Reduction Identities

Setting $A = B = \theta$ in the compound-angle formulas yields the Double-Angle Engine (critical for simplifying quadratic trig terms and telescoping products):

$$ \sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac{2\tan\theta}{1 + \tan^2\theta} $$ $$ \cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1 = \frac{1 - \tan^2\theta}{1 + \tan^2\theta} $$ $$ \tan 2\theta = \frac{2\tan\theta}{1 - \tan^2\theta} \qquad (\theta \neq 45^\circ, 90^\circ) $$

Rearranging $\cos 2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$ gives the Power-Reduction Formulas:

$$ \sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}, \qquad \cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}, \qquad \tan^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{1 + \cos 2\theta} $$
Olympiad Cascade Engine • Telescopic Trig Products & Sums

$$ \cos\theta \cdot \cos 2\theta \cdot \cos 4\theta \cdots \cos(2^{n-1}\theta) = \frac{\sin(2^n\theta)}{2^n\sin\theta} $$

14.5 Olympiad Bridge: Maximum & Minimum Values

Auxiliary-Angle Proof • Exact Range of $a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta$

Theorem: For any real constants $a, b$ (not both zero) and all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$,

$$ -\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \;\le\; a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta \;\le\; +\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} $$

First-Principles Proof (The $R$-Formula): Define the strictly positive hypotenuse $R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \gt 0$. Then the ordered pair $\left(\dfrac{a}{R},\; \dfrac{b}{R}\right)$ satisfies $\left(\dfrac{a}{R}\right)^2 + \left(\dfrac{b}{R}\right)^2 = \dfrac{a^2+b^2}{a^2+b^2} = 1$, so it lies on the unit circle. Therefore, there exists a unique angle $\alpha \in [0, 2\pi)$ such that:

$$ \cos\alpha = \frac{a}{R}, \qquad \sin\alpha = \frac{b}{R} $$

Factor $R$ out of $a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta$:

$$ a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta = R\left(\frac{a}{R}\sin\theta + \frac{b}{R}\cos\theta\right) = R(\sin\theta\cos\alpha + \cos\theta\sin\alpha) = \mathbf{R\sin(\theta + \alpha)} $$

Since $\sin(\theta + \alpha)$ ranges continuously over $[-1, +1]$ as $\theta$ varies over $\mathbb{R}$, multiplying by $R \gt 0$ proves:

$$ -R \le a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta \le +R \iff -\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \le a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta \le +\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} $$

Shift Rule: Adding a constant $c$ shifts the entire interval rigidly: $c - \sqrt{a^2+b^2} \le a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta + c \le c + \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.
Caution: You CANNOT claim $\max(a\sin\theta + b\cos\theta) = |a| + |b|$ because $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ can never equal $1$ simultaneously!

AM-GM Extreme Bounds & The Boundary-Attainability Trap

For any positive real numbers $x, y \gt 0$, the AM-GM inequality states $x + y \ge 2\sqrt{xy}$, with equality if and only if $x = y$. Applying this to squared trigonometric ratios (with constants $a, b \gt 0$ and $\theta$ in the domain):

  1. Tangent–Cotangent Bound: Since $\tan^2\theta \cdot \cot^2\theta = 1$, $$ a\tan^2\theta + b\cot^2\theta \ge 2\sqrt{ab\tan^2\theta\cot^2\theta} = \mathbf{2\sqrt{ab}} $$ Equality is always attainable because $\tan^2\theta$ takes all values in $(0, \infty)$; set $\tan^2\theta = \sqrt{b/a}$.
  2. Secant–Cosecant Bound: Rewrite using $\sec^2\theta = 1 + \tan^2\theta$ and $\csc^2\theta = 1 + \cot^2\theta$: $$ a\sec^2\theta + b\csc^2\theta = (a + b) + \left(a\tan^2\theta + b\cot^2\theta\right) \ge a + b + 2\sqrt{ab} = \mathbf{(\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})^2} $$ Again, equality is always attainable at $\tan^2\theta = \sqrt{b/a}$.
  3. THE IOQM BOUNDARY TRAP ($a\sin^2\theta + b\csc^2\theta$): Naive AM-GM gives $2\sqrt{ab}$, requiring $a\sin^2\theta = b\csc^2\theta \iff \sin^2\theta = \sqrt{\frac{b}{a}}$. Because $0 \lt \sin^2\theta \le 1$, check whether $\sqrt{\frac{b}{a}} \le 1$:
    • If $b \le a$: $\sin^2\theta = \sqrt{b/a} \le 1$ is valid $\implies \mathbf{\min = 2\sqrt{ab}}$.
    • If $b \gt a$: $\sqrt{b/a} \gt 1$ is impossible! Letting $t = \sin^2\theta \in (0, 1]$, the function $h(t) = at + \frac{b}{t}$ satisfies $h(t) - (a+b) = \frac{(1-t)(b - at)}{t} \ge 0$ since $b \gt a \ge at$. Thus the true minimum occurs at the boundary $t = \sin^2\theta = 1$, giving $\mathbf{\min = a + b}$ (strictly greater than $2\sqrt{ab}$)!
Bounds on Higher Power Sums: $\sin^n\theta + \cos^n\theta$

Case 1 — Even Powers $S_{2k}(\theta) = \sin^{2k}\theta + \cos^{2k}\theta$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}^+$): Let $x = \sin^2\theta \in [0, 1]$, so $\cos^2\theta = 1 - x$.

  • Upper Bound: Because $0 \le x \le 1$ and $k \ge 1$, raising to power $k$ never increases a number in $[0,1]$: $x^k \le x$ and $(1-x)^k \le 1-x$. Adding gives $x^k + (1-x)^k \le x + (1-x) = 1$, attained at $\theta = 0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$.
  • Lower Bound (Explicit for $k = 2, 3$): $$ \sin^4\theta + \cos^4\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = 1 - \frac{1}{2}\sin^2 2\theta \in \left[\mathbf{\frac{1}{2}},\; 1\right] $$ $$ \sin^6\theta + \cos^6\theta = 1 - 3\sin^2\theta\cos^2\theta = 1 - \frac{3}{4}\sin^2 2\theta \in \left[\mathbf{\frac{1}{4}},\; 1\right] $$ In general, by the Power-Mean inequality, $\left(\frac{x^k + (1-x)^k}{2}\right)^{1/k} \ge \frac{x + (1-x)}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \implies \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{2^{k-1}} \le \sin^{2k}\theta + \cos^{2k}\theta \le 1}$, with the minimum achieved at $x = \frac{1}{2}$ ($\theta = 45^\circ$).

Case 2 — Odd Powers $S_{2k+1}(\theta) = \sin^{2k+1}\theta + \cos^{2k+1}\theta$ ($k \ge 1$, e.g., $\sin^3\theta + \cos^3\theta$):

  • Over all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$: $|\sin^{2k+1}\theta + \cos^{2k+1}\theta| \le \sin^2\theta|\sin\theta| + \cos^2\theta|\cos\theta| \le \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$, attained at $\theta = 0^\circ$ ($+1$) and $\theta = 180^\circ$ ($-1$). Hence range is $\mathbf{[-1, 1]}$.
  • Restricted to acute angles $\theta \in [0^\circ, 90^\circ]$: for $\sin^3\theta + \cos^3\theta = (\sin\theta + \cos\theta)(1 - \sin\theta\cos\theta)$, letting $u = \sin\theta + \cos\theta \in [1, \sqrt{2}]$ gives $\sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac{u^2-1}{2}$ and $\sin^3\theta + \cos^3\theta = \frac{u(3 - u^2)}{2}$, which decreases strictly on $[1, \sqrt{2}]$. Hence on $[0^\circ, 90^\circ]$, $\mathbf{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \le \sin^3\theta + \cos^3\theta \le 1}$.

Level 1 Worked Example 1 • Ratio Construction
If $\theta$ is an acute angle such that $17\cos\theta = 8$, find the exact value of: $$ E = \frac{15(\csc\theta + \cot\theta)}{17\sin\theta + 8\tan\theta} + \sec^2\theta - \tan^2\theta $$

Step 1 — Construct the Reference Right Triangle: From $17\cos\theta = 8$, we obtain $\cos\theta = \dfrac{8}{17} = \dfrac{\text{base}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$. Let base $b = 8k$ and hypotenuse $h = 17k$ for some scale factor $k \gt 0$. By Pythagoras' theorem:

$$ p = \sqrt{h^2 - b^2} = \sqrt{(17k)^2 - (8k)^2} = k\sqrt{289 - 64} = k\sqrt{225} = 15k $$

Since $\theta$ is acute, all six trigonometric ratios are well-defined and strictly positive:

$$ \sin\theta = \frac{15}{17}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{15}{8}, \qquad \csc\theta = \frac{17}{15}, \qquad \cot\theta = \frac{8}{15} $$

Step 2 — Evaluate Each Block Separately:

$$ 15(\csc\theta + \cot\theta) = 15\left(\frac{17}{15} + \frac{8}{15}\right) = 17 + 8 = 25 $$ $$ 17\sin\theta + 8\tan\theta = 17\left(\frac{15}{17}\right) + 8\left(\frac{15}{8}\right) = 15 + 15 = 30 $$

Also, by the fundamental identity (valid since $\theta \neq 90^\circ$), $\sec^2\theta - \tan^2\theta = 1$ (no need to square $\frac{17}{8}$ and $\frac{15}{8}$ manually!).

Step 3 — Combine:

$$ E = \frac{25}{30} + 1 = \frac{5}{6} + 1 = \mathbf{\frac{11}{6}} $$
Level 1 Worked Example 2 • Standard & Complementary Angles
Compute the exact numerical value of: $$ K = \frac{4\sin^2 60^\circ + 3\tan^2 30^\circ - 2\sin 45^\circ\cos 45^\circ}{\sec^2 30^\circ - \cot^2 60^\circ} + \left(\tan 12^\circ \tan 38^\circ \tan 52^\circ \tan 78^\circ\right) + \frac{\sin 23^\circ}{\cos 67^\circ} $$

Step 1 — Evaluate the Standard-Angle Fraction: Substitute exact values from the reference triangles: $\sin 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, $\tan 30^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$, $\sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, $\sec 30^\circ = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$, and $\cot 60^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$:

$$ \text{Numerator} = 4\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 + 3\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^2 - 2\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = 4\left(\frac{3}{4}\right) + 3\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) - 2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = 3 + 1 - 1 = 3 $$ $$ \text{Denominator} = \left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = 1 $$

Thus the first fraction equals $\dfrac{3}{1} = 3$.

Step 2 — Pair Complementary Angles: Notice $12^\circ + 78^\circ = 90^\circ$ and $38^\circ + 52^\circ = 90^\circ$. Using $\tan(90^\circ - \theta) = \cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta}$:

$$ \tan 12^\circ \tan 38^\circ \tan 52^\circ \tan 78^\circ = \left(\tan 12^\circ \cdot \cot 12^\circ\right)\left(\tan 38^\circ \cdot \cot 38^\circ\right) = 1 \cdot 1 = 1 $$

Similarly, since $23^\circ + 67^\circ = 90^\circ$, $\cos 67^\circ = \cos(90^\circ - 23^\circ) = \sin 23^\circ \neq 0$, so:

$$ \frac{\sin 23^\circ}{\cos 67^\circ} = \frac{\sin 23^\circ}{\sin 23^\circ} = 1 $$

Step 3 — Sum All Three Terms:

$$ K = 3 + 1 + 1 = \mathbf{5} $$
Level 1 Worked Example 3 • Radical Identity & Domain Check
For an acute angle $\theta \in (0^\circ, 90^\circ)$, prove that: $$ \sqrt{\frac{1 + \sin\theta}{1 - \sin\theta}} + \sqrt{\frac{1 - \sin\theta}{1 + \sin\theta}} = 2\sec\theta $$ Hence evaluate the left-hand side when $\cos\theta = \dfrac{3}{5}$.

Step 1 — Verify Strict Positivity Inside Radicals: Since $0^\circ \lt \theta \lt 90^\circ$, we have $0 \lt \sin\theta \lt 1$ and $\cos\theta \gt 0$. Therefore, $1 + \sin\theta \gt 0$ and $1 - \sin\theta \gt 0$, so both denominators are non-zero and both radicands are strictly positive.

Step 2 — Rationalize Each Radical Using $\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta$:

$$ \sqrt{\frac{1 + \sin\theta}{1 - \sin\theta}} = \sqrt{\frac{(1 + \sin\theta)^2}{(1 - \sin\theta)(1 + \sin\theta)}} = \sqrt{\frac{(1 + \sin\theta)^2}{1 - \sin^2\theta}} = \sqrt{\frac{(1 + \sin\theta)^2}{\cos^2\theta}} = \frac{|1 + \sin\theta|}{|\cos\theta|} = \frac{1 + \sin\theta}{\cos\theta} $$

where removing the absolute value bars without a minus sign is justified strictly because $1 + \sin\theta \gt 0$ and $\cos\theta \gt 0$. Identically:

$$ \sqrt{\frac{1 - \sin\theta}{1 + \sin\theta}} = \sqrt{\frac{(1 - \sin\theta)^2}{1 - \sin^2\theta}} = \frac{|1 - \sin\theta|}{|\cos\theta|} = \frac{1 - \sin\theta}{\cos\theta} $$

Step 3 — Add & Substitute $\cos\theta = \frac{3}{5}$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{1 + \sin\theta}{\cos\theta} + \frac{1 - \sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{2}{\cos\theta} = 2\sec\theta \quad (\text{Proved}) $$

At $\cos\theta = \dfrac{3}{5}$, we have $\sec\theta = \dfrac{5}{3}$, so the expression evaluates to $2 \cdot \dfrac{5}{3} = \mathbf{\dfrac{10}{3}}$.

  • Hence $\sin\theta = \tan\theta \cdot \cos\theta = \dfrac{12}{5} \cdot \dfrac{5}{13} = \dfrac{12}{13}$. (Quick sanity check: $5^2 + 12^2 = 13^2$, and both $\sin\theta, \cos\theta \in (0,1)$, confirming $\theta$ is a valid acute angle.)

(a) If $3\sin\theta + 5\cos\theta = 5$, find all possible real values of $5\sin\theta - 3\cos\theta$, and verify that each value is actually attained by a real angle $\theta$.

Part (a) — Squaring & Adding Orthogonal Combinations: Let $k = 5\sin\theta - 3\cos\theta$. Square both equations:

$$ (3\sin\theta + 5\cos\theta)^2 = 25 \implies 9\sin^2\theta + 25\cos^2\theta + 30\sin\theta\cos\theta = 25 $$ $$ (5\sin\theta - 3\cos\theta)^2 = k^2 \implies 25\sin^2\theta + 9\cos^2\theta - 30\sin\theta\cos\theta = k^2 $$

Add both equations so the cross-terms $\pm 30\sin\theta\cos\theta$ cancel identically:

$$ 34(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta) = 25 + k^2 \implies 34(1) = 25 + k^2 \implies k^2 = 9 \implies k = \mathbf{\pm 3} $$

  • For $k = -3$: $\sin\theta = 0,\; \cos\theta = 1$ (attained at $\theta = 0^\circ$).

$$ x^2 - y^2 = a^2(\sec^2\theta - \tan^2\theta) - b^2(\sec^2\theta - \tan^2\theta) = a^2(1) - b^2(1) \implies \mathbf{x^2 - y^2 = a^2 - b^2} $$

Level 3 Worked Example 7 • $A + B = 45^\circ$ Product Cascade
If the trigonometric product $$ P = (1 + \tan 1^\circ)(1 + \tan 2^\circ)(1 + \tan 3^\circ)\cdots(1 + \tan 44^\circ)(1 + \tan 45^\circ) $$ equals $2^n$, find the exact integer value of $n$.

Full First-Principles Derivation:

Step 1 — Prove the Pairing Lemma: For any two acute angles $A, B$ satisfying $A + B = 45^\circ$, take the tangent of both sides (all angles lie in $[1^\circ, 44^\circ]$, safely away from $90^\circ$):

$$ \tan(A + B) = \tan 45^\circ = 1 \implies \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B} = 1 \implies \tan A + \tan B = 1 - \tan A\tan B $$

Move $-\tan A\tan B$ to the left and add $1$ to both sides:

$$ 1 + \tan A + \tan B + \tan A\tan B = 2 \implies (1 + \tan A)(1 + \tan B) = 2 $$

Step 2 — Partition the 45 Factors into Complementary $45^\circ$-Pairs: Isolate the final factor $(1 + \tan 45^\circ) = 1 + 1 = 2$. Pair the remaining $44$ factors from the outside in:

$$ P = \Big[(1+\tan 1^\circ)(1+\tan 44^\circ)\Big]\Big[(1+\tan 2^\circ)(1+\tan 43^\circ)\Big]\cdots\Big[(1+\tan 22^\circ)(1+\tan 23^\circ)\Big]\cdot(1+\tan 45^\circ) $$

Step 3 — Count Factors & Collapse: There are exactly $\dfrac{44}{2} = 22$ bracketed pairs, each equal to $2$, multiplied by the standalone factor $(1 + \tan 45^\circ) = 2$:

$$ P = \underbrace{2 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 2}_{22\text{ pairs}} \times 2 = 2^{22} \times 2^1 = 2^{23} \implies n = \mathbf{23} $$
30-Second BEST Exam Shortcut: Whenever you see $\prod_{k=1}^{45}(1 + \tan k^\circ)$, never expand! Every pair summing to $45^\circ$ contributes a factor of $2$, and $45^\circ$ itself contributes $(1 + 1) = 2$. Count $= \frac{44}{2} + 1 = 23 \implies 2^{23}$. (Watch for the trap: if the product starts at $0^\circ$, $(1 + \tan 0^\circ) = 1$, which does not change the power of $2$; if it stops at $44^\circ$, the answer drops to $22$.)

Level 3 Worked Example 9 • Auxiliary-Angle & AM-GM Extrema
(a) Find the exact maximum and minimum values of $f(\theta) = 5\cos\theta + 3\cos\!\left(\theta + \dfrac{\pi}{3}\right) + 4$ for all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$.
(b) Find the minimum value of $g(\theta) = 9\sec^2\theta + 16\csc^2\theta$ for $\theta \in \left(0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$, and state the exact value of $\tan\theta$ at which this minimum occurs.

Part (a) — Compound Expansion + Auxiliary-Angle $R$-Formula:

Expand $\cos\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$ using $\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$:

$$ \cos\!\left(\theta + \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\theta\cos\frac{\pi}{3} - \sin\theta\sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}\cos\theta - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\sin\theta $$

Substitute into $f(\theta)$ and group $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ terms:

$$ f(\theta) = 5\cos\theta + 3\left(\frac{1}{2}\cos\theta - \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\sin\theta\right) + 4 = \frac{13}{2}\cos\theta - \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\sin\theta + 4 $$

Compute the resultant amplitude $R = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ with $a = -\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $b = \dfrac{13}{2}$:

$$ R = \sqrt{\left(-\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{13}{2}\right)^2} = \sqrt{\frac{27}{4} + \frac{169}{4}} = \sqrt{\frac{196}{4}} = \sqrt{49} = 7 $$

Since $-R \le \frac{13}{2}\cos\theta - \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\sin\theta \le +R$, shifting by $+4$ yields $-7 + 4 \le f(\theta) \le 7 + 4$, so $\mathbf{f_{\max} = 11}$ and $\mathbf{f_{\min} = -3}$.

Part (b) — Pythagorean Decomposition + AM-GM:

Convert both terms into reciprocal squared functions $\tan^2\theta$ and $\cot^2\theta$:

$$ g(\theta) = 9(1 + \tan^2\theta) + 16(1 + \cot^2\theta) = 25 + 9\tan^2\theta + 16\cot^2\theta $$

Because $\theta \in (0, \pi/2)$, both $9\tan^2\theta \gt 0$ and $16\cot^2\theta \gt 0$. Apply AM-GM:

$$ 9\tan^2\theta + 16\cot^2\theta \ge 2\sqrt{(9\tan^2\theta)(16\cot^2\theta)} = 2\sqrt{144} = 24 $$

Therefore $g(\theta) \ge 25 + 24 = \mathbf{49}$. Check equality attainability:

$$ 9\tan^2\theta = 16\cot^2\theta = \frac{16}{\tan^2\theta} \implies \tan^4\theta = \frac{16}{9} \implies \tan^2\theta = \frac{4}{3} \implies \tan\theta = \mathbf{\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}} $$

(Positive root selected since $\theta \in (0, \pi/2)$; check directly: $9\left(1+\frac{4}{3}\right) + 16\left(1+\frac{3}{4}\right) = 21 + 28 = 49$.)

30-Second BEST Exam Shortcut:
For Part (a): Treat $p\cos\theta + q\cos(\theta + \phi)$ as two vectors of magnitudes $p = 5$, $q = 3$ separated by angle $\phi = 60^\circ$. Their resultant magnitude is $R = \sqrt{p^2 + q^2 + 2pq\cos\phi} = \sqrt{25 + 9 + 30(1/2)} = \sqrt{49} = 7$, giving range $[4 - 7,\; 4 + 7] = \mathbf{[-3,\; 11]}$.
For Part (b): $\min(a\sec^2\theta + b\csc^2\theta) = (\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b})^2 = (\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16})^2 = 7^2 = \mathbf{49}$, attained at $\tan\theta = \left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\dfrac{16}{9}\right)^{1/4} = \mathbf{\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}}$.
