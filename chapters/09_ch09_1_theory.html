1. Standard Form, Solving Methods & The $a = 0$ Degeneracy Trap

A quadratic equation in the variable $x$ over $\mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$) has the canonical form:

$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0, \qquad a \neq 0$$

Here $a$ is the leading coefficient, $b$ is the linear coefficient, and $c$ is the constant term. By the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, every genuine quadratic ($a \neq 0$) has exactly two roots in $\mathbb{C}$ (counted with multiplicity).

Critical Warning — The $a = 0$ Linear / Identity Degeneracy Trap

Whenever a problem asks about an equation $f(x) = a(k)x^2 + b(k)x + c(k) = 0$ (without explicitly calling it a quadratic equation), you MUST check the boundary parameter values where $a(k) = 0$ separately:

  • Case $a = 0,\ b \neq 0$: The equation degenerates to a linear equation $bx + c = 0$, having exactly one real root $x = -c/b$.
  • Case $a = 0,\ b = 0,\ c \neq 0$: Contradiction $c = 0 \implies$ no solution (0 roots).
  • Case $a = 0,\ b = 0,\ c = 0$: The equation becomes the identity $0 = 0$, satisfied by infinitely many (all real) values of $x$!

Identity Theorem Corollary: If a quadratic expression $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ is satisfied by more than two distinct values of $x$, it CANNOT be a quadratic—it MUST be the zero identity $a = b = c = 0$.

1.1 Completing the Square & Sridharacharya’s Formula (First-Principles Derivation)

Assuming $a \neq 0$, divide $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ by $a$ and isolate the constant term:

$$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a}$$

Add $\left(\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}$ to both sides to form a perfect square on the left:

$$\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}$$

Define the Discriminant $D = b^2 - 4ac$. Taking square roots of both sides gives Sridharacharya’s Quadratic Formula:

$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a}$$

Three Solving Tiers in Practice:

  1. Splitting the Middle Term / Factoring: Fastest when $ac$ has an obvious factor pair summing to $b$.
  2. Completing the Square: Optimal for finding vertex/extreme values ($a(x - h)^2 + k$) or symmetric parametric equations like $x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + a - 3) = 0$.
  3. Sridharacharya Formula: Universal solver; also yields the exact root gap immediately: $|\alpha - \beta| = \dfrac{\sqrt{D}}{|a|}$ (for $D \ge 0$).

2. Deep Nature of Roots, Conjugate Pairs & Rational/Integer Root Theorems

The nature of the roots $\alpha, \beta$ depends jointly on the sign and arithmetic structure of $D = b^2 - 4ac$ AND the number field of the coefficients ($a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, or $\mathbb{Z}$).

Coefficient Field Discriminant Condition Exact Nature of Roots $\alpha, \beta$ Pairing / Symmetry Property
$a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ $D > 0$ Real and distinct $|\alpha - \beta| = \dfrac{\sqrt{D}}{|a|} > 0$
$a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ $D = 0$ Real and equal (repeated) $\alpha = \beta = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$
$a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ $D < 0$ Non-real (complex) Complex conjugate pair: $p \pm iq$ ($p, q \in \mathbb{R},\ q \neq 0$)
$a, b, c \in \mathbb{Q}$ $D > 0$ and $D = r^2$ ($r \in \mathbb{Q}$) Rational and distinct Both $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{Q}$
$a, b, c \in \mathbb{Q}$ $D > 0$, NOT a rational square Irrational and distinct Irrational conjugate pair: $p \pm \sqrt{q}$ ($p, q \in \mathbb{Q},\ \sqrt{q} \notin \mathbb{Q}$)
$a = 1,\; b, c \in \mathbb{Z}$ $D = m^2$ ($m \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$) Integer roots ($\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{Z}$) $m$ automatically has the same parity as $b$ since $m^2 = b^2 - 4c \equiv b^2 \pmod{2}$
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Two Classic Conjugate & Integer-Root Traps

Trap 1 (Irrational Coefficients Break Conjugate Pairing): The rule “if $2 + \sqrt{3}$ is a root, then $2 - \sqrt{3}$ must be the other root” holds ONLY when $a, b, c \in \mathbb{Q}$! For example, $(x - 2 - \sqrt{3})(x - 1) = x^2 - (3 + \sqrt{3})x + (2 + \sqrt{3}) = 0$ has $2 + \sqrt{3}$ as a root, yet its second root is $1$, NOT $2 - \sqrt{3}$. Similarly, complex conjugate pairing $p \pm iq$ requires $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$.

Trap 2 (Odd-Coefficient Integer-Root Impossibility Theorem): If $a, b, c$ are ALL odd integers, then $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ can NEVER have a rational root!

3-Line Olympiad Proof: Suppose $D = b^2 - 4ac$ is the square of an integer $k$. Since $b$ is odd, $b^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$, while $a, c$ odd implies $4ac \equiv 4 \pmod{8}$. Therefore $D = b^2 - 4ac \equiv 1 - 4 \equiv 5 \pmod{8}$. However, every odd square satisfies $k^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{8}$ (never $5 \pmod{8}$)! Contradiction—hence $\sqrt{D}$ is always irrational.

3. Equations Reducible to Quadratics & Extraneous-Root Filtering

Higher-degree polynomials, exponentials, and radical equations regularly disguise a quadratic core. Every substitution carries a mandatory range constraint that filters out fake roots:

Equation Archetype Canonical Substitution Range / Domain Filter (Mandatory!)
Symmetric Reciprocal Quartic
$ax^4 + bx^3 + cx^2 + bx + a = 0$		 Divide by $x^2$ ($x \neq 0$), set $t = x + \dfrac{1}{x}$, so $x^2 + \dfrac{1}{x^2} = t^2 - 2$. For real $x$, $|t| = \left|x + \dfrac{1}{x}\right| \ge 2$ (by AM-GM). Reject any $|t| < 2$!
Anti-Symmetric Reciprocal
$ax^4 + bx^3 + cx^2 - bx + a = 0$		 Divide by $x^2$, set $t = x - \dfrac{1}{x}$, so $x^2 + \dfrac{1}{x^2} = t^2 + 2$. Every $t \in \mathbb{R}$ gives real roots (since $x^2 - tx - 1 = 0$ has $D = t^2 + 4 > 0$).
Exponential Quadratic
$A(b^x)^2 + B(b^x) + C = 0$ ($b > 0, b \neq 1$)		 Set $u = b^x$, yielding $Au^2 + Bu + C = 0$. Strictly $u > 0$! Reject $u \le 0$ immediately before taking logarithms.
Radical / Surd Equation
$\sqrt{P(x)} = Q(x)$		 Square both sides: $P(x) = [Q(x)]^2$. Require $Q(x) \ge 0$ (since principal $\sqrt{\cdot} \ge 0$). Squaring destroys signs and creates extraneous roots!
Paired Product Quartic
$(x+a)(x+b)(x+c)(x+d) = k$		 Pair factors with equal sum $a+b = c+d$ (or equal product $ab = cd$). Set $t = x^2 + (a+b)x$ (or divide by $x^2$ for equal products). Check real $x$ via $D \ge 0$.
Mini-Blueprint — Exponential Quadratic Filter

Consider $9^x - 2 \cdot 3^{x+1} - 27 = 0$. Rewrite $9^x = (3^x)^2$ and $3^{x+1} = 3 \cdot 3^x$, then let $u = 3^x > 0$:

$$u^2 - 6u - 27 = 0 \implies (u - 9)(u + 3) = 0 \implies u = 9 \quad \text{or} \quad u = -3$$

Since $3^x > 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, we immediately reject $u = -3$. Only $3^x = 9 = 3^2$ survives, giving the unique real root $x = 2$.

4. Quadratic Graph, All 6 Parabola Cases & Extreme Values

Consider the quadratic function $y = f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ ($a \neq 0$). Completing the square exposes its exact geometry:

$$y = a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 - \frac{D}{4a}, \qquad \text{where } D = b^2 - 4ac$$

The graph is a vertical parabola symmetric about the vertical axis $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$, with Vertex $V = \left(-\dfrac{b}{2a},\; -\dfrac{D}{4a}\right)$ and $y$-intercept $(0, c)$:

  • If $a > 0$ (opens upward ∪): Since $a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 \ge 0$, $f(x)$ attains its global minimum value $y_{\min} = -\dfrac{D}{4a}$ at $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$, and has no maximum (Range $= \left[-\dfrac{D}{4a},\ \infty\right)$).
  • If $a < 0$ (opens downward ∩): Since $a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 \le 0$, $f(x)$ attains its global maximum value $y_{\max} = -\dfrac{D}{4a}$ at $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$, and has no minimum (Range $= \left(-\infty,\ -\dfrac{D}{4a}\right]$).
Figure 9.1: All six geometric states of $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$. Notice the universal sign law: between real roots $(\alpha, \beta)$, $f(x)$ has the opposite sign of $a$; outside $[\alpha, \beta]$, $f(x)$ has the same sign as $a$.
Positive-Definite & Negative-Definite Criteria (High-Yield BEST Rule)
  • $ax^2 + bx + c > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R} \iff \mathbf{a > 0 \text{ AND } D < 0}$ (plus check degenerate case $a=b=0,\ c> 0$!).
  • $ax^2 + bx + c \ge 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R} \iff \mathbf{a > 0 \text{ AND } D \le 0}$ (plus $a = b = 0,\ c \ge 0$).
  • $ax^2 + bx + c < 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R} \iff \mathbf{a < 0 \text{ AND } D < 0}$ (plus $a=b=0,\ c < 0$).
  • $ax^2 + bx + c \le 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R} \iff \mathbf{a < 0 \text{ AND } D \le 0}$ (plus $a=b=0,\ c \le 0$).

5. Common Root Conditions (Both Roots vs. Exactly One Common Root)

Consider two quadratic equations with $a_1, a_2 \neq 0$:

$$a_1 x^2 + b_1 x + c_1 = 0 \qquad \text{(1)}$$ $$a_2 x^2 + b_2 x + c_2 = 0 \qquad \text{(2)}$$

5.1 Condition for Both Roots Common

Two quadratics share the exact same pair of roots $(\alpha, \beta)$ if and only if one polynomial is a non-zero scalar multiple of the other, i.e., $a_1 : b_1 : c_1 = a_2 : b_2 : c_2$:

$$\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} \qquad (\text{when } a_2, b_2, c_2 \neq 0)$$

In general (handling possible zero coefficients safely), both roots are common $\iff a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = 0$, $b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1 = 0$, and $c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1 = 0$.

5.2 Condition for Exactly One Common Root (First-Principles Derivation)

Suppose $\alpha$ is a common root, so both $a_1 \alpha^2 + b_1 \alpha + c_1 = 0$ and $a_2 \alpha^2 + b_2 \alpha + c_2 = 0$ hold simultaneously. Treat $\alpha^2$ and $\alpha$ as two linear unknowns and apply $2 \times 2$ cross-multiplication (Cramer’s Rule from Chapter 8!):

$$\frac{\alpha^2}{b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1} = \frac{\alpha}{c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1} = \frac{1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}$$

Assuming $a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \neq 0$ (otherwise $\alpha^2$ and $\alpha$ ratios collapse to the identical-equation check), this immediately gives TWO expressions for $\alpha$ and $\alpha^2$:

$$\alpha = \frac{c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}, \qquad \alpha^2 = \frac{b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}$$

Eliminating $\alpha$ via $(\alpha)^2 = \alpha^2$ produces the exact Sylvester Resultant Condition for a common root:

$$(c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1)^2 = (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)(b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1)$$

Operational Shortcut: In exams, rather than memorizing the raw formula blindly, simply eliminate $x^2$ by taking $a_2 \cdot (\text{Eq 1}) - a_1 \cdot (\text{Eq 2})$ to find the linear formula for $\alpha = -\dfrac{a_2 c_1 - a_1 c_2}{a_2 b_1 - a_1 b_2}$, and plug $\alpha$ back into either quadratic!

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Two Lethal Common-Root Traps

Trap 1 (Both Roots Disguised as One): Suppose $x^2 + kx + 1 = 0$ and $x^2 + x + k = 0$ have exactly one common root. Subtracting gives $(k - 1)x + (1 - k) = 0 \implies (k - 1)(x - 1) = 0$. Do NOT blindly accept $k = 1$! At $k = 1$, BOTH equations become $x^2 + x + 1 = 0$, so they share both roots (which are also non-real!). For exactly one common root, $k \neq 1 \implies x = 1 \implies 1 + k + 1 = 0 \implies k = -2$ only.

Trap 2 (Non-Real Common Root Forces BOTH Roots Common!): If two quadratics with real coefficients share a single non-real root $p + iq$ ($q \neq 0$), then by the Complex Conjugate Theorem both equations MUST also share $p - iq$—so they automatically share both roots ($\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2}$)! The exact same law applies if two quadratics with rational coefficients share an irrational root $p + \sqrt{q}$!

6. Location of Roots (Resonance / Olympiad Bridge)

Location of Roots translates inequalities on the roots $\alpha \le \beta$ of $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ ($a, b, c \in \mathbb{R},\ a \neq 0$) into elementary algebraic conditions on just three quantities: the Discriminant $D$, the signed endpoint value $a \cdot f(k)$, and the vertex $x$-coordinate $x_v = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$.

Why multiply by $a$? Notice that $a \cdot f(x) = a^2 x^2 + abx + ac$ ALWAYS opens upward! Thus writing $a \cdot f(k) > 0$ or $a \cdot f(k) < 0$ makes every condition automatically valid for both $a > 0$ and $a < 0$ without having to memorize separate cases!

Figure 9.2: Complete geometric summary of the four core Location of Roots configurations. Multiplying endpoint values by $a$ makes every condition invariant under sign flips of $a$.
Desired Root Configuration ($\alpha \le \beta$) Exact Necessary & Sufficient Conditions Why Each Condition Is Indispensable
(i) Both roots $> k$ $D \ge 0$, $\; a \cdot f(k) > 0$, $\; -\dfrac{b}{2a} > k$ $D \ge 0$ forces real roots; $a f(k) > 0$ puts $k$ outside $[\alpha, \beta]$; vertex $> k$ rules out both roots being $< k$.
(ii) Both roots $< k$ $D \ge 0$, $\; a \cdot f(k) > 0$, $\; -\dfrac{b}{2a} < k$ Mirror image of Case (i): vertex $< k$ forces both roots to the left of $k$.
(iii) $k$ strictly between roots ($\alpha < k < \beta$) $a \cdot f(k) < 0$ ONLY! If an upward parabola $a f(x)$ dips below the $x$-axis at $k$, it MUST cross the $x$-axis once to the left and once to the right ($D > 0$ is automatic!).
(iv) Both roots in $(k_1, k_2)$ ($k_1 < k_2$) $D \ge 0$, $\; a \cdot f(k_1) > 0$, $\; a \cdot f(k_2) > 0$, $\; k_1 < -\dfrac{b}{2a} < k_2$ Without the vertex constraint, both roots could sit to the left of $k_1$ or to the right of $k_2$ while still keeping $a f(k_1) > 0$ and $a f(k_2) > 0$!
(v) Interval $(k_1, k_2)$ lies strictly between roots ($\alpha < k_1 < k_2 < \beta$) $a \cdot f(k_1) < 0$ AND $a \cdot f(k_2) < 0$ Both $k_1$ and $k_2$ lie in the negative trough of $a f(x)$, forcing $\alpha < k_1 < k_2 < \beta$ automatically.
(vi) Exactly one root in $(k_1, k_2)$ $f(k_1) \cdot f(k_2) < 0$ (plus check boundary cases $f(k_1)=0$ or $f(k_2)=0$ separately!) Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees one crossing inside $(k_1, k_2)$ when signs differ; boundary zeros can still put the other root inside $(k_1, k_2)$!
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Strict vs. Non-Strict Inequality Check

Always notice whether the problem says “both roots are distinct and $> k$” (use $D > 0$) versus “both roots are $> k$” (use $D \ge 0$, because equal roots $\alpha = \beta > k$ are still both $> k$!). Similarly, if $k = 0$ (both roots positive), the three conditions simplify to Vieta’s familiar triple: $D \ge 0$, product $c/a > 0$, and sum $-b/a > 0$.

7. Quadratic & Rational Inequalities (Wavy Curve / Sign-Scheme Method)

To solve any polynomial or rational inequality of the form

$$R(x) = \frac{(x - a_1)^{m_1}(x - a_2)^{m_2} \cdots}{(x - b_1)^{n_1}(x - b_2)^{n_2} \cdots} \lesseqgtr 0$$

NEVER cross-multiply by an expression containing $x$ (you do not know whether it is positive or negative, so you do not know whether the inequality sign reverses!). Instead, execute the 5-step Wavy Curve Algorithm:

  1. Bring Everything to LHS: Move all terms to one side so the RHS is strictly $0$, and combine into a single factored fraction $R(x) = \frac{N(x)}{D(x)}$.
  2. Normalize Leading Coefficients: Make the coefficient of $x$ in every linear factor strictly positive! (For example, replace $(3 - x)$ by $-(x - 3)$ and multiply both sides by $-1$, flipping the inequality sign. Discard any irreducible positive-definite quadratics like $x^2 + x + 1 > 0$ cleanly.)
  3. Plot All Critical Points: Mark every numerator zero $a_i$ and every denominator pole $b_j$ in ascending order on the real number line.
    • Denominator poles ($b_j$): ALWAYS marked with a hollow circle ($\circ$)—STRICTLY EXCLUDED even for $\le$ or $\ge$!
    • Numerator zeros ($a_i$): Marked with a solid dot ($\bullet$) for $\le, \ge$ (included), or a hollow circle ($\circ$) for $<,>$ (excluded).
  4. Sweep Signs Right-to-Left (Odd/Even Multiplicity Law): Because all leading coefficients are $+1$, the rightmost open interval is ALWAYS positive ($+$). Moving leftward across each critical point $c$ with total exponent $p$:
    • If $p$ is ODD: The sign flips ($+ \leftrightarrow -$). The curve crosses the axis.
    • If $p$ is EVEN: The sign stays the same ($+ \to +$ or $- \to -$). The curve bounces off the axis!
  5. Collect Solution Set & Check Isolated Points: Union the intervals matching your target sign, double-checking every solid dot $\bullet$ (an even-multiplicity numerator root surrounded by two wrong-sign intervals becomes an isolated single-point solution $\{a_i\}$ under $\le$ or $\ge$!).

Level 1 Discriminant & $a=0$ Trap Worked Example 1: Nature of Roots with a Vanishing Leading Coefficient
Consider the equation $(k - 1)x^2 + 2kx + (k + 3) = 0$ where $k \in \mathbb{R}$. Find the complete set of values of $k$ for which this equation has:
(a) two equal real roots,
(b) two distinct real roots,
(c) at least one real root.

Step 1 — Inspect the Degenerate Boundary $a = 0$ First:

The leading coefficient is $a = k - 1$, which vanishes at $k = 1$. Substituting $k = 1$ directly into the equation gives:

$$0 \cdot x^2 + 2(1)x + (1 + 3) = 0 \implies 2x + 4 = 0 \implies x = -2$$

Thus at $k = 1$, the equation is linear and possesses exactly one real root ($x = -2$). It does NOT have two roots (neither distinct nor repeated quadratic roots), yet it DOES have a real root!

Step 2 — Analyze the Quadratic Regime ($k \neq 1$):

For $k \neq 1$, compute the discriminant $D = b^2 - 4ac$:

$$D = (2k)^2 - 4(k - 1)(k + 3) = 4k^2 - 4(k^2 + 2k - 3) = 4(3 - 2k)$$
  • Part (a) — Two equal real roots: Require $k \neq 1$ and $D = 0 \implies 3 - 2k = 0 \implies k = \dfrac{3}{2}$. (Verification: $\frac{1}{2}x^2 + 3x + \frac{9}{2} = 0 \iff (x + 3)^2 = 0$, giving repeated root $x = -3$.)
  • Part (b) — Two distinct real roots: Require $k \neq 1$ and $D > 0 \implies 3 - 2k > 0 \implies k < \dfrac{3}{2}$ with $k \neq 1$, i.e., $k \in \left(-\infty,\ \dfrac{3}{2}\right) \setminus \{1\}$.
  • Part (c) — At least one real root: Combine the quadratic real-root region $k \in \left(-\infty,\ \frac{3}{2}\right] \setminus \{1\}$ WITH the linear real-root value $k = 1$ from Step 1! This seals the hole at $k = 1$, giving $k \in \left(-\infty,\ \dfrac{3}{2}\right]$.

Final Answer: (a) $k = \dfrac{3}{2}$; (b) $k \in \left(-\infty, \dfrac{3}{2}\right) \setminus \{1\}$; (c) $k \in \left(-\infty, \dfrac{3}{2}\right]$.

Level 1 Conjugates & Integer Roots Worked Example 2: Irrational Conjugate Reconstruction & Integer-Root Enumeration
(a) If $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$ and one root of $x^2 + ax + b = 0$ is $2 - \sqrt{3}$, find $a$ and $b$.
(b) Find all integer values of $p$ for which $x^2 + px + 12 = 0$ has both roots as integers.

Part (a) — Irrational Conjugate Theorem:

Because all coefficients $1, a, b$ are rational and $2 - \sqrt{3}$ is an irrational quadratic surd, the second root MUST be its conjugate $2 + \sqrt{3}$. By Vieta’s relations:

$$-a = (2 - \sqrt{3}) + (2 + \sqrt{3}) = 4 \implies a = -4$$ $$b = (2 - \sqrt{3})(2 + \sqrt{3}) = 2^2 - (\sqrt{3})^2 = 4 - 3 = 1$$

Check: $x^2 - 4x + 1 = 0$ has $D = 16 - 4 = 12$ and roots $\frac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2 \pm \sqrt{3}$.

Part (b) — Integer Roots via Discriminant Perfect-Square Factorization:

For a monic integer quadratic $x^2 + px + 12 = 0$, the roots are integers $\iff D = p^2 - 48$ is the square of a non-negative integer $m \ge 0$:

$$p^2 - 48 = m^2 \implies (|p| - m)(|p| + m) = 48$$

Since $|p| - m$ and $|p| + m$ have the same parity and their product $48$ is even, both factors must be even positive integers with $|p| - m \le |p| + m$:

  • $|p| - m = 2,\; |p| + m = 24 \implies |p| = 13,\; m = 11 \implies p = \pm 13$.
  • $|p| - m = 4,\; |p| + m = 12 \implies |p| = 8,\; m = 4 \implies p = \pm 8$.
  • $|p| - m = 6,\; |p| + m = 8 \implies |p| = 7,\; m = 1 \implies p = \pm 7$.

Vieta Verification: Integer factor pairs of $12$ are $\pm(1, 12)$, $\pm(2, 6)$, and $\pm(3, 4)$, whose negated sums are precisely $\mp 13$, $\mp 8$, and $\mp 7$.

Final Answer: (a) $a = -4,\ b = 1$; (b) $p \in \{-13, -8, -7, 7, 8, 13\}$ ($6$ values).

Level 1 Vertex & Always Positive Worked Example 3: Global Minimum & Positive-Definite Condition (with $a=0$ Check)
(a) Find the minimum value of $f(x) = 2x^2 - 12x + 25$ over $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and the value of $x$ at which it occurs.
(b) Find all real values of $m$ such that $g(x) = (m - 1)x^2 + 2(m - 1)x + 3 > 0$ for all real $x$.

Part (a) — Vertex Formula & Completing the Square:

Here $a = 2 > 0$, $b = -12$, $c = 25$. Since $a > 0$, the parabola opens upward and attains its global minimum at the vertex:

$$x_v = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-12}{2(2)} = 3, \qquad D = (-12)^2 - 4(2)(25) = 144 - 200 = -56$$ $$y_{\min} = -\frac{D}{4a} = -\frac{-56}{4(2)} = 7$$

Check by completing the square: $f(x) = 2(x^2 - 6x + 9) + 7 = 2(x - 3)^2 + 7 \ge 7$, with equality strictly at $x = 3$.

Part (b) — Always-Positive Criterion ($a = 0$ Check + $a > 0, D < 0$):

  • Case 1 ($a = 0 \implies m = 1$): Substituting $m = 1$ gives $g(x) = 0x^2 + 0x + 3 = 3 > 0$ for ALL $x \in \mathbb{R}$! Hence $m = 1$ is VALID.
  • Case 2 ($a \neq 0 \implies m \neq 1$): A genuine parabola stays strictly above the $x$-axis iff $a > 0$ AND $D < 0$:
    1. Leading coefficient positive: $m - 1 > 0 \implies m > 1$.
    2. Negative discriminant: $$D = \bigl[2(m - 1)\bigr]^2 - 4(m - 1)(3) = 4(m - 1)\bigl[(m - 1) - 3\bigr] = 4(m - 1)(m - 4) < 0$$ Since $4> 0$, $(m - 1)(m - 4) < 0 \implies 1 < m < 4$.

Combining Case 1 ($m = 1$) and Case 2 ($1 < m < 4$) gives the half-closed interval $m \in [1, 4)$.

Final Answer: (a) Minimum value $= 7$ at $x = 3$; (b) $m \in [1, 4)$.

LEVEL 2: EXAM-REAL — Reducible Equations, Common Roots & Wavy Curve
Level 2 Reducible Quadratics Worked Example 4: Symmetric Reciprocal Quartic & Radical Extraneous-Root Filter
Find all real solutions of:
(a) $2x^4 - 9x^3 + 14x^2 - 9x + 2 = 0$,
(b) $\sqrt{2x + 7} = x + 2$.

Part (a) — Symmetric Reciprocal Reduction:

Clearly $x = 0$ is not a root (since $2 \neq 0$). Divide through by $x^2$ and pair symmetric terms:

$$2\left(x^2 + \frac{1}{x^2}\right) - 9\left(x + \frac{1}{x}\right) + 14 = 0$$

Let $t = x + \dfrac{1}{x}$. Then $x^2 + \dfrac{1}{x^2} = t^2 - 2$, transforming the quartic into a quadratic in $t$:

$$2(t^2 - 2) - 9t + 14 = 0 \implies 2t^2 - 9t + 10 = 0 \implies (2t - 5)(t - 2) = 0$$

Hence $t = \dfrac{5}{2}$ or $t = 2$. Both satisfy the real-root range condition $|t| \ge 2$:

  • For $t = \dfrac{5}{2}$: $x + \dfrac{1}{x} = \dfrac{5}{2} \implies 2x^2 - 5x + 2 = 0 \implies (2x - 1)(x - 2) = 0 \implies x = \dfrac{1}{2},\ 2$.
  • For $t = 2$: $x + \dfrac{1}{x} = 2 \implies x^2 - 2x + 1 = 0 \implies (x - 1)^2 = 0 \implies x = 1$ (double root).

Thus the real roots are $x \in \left\{\dfrac{1}{2},\ 1,\ 2\right\}$ (with $1$ having multiplicity $2$).

Part (b) — Radical Equation with Domain & Non-Negativity Check:

Before squaring, impose the non-negativity of the principal square root: $\sqrt{2x + 7} \ge 0 \implies x + 2 \ge 0 \implies x \ge -2$ (which also guarantees $2x + 7 \ge 3 > 0$). Now square both sides:

$$2x + 7 = (x + 2)^2 = x^2 + 4x + 4 \implies x^2 + 2x - 3 = 0 \implies (x + 3)(x - 1) = 0$$

Candidate roots are $x = -3$ and $x = 1$:

  • Check $x = -3$: Fails $x \ge -2$! Explicitly, $\text{LHS} = \sqrt{2(-3) + 7} = \sqrt{1} = +1 \neq -1 = \text{RHS}$. EXTRANEOUS — REJECT!
  • Check $x = 1$: $\text{LHS} = \sqrt{9} = 3 = 1 + 2 = \text{RHS}$. VALID!

Final Answer: (a) $x \in \left\{\dfrac{1}{2}, 1, 2\right\}$; (b) $x = 1$ only.

Level 2 Common Root Worked Example 5: Finding Parameters for a Common Root via Elimination & Verification
Find all real values of $k$ for which the quadratic equations $$x^2 - 5x + k = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad x^2 - 7x + 2k = 0$$ have a common root, and verify whether they share one or both roots for each valid $k$.

Step 1 — Check Whether Both Roots Can Be Common:

For both roots to be common, we need $\dfrac{1}{1} = \dfrac{-5}{-7} = \dfrac{k}{2k}$, which is impossible since $1 \neq \frac{5}{7}$. Hence any common root must be unique (exactly one common root).

Step 2 — Eliminate $\alpha^2$ Directly:

Let $\alpha$ be the common root. Then:

$$\alpha^2 - 5\alpha + k = 0 \qquad \text{(1)}$$ $$\alpha^2 - 7\alpha + 2k = 0 \qquad \text{(2)}$$

Subtracting Eq (2) from Eq (1) eliminates $\alpha^2$ cleanly:

$$(-5\alpha + 7\alpha) + (k - 2k) = 0 \implies 2\alpha - k = 0 \implies \alpha = \frac{k}{2}$$

Step 3 — Substitute $\alpha = \dfrac{k}{2}$ Back into Eq (1):

$$\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^2 - 5\left(\frac{k}{2}\right) + k = 0 \implies \frac{k^2}{4} - \frac{3k}{2} = 0 \implies k^2 - 6k = 0 \implies k(k - 6) = 0$$

Thus $k = 0$ or $k = 6$.

Step 4 — Verify Both Parameter Values Explicitly:

  • $k = 0$: Equations become $x(x - 5) = 0$ (roots $0, 5$) and $x(x - 7) = 0$ (roots $0, 7$). Exactly one common root: $\alpha = 0$.
  • $k = 6$: Equations become $(x - 2)(x - 3) = 0$ (roots $2, 3$) and $(x - 3)(x - 4) = 0$ (roots $3, 4$). Exactly one common root: $\alpha = 3$.

Resultant Formula Cross-Check: $(c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1)^2 = (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)(b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1) \implies (k - 2k)^2 = (-7 + 5)(-10k + 7k) \implies k^2 = (-2)(-3k) = 6k \implies k = 0, 6$. Exact match!

Final Answer: $k = 0$ (common root $0$) and $k = 6$ (common root $3$).

Level 2 Wavy Curve Inequality Worked Example 6: Rational Inequality with Even Multiplicity & Excluded Poles
Solve the rational inequality over $\mathbb{R}$: $$\frac{(x - 1)^2 (x + 2)}{(x - 4)(x + 1)^3} \le 0$$ Also state how the answer changes if $\le 0$ is replaced by $\ge 0$.

Step 1 — Check Normalization & Classify Critical Points (see Figure 9.3):

Every linear factor already has leading coefficient $+1$, and the RHS is $0$. Identify all critical points with their multiplicities and inclusion status:

  • $x = -2$: Numerator zero, multiplicity $1$ (odd) $\implies$ included ($\bullet$).
  • $x = -1$: Denominator pole, multiplicity $3$ (odd) $\implies$ EXCLUDED ($\circ$).
  • $x = 1$: Numerator zero, multiplicity $2$ (even) $\implies$ included ($\bullet$).

  1. $(4, \infty)$: $+$ (rightmost interval is always positive).
  2. Cross $x = 4$ (odd mult $1$): flip to $-$ on $(1, 4)$.
  3. Cross $x = 1$ (even mult $2$): BOUNCE — stays $-$ on $(-1, 1)$.
  4. Cross $x = -1$ (odd mult $3$): flip to $+$ on $(-2, -1)$.
  5. Cross $x = -2$ (odd mult $1$): flip to $-$ on $(-\infty, -2)$.

$$x \in (-\infty, -2] \cup (-1, 4)$$

Step 4 — Bonus Check (If Inequality Were $\ge 0$):

Taking positive intervals gives $(-2, -1) \cup (4, \infty)$. Adding the included numerator zeros $\{-2, 1\}$ closes $-2$ AND adds the isolated single point $\{1\}$ (where the expression equals $0$ even though both neighboring intervals are negative!): $[-2, -1) \cup \{1\} \cup (4, \infty)$.

Final Answer: For $\le 0$: $x \in (-\infty, -2] \cup (-1, 4)$; for $\ge 0$: $x \in [-2, -1) \cup \{1\} \cup (4, \infty)$.

LEVEL 3: RANK-BOOSTER — Olympiad Location of Roots & Range Synthesis (with 30-Second Tricks)
Level 3 Location of Roots (Case ii) Worked Example 7: Both Roots Strictly Less Than a Threshold $k$
Find all real values of $a$ for which both roots of the quadratic equation $$x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + a - 3) = 0$$ are real and strictly less than $3$.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Location of Roots Case ii):

Let $f(x) = x^2 - 2ax + (a^2 + a - 3)$, which has leading coefficient $A = 1 > 0$. For both roots to be real and strictly less than $k = 3$, impose all three Location-of-Roots conditions simultaneously:

  1. Real Roots ($D \ge 0$): $$D = (-2a)^2 - 4(1)(a^2 + a - 3) = 4a^2 - 4a^2 - 4a + 12 = 4(3 - a) \ge 0 \implies a \le 3$$
  2. Endpoint Sign ($A \cdot f(3) > 0$): $$f(3) = 3^2 - 2a(3) + a^2 + a - 3 = a^2 - 5a + 6 = (a - 2)(a - 3) > 0$$ By Wavy Curve, $(a - 2)(a - 3) > 0 \implies a \in (-\infty, 2) \cup (3, \infty)$.
  3. Vertex Position ($x_v < 3$): $$x_v = -\frac{-2a}{2(1)} = a < 3$$

Take the Intersection of (1), (2), and (3):

$$(-\infty, 3] \;\cap\; \bigl((-\infty, 2) \cup (3, \infty)\bigr) \;\cap\; (-\infty, 3) = (-\infty, 2)$$

Boundary Sanity Check: At $a = 2$, $x^2 - 4x + 3 = (x - 1)(x - 3) = 0$ has a root equal to $3$ (not strictly less). At $a = 1$, $x = 1 \pm \sqrt{2} \approx -0.41, 2.41$, both strictly less than $3$.

30-Second Exam Trick (Perfect-Square Collapse):

Spot $x^2 - 2ax + a^2 = (x - a)^2$ instantly! Rewrite the equation as $(x - a)^2 = 3 - a$. Real roots require $3 - a \ge 0$, and the larger root is $x_{\max} = a + \sqrt{3 - a}$. Requiring $x_{\max} < 3$ gives:

$$\sqrt{3 - a} < 3 - a$$

Setting $u = \sqrt{3 - a} \ge 0$, we need $u < u^2 \iff u> 1 \iff 3 - a > 1 \iff a < 2$ in 15 seconds!

Final Answer: $a \in (-\infty, 2)$.

Level 3 Location of Roots (Case iv) Worked Example 8: Both Roots Confined Strictly Inside an Open Interval $(k_1, k_2)$
Find the complete set of real values of $m$ such that both roots of the quadratic equation $$x^2 - 2mx + m^2 - 1 = 0$$ lie strictly inside the open interval $(-2, 4)$.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Location of Roots Case iv):

Let $f(x) = x^2 - 2mx + (m^2 - 1)$, with leading coefficient $A = 1 > 0$, $k_1 = -2$, and $k_2 = 4$. Apply all four necessary and sufficient conditions:

  1. Real Roots ($D \ge 0$): $$D = (-2m)^2 - 4(1)(m^2 - 1) = 4m^2 - 4m^2 + 4 = 4 > 0 \quad (\text{holds for all } m \in \mathbb{R})$$
  2. Left Endpoint ($A \cdot f(-2) > 0$): $$f(-2) = (-2)^2 - 2m(-2) + m^2 - 1 = m^2 + 4m + 3 = (m + 1)(m + 3) > 0$$ $$\implies m \in (-\infty, -3) \cup (-1, \infty)$$
  3. Right Endpoint ($A \cdot f(4) > 0$): $$f(4) = 4^2 - 2m(4) + m^2 - 1 = m^2 - 8m + 15 = (m - 3)(m - 5) > 0$$ $$\implies m \in (-\infty, 3) \cup (5, \infty)$$
  4. Vertex Inside Interval ($-2 < -\dfrac{b}{2A} < 4$): $$-2 < m < 4$$

Intersection of All 4 Conditions:

Notice how Condition 4 eliminates both outer branches $(-\infty, -3)$ and $(5, \infty)$! Intersecting $(-2, 4)$ with $(-1, \infty)$ gives $(-1, 4)$; intersecting that with $(-\infty, 3)$ yields $m \in (-1, 3)$.

30-Second Exam Trick (Direct Difference-of-Squares Factorization):

Always check if a parametric quadratic factors cleanly before grinding out 4 inequalities! Here:

$$x^2 - 2mx + m^2 - 1 = (x - m)^2 - 1^2 = \bigl(x - (m - 1)\bigr)\bigl(x - (m + 1)\bigr) = 0$$

The two roots are explicitly $m - 1$ and $m + 1$. Both lie strictly inside $(-2, 4) \iff m - 1 > -2$ AND $m + 1 < 4 \iff -1 < m < 3$ in 10 seconds!

Final Answer: $m \in (-1, 3)$.

Level 3 Rational Quadratic Range Worked Example 9: Exact Range of a Rational Quadratic Expression via $D \ge 0$ & Wavy Curve
Find the exact minimum and maximum values (i.e., complete range) of the rational function $$y = \frac{x^2 - 3x + 4}{x^2 + 3x + 4}$$ for all real values of $x$.

Full First-Principles Derivation (Discriminant + $a = 0$ Check + Wavy Curve):

Step 1 — Verify Denominator Has No Real Poles:

For $x^2 + 3x + 4$, $a = 1 > 0$ and $D = 3^2 - 4(1)(4) = -7 < 0$. By Section 4, $x^2 + 3x + 4> 0$ for ALL $x \in \mathbb{R}$, so $y$ is defined everywhere and we may legally cross-multiply without losing poles.

Step 2 — Form a Quadratic in $x$ with Parameter $y$:

$$y(x^2 + 3x + 4) = x^2 - 3x + 4 \implies (y - 1)x^2 + 3(y + 1)x + 4(y - 1) = 0$$

Step 3 — Check the Degenerate Case $a = 0$ ($y = 1$):

When $y = 1$, the equation becomes $0 \cdot x^2 + 3(2)x + 0 = 0 \implies 6x = 0 \implies x = 0 \in \mathbb{R}$. Hence $y = 1$ IS achieved (at $x = 0$).

Step 4 — Impose $D_x \ge 0$ for Real $x$ ($y \neq 1$):

$$D_x = \bigl[3(y + 1)\bigr]^2 - 4(y - 1)\bigl[4(y - 1)\bigr] = 9(y + 1)^2 - 16(y - 1)^2 \ge 0$$

Factor cleanly using $P^2 - Q^2 = (P - Q)(P + Q)$:

$$\bigl[3(y + 1) - 4(y - 1)\bigr]\bigl[3(y + 1) + 4(y - 1)\bigr] \ge 0$$ $$(7 - y)(7y - 1) \ge 0$$

Normalize the leading coefficient of $(7 - y)$ by multiplying both sides by $-1$ (flipping $\ge$ to $\le$):

$$(y - 7)(7y - 1) \le 0$$

By Wavy Curve (roots $\frac{1}{7}$ and $7$, both odd multiplicity), this holds for $y \in \left[\dfrac{1}{7},\ 7\right]$. Since $y = 1$ already lies inside this interval, the exact range is $\left[\dfrac{1}{7},\ 7\right]$.

Attainment Check: At $x = 2$, $y = \frac{4 - 6 + 4}{4 + 6 + 4} = \frac{2}{14} = \frac{1}{7}$ (minimum); at $x = -2$, $y = \frac{4 + 6 + 4}{4 - 6 + 4} = \frac{14}{2} = 7$ (maximum).

30-Second Exam Trick (Divide by $x$ + AM-GM Reciprocal Bound):

First note $x = 0 \implies y = 1$. For $x \neq 0$, divide top and bottom by $x$ and set $t = x + \dfrac{4}{x}$:

$$y = \frac{\left(x + \frac{4}{x}\right) - 3}{\left(x + \frac{4}{x}\right) + 3} = \frac{t - 3}{t + 3} = 1 - \frac{6}{t + 3}$$

By AM-GM, $t \ge 2\sqrt{4} = 4$ for $x > 0$ (giving $y_{\min} = \frac{4 - 3}{4 + 3} = \frac{1}{7}$ at $t = 4$) and $t \le -4$ for $x < 0$ (giving $y_{\max}=\frac{-4 - 3}{-4 + 3}=7$ at $t=-4$). Since $y$ sweeps monotonically over $[\frac{1}{7}, 1)$ and $(1, 7]$, adding $y(0)=1$ gives Range $=\left[\dfrac{1}{7},\ 7\right]$ in 15 seconds!

Final Answer: Minimum value $= \dfrac{1}{7}$, Maximum value $= 7$; Range $= \left[\dfrac{1}{7},\ 7\right]$.
