1 · Quantitative Comparison & Inequality Logic

Core Idea — The Four Verdicts

You are given Column A and Column B and must pick one of four verdicts: (A) A is always greater, (B) B is always greater, (C) they are always equal, (D) the relation cannot be determined. "Always" means for every value allowed by the given conditions.

  • Verdicts A, B and C each need a proof that covers every admissible case.
  • Verdict D needs only two admissible inputs that give different outcomes. One counter-example kills any "always".
  • First squeeze every given condition for sign and size information, then test boundary cases.

1.1 Sign analysis from first principles

  • A product or quotient is negative exactly when it has an odd number of negative factors.
  • $x^2 \ge 0$ for every real $x$, and $x^2 = 0$ only when $x=0$.
  • If $b^2c > 0$, then $b^2 \neq 0$, so $b^2>0$. A positive number times $c$ is positive only if $c>0$. So $b\neq 0$ and $c>0$.
  • If also $abc < 0$, write $abc=(ab)\cdot c$. With $c>0$ this forces $ab<0$, so $a$ and $b$ have opposite signs. This does not tell you which one is positive.
Fig 5.1 — $x^2 - x = x(x-1)$ is positive when $x<0$ or $x>1$, negative on $(0,1)$, and zero at $0$ and $1$. This is why "$x^2 > x$" is a classic "cannot be determined" trap.

1.2 The boundary-case test set

Test value What it breaks
$0$ Strict inequalities, division, "positive ⇒ bigger", $x^2 > x$

$-1$ Odd and even powers flip sign. $x^2=1>x$, and $1/x=x$.
$\tfrac12$ (and any $0<x<1$) Powers shrink: $x^3<x^2<x<\sqrt{x}<1<\tfrac1x$
$-\tfrac12$ (and any $-1<x<0$) $x<x^3<0<x^2$, and $\tfrac1x=-2<x$

Equal variables ($a=b$) Reveals equality cases in AM–GM and squares

1.3 Legal moves on inequalities

  • Multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality. Never multiply by an expression whose sign is unknown.
  • Reciprocals: if $a>b>0$ then $\tfrac1a<\tfrac1b$. If $a>0>b$ then $\tfrac1a>\tfrac1b$, because the sign changes.
  • Squaring preserves order only when both sides are $\ge 0$. Counter-example: $-3<2$ but $(-3)^2=9>4=2^2$.
  • Safest method: study the sign of $A-B$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Squares and AM–GM

From $(a-b)^2\ge 0$ we get $a^2+b^2\ge 2ab$ for all real $a,b$. For positive $a,b$ this gives $\dfrac{a+b}{2}\ge\sqrt{ab}$, with equality only when $a=b$.

Derivation of a famous comparison. For $x\neq 0$, $x+\dfrac1x-2=\dfrac{x^2-2x+1}{x}=\dfrac{(x-1)^2}{x}$. So for $x>0$, $x+\tfrac1x\ge 2$ with equality only at $x=1$. For $x<0$, $x+\tfrac1x\le -2$. If the question says "$x>0$", Column $x+\tfrac1x$ versus Column $2$ is verdict "A ≥ B", but not strictly greater unless $x\ne1$ is also given. Always check whether the equality case is allowed.

2 · Cryptarithmetic (Alphametics)

Rules of the Game
  • Each letter stands for one digit, and different letters stand for different digits (unless the question says otherwise).
  • The leading letter of any number is not 0.
  • The puzzle is ordinary integer arithmetic. A well-set exam puzzle has exactly one solution. Some famous puzzles do not. $TWO+TWO=FOUR$ has both $734+734=1468$ and $765+765=1530$, so you must verify uniqueness, not just find an answer.

Column equation. Number the columns from the right and let $c_i$ be the carry into column $i$ ($c_1=0$). Then $$a_i+b_i+c_i=r_i+10\,c_{i+1}.$$

Carry bounds. Adding two numbers gives a carry $\le 1$, and adding three gives $\le 2$. In a column of a number times a single digit $k$, the carry is $\le k-1$. Proof for the last: the column value is at most $9k+(k-1)=10k-1$, and its carry is $\lfloor (10k-1)/10\rfloor=k-1$.

2.1 The attack ladder (use in this order)

  1. Length mismatch. If a sum of two $n$-digit numbers has $n+1$ digits, its leading digit is 1, because the carry is at most 1.
  2. Leading column. Write its equation and bound the letters. Usually only one or two cases survive.
  3. Cancelling letter. If a letter appears as an addend and as the result in the same column, then $X+Y+c=X+10c'$, so $Y+c\in\{0,10\}$. That means $(Y,c)=(0,0)$ or $(9,1)$.
  4. Parity. $A+A$ is even, so the units digit of any doubled letter is even.
  5. Casting out nines. The digit-sum relation $\text{addend}_1+\text{addend}_2\equiv\text{sum}\pmod 9$ gives a linear equation among the letters.
  6. Branch last. Split into cases only when forced. If more than two branches remain, you missed a constraint.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Modular Filters

A number is congruent to its digit sum modulo $9$ and, with alternating signs, modulo $11$. For $SEND+MORE=MONEY$, reducing mod $9$ gives $S+E+D+R-Y\equiv 0\pmod 9$. This is an extra check on any candidate solution, and Worked Example 7 uses it.

3 · Coding-Decoding & Number / Letter Series

The Alphabet as a Number Line

Let $A=1,\,B=2,\dots,Z=26$. Memorise the anchors E J O T Y = 5, 10, 15, 20, 25.

  • Opposite letter of position $p$ is $27-p$ (A↔Z, B↔Y, …, M↔N).
  • Shift by $s$ with wrap-around: $p\mapsto\big((p+s-1)\bmod 26\big)+1$. Example: $Y(25)+3=28\to 28-26=2=B$.

Derivation (Newton's forward formula). If $\Delta_1$ is the first first-difference and $\Delta_2$ the constant second difference, then $$T_n=T_1+(n-1)\Delta_1+\binom{n-1}{2}\Delta_2 .$$ Check on $2,3,6,11,18$: $T_1=2,\ \Delta_1=1,\ \Delta_2=2$ gives $T_5=2+4+6\cdot 2/2\cdot 1\cdot\!\!\!\!\quad$ — more simply $T_5=2+4(1)+\binom42(2)=2+4+12=18$ ✓.

Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Last-Digit Cycles

Last digits of powers repeat: $2:\,2,4,8,6$; $3:\,3,9,7,1$; $7:\,7,9,3,1$; $8:\,8,4,2,6$ (cycle length 4); $4:\,4,6$ and $9:\,9,1$ (length 2). Example: the last digit of $7^{2026}$. Since $2026\equiv 2\pmod 4$, it is the 2nd entry of the cycle, namely $9$. If the $k$-th differences of a series are constant, the series is a polynomial of degree $k$ in $n$.

4 · Seating Arrangements & Puzzles

Facing Rules — Never Guess Left and Right

Linear rows. A person facing North has West on their left and East on their right. A person facing South has East on their left and West on their right. In two parallel rows facing each other, one row faces North and the other faces South, so left and right swap between the rows.

Circular tables (clockwise is measured as seen from above):

  • Facing the centre: left hand = clockwise, right hand = anticlockwise.
  • Facing outward: left hand = anticlockwise, right hand = clockwise.
  • In a circle of $n$ ($n$ even), the seat opposite seat $k$ is seat $k+\tfrac n2$.
Fig 5.2 — Linear row facing North. "A is to the immediate left of B" means A is on B's west side. Reverse every statement for a row facing South.
Fig 5.3 — Both people look "up the page", so both have West on their left. Clockwise, however, runs East at the top and West at the bottom. Memory hook: Inside-Left = Clockwise.

4.1 The five-step method

  1. Anchor. A circle has rotational symmetry, so seat one person anywhere. Their seat number is free.
  2. Draw all slots and mark IN/OUT arrows as soon as the facing pattern is known.
  3. Place the definite facts first (exact positions, "opposite", "immediate").
  4. Branch only when forced, and prune a branch the moment a clue is violated.
  5. Re-verify every clue against the final picture.

4.2 Multi-attribute grid elimination

For "who has which colour / pet / city", draw a table with persons as rows and each attribute as column groups. Every value is used exactly once (a Latin-square property), so:

  • A ✗ in a cell is as valuable as a ✓.
  • When three cells in a row or column are crossed out, the fourth is forced.
  • Write each negative clue ("P does not like Red") as ✗ immediately, and each chain clue ("the person who likes Red sits left of Q") as a linked pair.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Counting Arrangements

$n$ distinct people around a circle can be arranged in $(n-1)!$ ways (fix one person to kill the rotations). If clockwise and anticlockwise count as the same (e.g. a necklace), there are $\dfrac{(n-1)!}{2}$ ways for $n\ge3$. A clue such as "A and B are neighbours" glues them into one block of $2$ (with $2!$ internal orders).

Pointing-to-a-Photo Traps
  1. "Only" collapse. Phrases like "only daughter of my mother's mother" return to a person you already know (your mother).
  2. Speaker's gender. If the speaker's gender is not given, "my father's only son" can be the speaker (male) or a brother (female). If the answer changes, the correct verdict is "cannot be determined".
  3. Direction. "He is the son of X" describes the man from X. Always rebuild the tree starting from the speaker, never from the photo.

Coded relations. Suppose $A+B$ means "A is the mother of B", $A-B$ means "A is the brother of B", and $A\times B$ means "A is the father of B". Then $P+Q\times R$ means P is the mother of Q, and Q is the father of R. So P is R's paternal grandmother. Decode left to right and build a tree.

5.2 Syllogisms

Fig 5.5 — The three building blocks. "All A are B": A sits inside B. "No A is B": the circles are disjoint. "Some A are B": the circles overlap.
Statement Certain consequences Not guaranteed
All A are B Some A are B; Some B are A All B are A
No A is B No B is A; Some A are not B —
Some A are B Some B are A Some A are not B (a classic trap)
Some A are not B — Some B are not A (no conversion)
Only a few A are B Some A are B; Some A are not B; Some B are A All A are B (false); Some B are not A (only possible)

Two-premise rules. Arrange the statements so the middle term $B$ is the predicate of the first premise and the subject of the second:

Premise 1 Premise 2 Conclusion
All A are B All B are C All A are C
All A are B No B is C No A is C
Some A are B All B are C Some A are C
Some A are B No B is C Some A are not C
No A is B All B are C Some C are not A (reverse)
No A is B Some B are C Some C are not A (reverse)

Pairs not listed give no definite conclusion. Examples: two "Some" premises, "All A are B + Some B are C", and any pair starting with "Some A are not B".

Either–Or and Possibility
  • Either–Or applies only when (i) both conclusions have the same subject and predicate, (ii) neither follows on its own, and (iii) they are contradictories: "All A are B" with "Some A are not B", or "No A is B" with "Some A are B". "Some A are B" and "Some A are not B" are not contradictories, since both can be true.
  • Possibility conclusions ("can be", "may be") are true if at least one valid diagram makes them true, so they need only to be consistent with the statements. A possibility fails only if it directly contradicts a given statement.

6 · Clocks & Calendars

6.1 Clocks

Derivation of the Angle Formula

The minute hand turns $360^\circ$ in $60$ min, so it moves $6^\circ$ per minute. The hour hand turns $30^\circ$ per hour, so it moves $0.5^\circ$ per minute. At $H$ hours $M$ minutes: $$\text{minute hand}=6M,\qquad \text{hour hand}=30H+0.5M,$$ $$\theta=\big|30H-5.5M\big|\ \ (\text{if }\theta>180^\circ,\text{ take }360^\circ-\theta).$$ The hands close in on each other at a relative speed of $5.5^\circ$ per minute.

Fig 5.6 — 2:30. Hour hand $=30(2)+0.5(30)=75^\circ$, minute hand $=6(30)=180^\circ$, so $\theta=|75-180|=105^\circ$. The formula gives $|60-165|=105^\circ$ ✓.
Situation (between $H$ and $H+1$ o'clock) Minutes past $H$
Hands coincide ($\theta=0$) $M=\dfrac{60H}{11}$
Hands opposite ($\theta=180^\circ$) $M=\dfrac{60H+360}{11}$ for $H=0,\dots,4$; $\ M=\dfrac{60H-360}{11}$ for $H=6,\dots,11$
Right angle ($\theta=90^\circ$) $M=\dfrac{60H\pm180}{11}$ (keep values with $0\le M<60$)

Counts. In 12 hours the hands coincide 11 times, are opposite 11 times and are at right angles 22 times. In 24 hours these are 22, 22 and 44.

Faulty clocks. If a clock gains $g$ minutes per real hour, it shows $60+g$ minutes of "clock time" per $60$ real minutes. So $$\text{real elapsed}=\text{shown elapsed}\times\frac{60}{60+g}.$$ Example: a clock gaining $5$ min/h, set right at 6 am, shows 12:30 pm. Shown elapsed $=390$ min, so real elapsed $=390\times\tfrac{60}{65}=360$ min $=6$ h, and the true time is 12 noon. Mirror image of time $T$ is $11{:}60-T$ (for $4{:}20$ it is $7{:}40$).

6.2 Calendars

Odd Days Method

Odd days are the days left over after removing complete weeks. An ordinary year ($365=52\times7+1$) has 1 odd day, and a leap year has 2.

Leap-year rule: divisible by 4, but a century year must be divisible by 400. So 1900 was not a leap year and 2000 was.

Day code: $0=$ Sunday, $1=$ Monday, $2=$ Tuesday, $3=$ Wednesday, $4=$ Thursday, $5=$ Friday, $6=$ Saturday.

Block Odd days Why
100 years 5 $76\times1+24\times2=124=17\times7+5$
200 years 3 $2\times5=10\equiv3$
300 years 1 $3\times5=15\equiv1$
400 years 0 $4\times5+1=21\equiv0$ (the 400th year is a leap century)
Month Jan Feb Mar Apr May Jun Jul Aug Sep Oct Nov Dec
Odd days 3 0 (1 leap) 3 2 3 2 3 3 2 3 2 3

Algorithm. (1) Count the completed years before the target date. (2) Split them into complete centuries and $n$ leftover years, where $n$ lies within a century. The leftover years contribute $\big(n+\lfloor n/4\rfloor\big)\bmod 7$ odd days. (3) Add the days elapsed in the target year, up to and including the date. (4) Reduce mod 7 and read the day code.

Repeating calendars. From year $Y$, add $1$ per ordinary year and $2$ per leap year through year $Y'-1$. The calendar of $Y$ repeats in $Y'$ when the total is $\equiv 0\pmod 7$ and $Y'$ is the same type (leap or ordinary) as $Y$. In 1901–2099 an ordinary year repeats after 6 or 11 years, and a leap year after 28 years.

Counting figures. These counts are all verifiable by direct enumeration:

  • Triangles from an apex. If $p$ points lie on the base (including both end-points) and each is joined to the apex, the number of triangles is $\binom p2$. For $p=4$ this is $6$.
  • Rectangles in an $m\times n$ grid of unit squares: $\binom{m+1}{2}\binom{n+1}{2}$. For $2\times2$ this is $3\cdot3=9$.
  • Squares in an $n\times n$ grid: $\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2=\dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$. For $n=2$ this is $5$.
Resonance / Olympiad Bridge — Invariants

Under a rotation or reflection the number of shaded cells never changes. Under XOR, $|A\oplus B|=|A|+|B|-2|A\cap B|$. Checking this count first rules out wrong options in seconds, before you compare any shapes.

Formula Vault — Chapter 5
  • Inequality: $x^2>x\iff x<0$ or $x>1$. For $0<x<1$: $x^3<x^2<x<\sqrt{x}<1<\tfrac1x$. AM–GM: $\tfrac{a+b}{2}\ge\sqrt{ab}$ ($a,b>0$). $x+\tfrac1x\ge2$ for $x>0$.

Level 1Quantitative Comparison Example 1 — Sign Analysis

Real numbers $a,b,c$ satisfy $abc<0$ and $b^2c>0$. Compare Column A: $ab$ and Column B: $c$.

(A) A is greater   (B) B is greater   (C) equal   (D) cannot be determined

Step 1. Since $b^2\ge0$ and $b^2c>0$, we need $b^2\neq0$, so $b^2>0$. A positive number times $c$ is positive only if $c>0$. Hence $b\ne0$ and $c>0$.

Step 2. $abc=(ab)\cdot c<0$ with $c>0$ forces $ab<0$.

Step 3. Column A $=ab<0<c=$ Column B in every admissible case.

Check with two different inputs. $(a,b,c)=(-2,1,3)$: $abc=-6<0$, $b^2c=3>0$, $ab=-2<3$ ✓. $(a,b,c)=(2,-1,3)$: $abc=-6$, $b^2c=3$, $ab=-2<3$ ✓.

Answer: (B). The trap is assuming $a>0$. The two checks above use opposite signs of $a$ and the verdict holds in both, which is exactly what "always" demands.

Level 1Clocks Example 2 — Angle and Coinciding Time

(a) Find the angle between the hands at 4:20. (b) After 4:00, at what time do the hands first coincide?

(a) $\theta=|30H-5.5M|=|30(4)-5.5(20)|=|120-110|=\mathbf{10^\circ}$.

Check from first principles: hour hand $=30(4)+0.5(20)=130^\circ$, minute hand $=6(20)=120^\circ$, difference $10^\circ$ ✓.

(b) Coinciding means $30H+0.5M=6M$, so $5.5M=30H$ and $M=\dfrac{60H}{11}$. For $H=4$: $M=\dfrac{240}{11}=21\tfrac{9}{11}$.

Check: hour hand $=120+0.5\cdot\tfrac{240}{11}=130.909^\circ$, minute hand $=6\cdot\tfrac{240}{11}=130.909^\circ$ ✓.

Answers: (a) $10^\circ$; (b) $4{:}21\tfrac{9}{11}$.

Level 1Blood Relations Example 3 — The "Only" Collapse

Pointing to a man in a photograph, Meera says, "His mother is the only daughter of my mother's mother." How is the man related to Meera?

Fig 5.8 — The only daughter of Meera's mother's mother is Meera's own mother.

Step 1. Rebuild from the speaker. Meera's mother's mother has an only daughter, and Meera's mother is a daughter of that woman. So the only daughter is Meera's mother.

Step 2. The man's mother is Meera's mother, so the man is a son of Meera's mother, i.e. Meera's male sibling.

Answer: the man is Meera's brother. The common wrong answers are "cousin" and "uncle", which come from treating the phrase as a new person instead of collapsing it onto a known one.

Level 2 · Exam-Real — Multi-Step Control
Level 2Syllogism Example 4 — "Only a Few" and Either–Or

Statements: Only a few Pens are Books. All Books are Cups. No Cup is a Ladle.

Conclusions: I. Some Pens are Cups.   II. Some Pens are not Ladles.   III. Some Books are not Pens.   IV. All Books are Pens.

Which of the conclusions follow?

Fig 5.9 — Pens (dashed) overlap Books but also have a part outside Books. Books sit inside Cups. Ladle is outside Cups.

Step 1 — Decode. "Only a few Pens are Books" gives: Some Pens are Books, Some Pens are not Books, Some Books are Pens.

Step 2 — I. Those Pens that are Books are inside Cups (All Books are Cups), so Some Pens are Cups ✓ certain.

Step 3 — II. No Cup is a Ladle, and the Pens-that-are-Books are Cups, so those Pens are not Ladles. Some Pens are not Ladles ✓ certain.

Step 4 — III and IV. Pens must have a part outside Books, but Books may lie entirely inside Pens or may stick out of Pens. So neither "Some Books are not Pens" nor "All Books are Pens" is certain alone. Both have the same subject (Books) and predicate (Pens), and they are contradictories (O and A). Neither follows individually, so the Either–Or rule applies.

Answer: I and II follow, and either III or IV follows.

Level 2Series & Coding Example 5 — Difference of Differences, Alternation, Progressive Shift

(a) In $2,3,6,11,18,\dots$ find the 6th term and the 10th term.
(b) Find the next term of $5,10,8,16,14,28,\ ?$
(c) In a code, FIRE is written as GKUI. How is WATER written in that code?

(a) First differences: $1,3,5,7$. Second differences: $2,2,2$ (constant), so the series is quadratic with $\Delta_1=1,\ \Delta_2=2$. The next difference is $9$, so $T_6=18+9=\mathbf{27}$. For $T_{10}$: $T_{10}=2+9(1)+\binom92(2)=2+9+72=\mathbf{83}$.

Check with a closed form. Fitting $T_n=n^2+bn+c$ to $T_1=2,T_2=3$ gives $b=-2,c=3$, so $T_n=(n-1)^2+2$. Then $T_6=25+2=27$ ✓ and $T_{10}=81+2=83$ ✓.

(b) Read adjacent terms in pairs: $5\to10$ ($\times2$), $10\to8$ ($-2$), $8\to16$ ($\times2$), $16\to14$ ($-2$), $14\to28$ ($\times2$), so the next step is $-2$ and the next term is $28-2=\mathbf{26}$.

(c) FIRE: $F+1=G,\ I+2=K,\ R+3=U,\ E+4=I$. The shifts are $+1,+2,+3,+4$. For WATER (5 letters) use $+1,+2,+3,+4,+5$:

$W(23)+1=24=X,\ A(1)+2=3=C,\ T(20)+3=23=W,\ E(5)+4=9=I,\ R(18)+5=23=W$.

Answers: (a) 27 and 83; (b) 26; (c) XCWIW.

Level 2Non-Verbal Matrix Example 6 — XOR Superposition

Each row follows the same rule. Find the missing third figure of the third row. (Shaded cells are dark.)

Step 1 — Hypothesis. Row 1: both figures shade the centre, and the result has no centre but keeps the four corners. So common cells vanish and non-common cells stay, which is XOR.

Step 2 — Test on Row 2. Top row $\{(0,0),(0,1),(0,2)\}$ and middle column $\{(0,1),(1,1),(2,1)\}$ share $(0,1)$. XOR leaves $\{(0,0),(0,2),(1,1),(2,1)\}$, which is exactly the third figure of Row 2 ✓. The rule holds on both rows.

Step 3 — Apply to Row 3. Left column $\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,0)\}$ and bottom row $\{(2,0),(2,1),(2,2)\}$ share $(2,0)$. Removing it leaves $\{(0,0),(1,0),(2,1),(2,2)\}$.

Check by counting. $|E\oplus F|=3+3-2(1)=4$ shaded cells ✓. The answer figure has 4.

Answer: the figure with shaded cells top-left, middle-left, bottom-middle and bottom-right.

Level 3Cryptarithmetic Example 7 — SEND + MORE = MONEY

Each letter is a different digit and $S,M\neq0$. Solve $\ SEND+MORE=MONEY$ completely and prove the solution is unique.

Let $c_2,c_3,c_4$ be the carries out of the units, tens and hundreds columns, and $c_5$ the carry out of the thousands column.

1. Leading digit. Two 4-digit numbers sum to at most $19998$, so $M=\mathbf1$.

2. Thousands column. $S+M+c_4=O+10M\Rightarrow S+1+c_4=O+10\Rightarrow O=S+c_4-9\le 1$. Since $M=1$, $O\neq1$, so $O=0$ and $S+c_4=9$, i.e. $(S=9,c_4=0)$ or $(S=8,c_4=1)$.

3. Hundreds column. $E+O+c_3=N+10c_4\Rightarrow E+c_3=N+10c_4$.

  • If $c_4=1$: $E+c_3\ge10$ forces $E=9,c_3=1$, so $N=0=O$. Contradiction. So $c_4=0$ and $S=9$.
  • With $c_4=0$: $N=E+c_3$. Since $N\ne E$, $c_3=1$ and $N=E+1$.

4. Tens column. $N+R+c_2=E+10c_3=E+10$. Substituting $N=E+1$: $R+c_2=9$. $R\ne9$ (since $S=9$), so $c_2=1$ and $R=8$.

5. Units column. $D+E=Y+10$ (carry $c_2=1$). Used digits so far: $M=1,O=0,S=9,R=8$. The pool for $E,N,D,Y$ is $\{2,3,4,5,6,7\}$, with $N=E+1$ and $D+E\ge12$ (since $Y\ge2$).

$(E,N)$ Need $D\ge 12-E$ Verdict
(2,3) $D\ge10$ impossible
(3,4) $D\ge9$ impossible
(4,5) $D\ge8$ impossible
(5,6) $D\ge7$; free digits $\{2,3,4,7\}$ so $D=7$, $Y=7+5-10=2$ ✓ free works
(6,7) $D\ge6$; free digits $\{2,3,4,5\}$ impossible

Only one branch survives, so the solution is unique: $E=5,N=6,D=7,Y=2$.

Verification. $9567+1085=10652$ ✓. The letters $S,E,N,D,M,O,R,Y=9,5,6,7,1,0,8,2$ are all different. Mod 9 filter: $S+E+D+R-Y=9+5+7+8-2=27\equiv0\pmod9$ ✓.

Answer: $9567+1085=10652$.

⚡ 30-Second Shortcut: three forced facts come at once: $M=1$ (carry), $O=0$ (the only digit left once $M=1$), $S=9$ (because $c_4=0$). Then the hundreds and tens columns give $N=E+1$ and $R=8$ by the cancelling argument. Only the units column remains: you need $D+E\ge12$ with $E,N$ consecutive in $\{2,\dots,7\}$. Scan from the top: $(E,D)=(5,7)$ is the only pair that fits, which gives $Y=2$. Total time: under 30 seconds, with no guessing.
Level 3Circular Seating Example 8 — Facing Directions with Eight Persons

Eight friends A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H sit around a circular table. Four face the centre and four face outward, and no two adjacent persons face the same direction. Further:

  1. A faces the centre.
  2. C sits second to the right of A.
  3. B is the immediate left neighbour of A.
  4. D sits exactly opposite C.
  5. E sits to the immediate right of C.
  6. H is an immediate neighbour of A.
  7. G and H face opposite directions.

(i) Who sits second to the left of F?   (ii) Which direction does D face?   (iii) Who sits to the immediate right of H?

Step 1 — Anchor and pattern. Number the seats $P_1,\dots,P_8$ clockwise and place A at $P_1$ (rotational symmetry). A faces the centre and neighbours must differ, so directions alternate: $P_1,P_3,P_5,P_7$ face IN; $P_2,P_4,P_6,P_8$ face OUT.

Step 2 — Left/right rule. IN-facing: right $=$ anticlockwise ($P_{k-1}$), left $=$ clockwise ($P_{k+1}$). OUT-facing: the reverse.

Step 3 — Place people.

  • (2) A is IN, so "right" is anticlockwise: $P_1\to P_8\to P_7$. C at $P_7$ (IN).
  • (3) Immediate left of A (clockwise): B at $P_2$ (OUT).
  • (4) Opposite of $P_7$ is $P_3$: D at $P_3$ (IN).
  • (5) C is IN, so its immediate right is anticlockwise: $P_6$. E at $P_6$ (OUT).
  • (6) H neighbours A, so $P_2$ or $P_8$. $P_2$ is taken, so H at $P_8$ (OUT).
  • (7) G faces opposite to H (OUT), so G faces IN. The only free IN seat is $P_5$, so G at $P_5$. F takes the last seat, $P_4$ (OUT).

Every step was forced, so the arrangement is unique.

Fig 5.10 — Final arrangement, clockwise from the top: A, B, D, F, G, E, C, H. Arrows show facing direction: A, D, G, C face the centre, and B, F, E, H face outward.

Step 4 — Answer the questions.

  • (i) F is at $P_4$ (OUT), so left is anticlockwise: first left $=P_3$ (D), second left $=P_2$. B.
  • (ii) D is at $P_3$, an IN seat. Centre.
  • (iii) H is at $P_8$ (OUT), so right is clockwise: $P_1$. A.

Verification. All seven clues re-checked on Fig 5.10: ✓. Alternation: A(in) B(out) D(in) F(out) G(in) E(out) C(in) H(out) ✓.

⚡ 30-Second Shortcut — the Direction Sign: give each seat a sign $\sigma=+1$ if IN and $\sigma=-1$ if OUT. Then the person $k$ places to the left of seat $p$ is at seat $p+k\sigma$ (wrap $9\to1$, $0\to8$), and to the right at $p-k\sigma$. Check (i): $p=4,\sigma=-1,k=2\Rightarrow 4-2=2$ (B) ✓. Check (iii): right of $p=8$, $\sigma=-1$: $8-(1)(-1)=9\to1$ (A) ✓. Write the IN/OUT alternation first and never re-derive "left" again.
Level 3Calendars Example 9 — Day of the Week and Repeating Calendar

(a) Find the day of the week on 15 August 1947.
(b) In which year after 2026 will the calendar of 2026 repeat exactly?

(a) Full odd-days derivation. Completed years before 1947: $1946=1600+300+46$.

  • $1600$ years $=4$ full 400-year cycles $\to0$ odd days.
  • $300$ years $\to1$ odd day.
  • Remaining $46$ years: $\lfloor46/4\rfloor=11$ leap and $35$ ordinary years, giving $11\cdot2+35=57=8\cdot7+1\to1$ odd day. (Formula: $(46+11)\bmod7=1$ ✓.)
  • Days of 1947 up to 15 August: $31+28+31+30+31+30+31=212$ for January–July, plus $15=227=32\cdot7+3\to3$ odd days. (1947 is not a leap year, since $1947$ is not divisible by 4.)

Total odd days $=0+1+1+3=5\to$ Friday.

(b) Repeat of 2026. 2026 is an ordinary year. Add $1$ per ordinary year and $2$ per leap year (2028, 2032, 2036 are leap), as a running total from the start of 2026:

After year 2026 2027 2028 2029 2030 2031 2032 2033 2034 2035 2036
Running odd days 1 2 4 5 6 7 9 10 11 12 14

The total is a multiple of 7 after 2031, so the candidate is 2032. But 2032 is a leap year, while 2026 is ordinary, so the calendars cannot match (February would differ). The next multiple of 7 is $14$, after 2036, so the candidate is 2037, an ordinary year ✓.

Answers: (a) Friday; (b) 2037.

⚡ 30-Second Shortcut: (a) Use the Doomsday method. The century anchor for the 1900s is Wednesday. For year $47$: $\lfloor47/12\rfloor=3$, remainder $11$, $\lfloor11/4\rfloor=2$, and $3+11+2=16\equiv2$. So the doomsday of 1947 is Wednesday $+2=$ Friday. The date 8 August (8/8) is always a doomsday, and $15$ August is exactly 7 days later, so Friday. (b) Memorise the gaps: an ordinary year repeats after 6 or 11 years. Try $+6=2032$ (leap, so fail), then $+11=2037$ ✓. A leap year repeats after 28 years.
