Level 1 — Warm-up · Solutions Q1–Q8
Q1Level 1NAT

Concept: the perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord.

  1. Let $OM \perp AB$. Then $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$, so $AM = 48 \div 2 = 24$ cm.
  2. In the right triangle $OMA$: $OA = 25$ (radius) and $AM = 24$.
  3. $OM^2 = 25^2 - 24^2 = 625 - 576 = 49$, so $OM = 7$ cm.

Check: $(7, 24, 25)$ is a Pythagorean triple. ✓

Answer: 007

Q2Level 1MCQ

Concept: Pitot's theorem. For a quadrilateral with an incircle, $AB + CD = BC + AD$.

  1. Let the tangent lengths from $A, B, C, D$ be $a, b, c, d$. Then $AB = a + b$, $BC = b + c$, $CD = c + d$, $DA = d + a$.
  2. So $AB + CD = a + b + c + d = BC + DA$.
  3. $13 + 11 = 10 + AD$, so $AD = 24 - 10 = 14$ cm.

Check: $AB + CD = 24$ and $BC + AD = 10 + 14 = 24$. ✓ The other options are $BC = 10$ and $AB = 13$ (copied from the data), and $12$ (no source).

Answer: (D) 14

Q3Level 1MCQ

Concept: the central angle is twice the inscribed angle on the same arc.

  2. $\angle AOB = 2 \times 35^\circ = 70^\circ$.
  3. $OA = OB$ (radii), so $\triangle OAB$ is isosceles and $\angle OAB = \angle OBA$.

Check: $55^\circ + 55^\circ + 70^\circ = 180^\circ$. ✓ The option $70^\circ$ is $\angle AOB$ itself. The option $125^\circ = 180^\circ - 55^\circ$ is the supplement.

Q9Level 2MCQ

Concept: the tangent–chord angle equals the angle in the alternate segment. [See T5]

  1. $XTY$ is a straight line, so $\angle BTY = 180^\circ - \angle XTA - \angle ATB = 180^\circ - 52^\circ - 65^\circ = 63^\circ$.
  2. $\angle BTY$ is the angle between the tangent and chord $TB$. The alternate segment of chord $TB$ is the one on the other side of $TB$ from $Y$, and it contains $A$.
  3. By the alternate segment theorem, $\angle TAB = \angle BTY = 63^\circ$.

Check: $\angle TBA = \angle XTA = 52^\circ$ (alternate segment on chord $TA$). In $\triangle TAB$: $\angle TAB = 180^\circ - 65^\circ - 52^\circ = 63^\circ$. ✓

Q10Level 2NAT

Concept: direct tangent $= \sqrt{d^2 - (r_1 - r_2)^2}$, transverse tangent $= \sqrt{d^2 - (r_1 + r_2)^2}$. [See T6]

  1. $r_1 - r_2 = 32 - 7 = 25$ and $r_1 + r_2 = 32 + 7 = 39$. The circles are separate because $d = 65 \gt 39$.
  2. Direct: $D = \sqrt{65^2 - 25^2} = \sqrt{4225 - 625} = \sqrt{3600} = 60$ cm.
  3. Transverse: $T = \sqrt{65^2 - 39^2} = \sqrt{4225 - 1521} = \sqrt{2704} = 52$ cm.

Check: $(25, 60, 65)$ and $(39, 52, 65)$ are Pythagorean triples ($5\times(5, 12, 13)$ and $13\times(3, 4, 5)$). ✓ Also $D \gt T$, as it must be, since $r_1 - r_2 \lt r_1 + r_2$.

Answer: 008

Q11Level 2NAT

Concept: tangent lengths from one point are equal, and $\Delta = rs$.

  1. Let the incircle touch $AB$ at $F$ and $AC$ at $E$, and let $AF = AE = x$. Then $BF = BD = 6$ and $CE = CD = 8$.
  2. Sides: $BC = 14$, $AB = x + 6$, $AC = x + 8$. The semi-perimeter is $s = \dfrac{14 + (x+6) + (x+8)}{2} = x + 14$.
  3. Area from the inradius: $\Delta = rs = 4(x + 14)$.
  4. Area from Heron, with $s - BC = x$, $s - AC = 6$, $s - AB = 8$: $\Delta^2 = (x+14)\cdot x \cdot 6 \cdot 8 = 48x(x+14)$.
  5. Equate: $48x(x+14) = 16(x+14)^2$. Divide by $16(x+14)$: $3x = x + 14$, so $x = 7$.
  6. $AB = 13$ and $AC = 15$, so $AB + AC = 28$.

Check: the triangle is $13, 14, 15$ with $s = 21$. Then $\Delta = \sqrt{21 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6} = 84$ and $r = \dfrac{84}{21} = 4$. ✓

Answer: 028

Q13Level 2MCQ

Concept: an isosceles trapezium is cyclic, so Ptolemy applies.

  1. Since $AD = BC$, triangles $ABD$ and $BAC$ are congruent (SAS: $AD = BC$, $AB$ common, $\angle DAB = \angle CBA$ as base angles). Hence the diagonals are equal: $AC = BD$.

  3. Ptolemy: $AC \cdot BD = AB \cdot CD + AD \cdot BC$.
  4. $AC^2 = 16 \times 4 + 15 \times 15 = 64 + 225 = 289$, so $AC = 17$ cm.

Check (coordinates): take $A(0,0)$, $B(16,0)$, $D(6,h)$, $C(10,h)$ with $h^2 = 15^2 - 6^2 = 189$. Then $AC^2 = 10^2 + 189 = 289$. ✓

Q15Level 2NAT

Concept: each petal is two $90^\circ$ minor segments, or equivalently the overlap of semicircles by inclusion–exclusion.

Method 1 (segments).

  1. Each petal lies at a corner, between two adjacent semicircles. Take the corner $A$ with the semicircles on $AB$ and $AD$, each of radius $7$.
  2. The centres are the midpoints of $AB$ and $AD$. Both circles pass through $A$ and through the centre $O$ of the square. The chord $AO$ has length $7\sqrt2 = r\sqrt2$, so it subtends $90^\circ$ at each circle's centre.
  3. The petal is the union of two $90^\circ$ minor segments on either side of $AO$. One segment: $\tfrac14 \cdot \dfrac{22}{7} \cdot 49 - \tfrac12 \cdot 49 = 38.5 - 24.5 = 14$.
  4. One petal $= 2 \times 14 = 28$ cm$^2$. Four petals $= 4 \times 28 = 112$ cm$^2$.

Method 2 (inclusion–exclusion). Check.

  1. Centre the square at the origin, $[-7, 7]^2$. A point $(x, y)$ lies in the disc on the bottom side iff $x^2 + y^2 \le -14y$. The other three sides give $14y$, $-14x$ and $14x$. So the point lies in at least one disc iff $x^2 + y^2 \le 14\max(|x|, |y|)$.
  2. For a point of the square, let $m = \max(|x|,|y|) \le 7$. Then $x^2 + y^2 \le 2m^2 \le 14m$, so every point of the square is covered.
  3. Opposite discs satisfy both $x^2 + y^2 \le 14y$ and $\le -14y$, which forces $(x,y) = (0,0)$. So each point lies in at most two semicircles.
  4. Sum of the four semicircle areas $= 4 \times \tfrac12 \cdot \dfrac{22}{7} \cdot 49 = 308$. This equals (area covered once) $+ 2 \times$ (petals). Also the union is the whole square: (covered once) + (petals) $= 196$.
  5. Subtract: petals $= 308 - 196 = 112$ cm$^2$. ✓ Both methods agree.

Answer: 112

Trap & Error Forensics

T1 — Power of a Point: "external × internal chord" instead of $PA \cdot PB$ (Q12, Q28)

The mistake. With a secant or tangent–secant from an outside point $P$, students multiply the external part by the internal chord ($PA \cdot AB$) instead of by the whole secant from $P$ ($PA \cdot PB$).

Why it fails. The theorem multiplies the two distances measured from $P$ to the two points where the line meets the circle. For an outside point, the far point is at distance $PA + AB$, not $AB$. The chord $AB$ is the gap between the two points, not a distance from $P$.

Test on Q28.

  • Correct: $MT^2 = MA \cdot MB = 8 \times (8 + 24) = 256$, so $MT = 16$.
  • Wrong: $MT^2 = MA \cdot AB = 8 \times 24 = 192$, so $MT = 8\sqrt3 \approx 13.9$. This is not a clean number, which is itself a warning sign.

The mirror error for $P$ inside (Q12). Here the two segments of a chord are its two parts, which already start at $P$. Do not add the other chord's length. Use $AP \cdot PB = CP \cdot PD$, which gave $x(x+5) = 24$.

Fix. Before multiplying, write the secant as $P \to$ near point $\to$ far point. The product is always $(\text{distance to near}) \times (\text{distance to far})$. A tangent has near $=$ far $= PT$, which is where $PT^2$ comes from.

T2 — Forgetting $+2r$ in the perimeter of a sector (Q5)

The mistake. Perimeter of a sector $= l$ (arc length only).

Why it fails. A sector is a closed region bounded by an arc and two radii. Perimeter is the whole boundary, so $P = l + 2r$.

Test on Q5. $l = 22$ cm. Wrong: $22$ (option A). Correct: $22 + 2(21) = 64$ cm (option B).

Related slips.

  • A segment's perimeter is $l + \text{chord}$, not $l + 2r$. The chord is $2r\sin\tfrac\theta2$.
  • Area instead of perimeter: $231$ is the sector area, and $1386 = \pi r^2$ is the full circle's area.

Fix. Sketch the shape and trace the boundary with a finger: arc, radius, radius. Count each piece once.

T3 — Hidden faces vs cavity surface in joined and hollowed solids (Q17, Q18, Q26)

The mistake. Using one blanket rule for every combined solid, or a rule from the wrong case. Joined solids lose area at the joint, while hollowed solids gain area inside the cavity.

Case Correct accounting
Join two solids (hemisphere on cone) Subtract the joined face from each solid: the shared circle is hidden on both. Add only exposed parts.
Hollow out (cone cavity in a cylinder, Q17) Remove the opening, then add the cavity's own curved surface.
Through-hole (Q26) Remove two openings, add one inner wall.
Open vessel (Q18) Count only the faces that are actually metal.

  • Wrong 1, cavity surface forgotten: cylinder TSA minus the opening, $2\pi(3.5)(15.5) - 12.25\pi = 96.25\pi \approx 302.5$.
  • Wrong 2, top face kept even though the cone's base equals the whole top face: $108.5\pi + 43.75\pi = 152.25\pi \approx 478.5$.

  • Correct: $1176 - 24.5\pi + 98\pi = 1407$.
  • Wrong, the inner wall forgotten: $1176 - 77 = 1099$.

T4 — Major vs minor segment, and the reflex-angle sign (Q14)

The mistake. Using the minor-segment formula for a major segment, or applying "sector $-$ triangle" when the shaded part is the larger side.

Test on Q14 ($r = 12$, $\theta = 150^\circ$).

  • Minor segment: $60\pi - 36$.
  • Major segment: $144\pi - (60\pi - 36) = 84\pi + 36$ (option A).
  • Typical wrong options: $84\pi - 36$ (sign slip: the triangle must be added to the reflex sector) and $60\pi + 36$ (wrong sector angle).

Why the sign flips. The major segment is the reflex sector $210^\circ$ plus triangle $OAB$. The formula $\dfrac\theta{360^\circ}\pi r^2 - \tfrac12 r^2 \sin\theta$ gives this automatically for $\theta = 210^\circ$, since $\sin 210^\circ \lt 0$.

Fix. Decide which side is shaded before choosing a formula. For any shaded side, check that the area is smaller than $\pi r^2$ and, for a major segment, more than half the disc.

T5 — Choosing the wrong segment for the Alternate Segment Theorem (Q9)

The mistake. Equating the tangent–chord angle to an angle on the same side of the chord, or to the angle at the point of contact. Options $52^\circ$ and $65^\circ$ in Q9 are exactly these.

Rule. The angle between the tangent and chord $TB$ equals the angle that $TB$ subtends at any point on the arc on the other side of $TB$. The point of contact $T$ is not on that arc.

Test on Q9.

  • $\angle BTY = 63^\circ$, so $\angle TAB = 63^\circ$ ($A$ is on the far side of $TB$ from $Y$).
  • Wrong: $\angle TAB = \angle XTA = 52^\circ$ or $\angle ATB = 65^\circ$.

Fix. Always do the triangle-sum check: the three angles of $\triangle TAB$ must add to $180^\circ$ ($63 + 52 + 65$).

T6 — Mixing up $R - r$ and $R + r$: frustum slant height and common tangents (Q10, Q18)

The mistake. Writing $l = \sqrt{h^2 + (R + r)^2}$ for a frustum, or swapping the direct and transverse tangent formulas.

Why the slant height uses $R - r$. In the axial cross-section, the slant side, the height, and the horizontal gap between the two rims form a right triangle. The horizontal gap is the difference of the radii, $R - r$, not their sum.

Test on Q18 ($R = 10$, $r = 5$, $h = 12$).

  • Correct: $l = \sqrt{144 + 25} = 13$.
  • Wrong: $\sqrt{144 + 225} = \sqrt{369} \approx 19.2$. This is not a clean number, which is itself a warning sign.

The sum $R + r$ belongs in the CSA factor, $\pi(R + r)l$. Do not move it into the slant height.

The same sign logic for tangents (Q10). A direct tangent keeps both circles on the same side, so the radii effectively subtract: $\sqrt{d^2 - (r_1 - r_2)^2}$. A transverse tangent crosses between them, so they add: $\sqrt{d^2 - (r_1 + r_2)^2}$. Swapping them gives $D - T = 52 - 60 = -8$. A negative $D - T$ should stop you, because the direct tangent is always the longer one.
